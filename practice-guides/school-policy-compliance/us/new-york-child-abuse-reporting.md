# School Policy Compliance in New York: Child Abuse Reporting and Prevention[^about]

What a New York nonpublic K–12 school's child-protection policies must reflect: Education Law Article 23-B allegations against employees and volunteers, mandated reporting, abuse-reporting training, fingerprinting and Clean Slate, and the anti-bullying and harassment policy.

## What must a New York nonpublic school do when an employee or volunteer is accused of abusing a student? {#child-abuse-allegation-duties}

**Short answer.** Under Education Law Article 23-B, which reaches nonpublic schools, a teacher or other listed employee who receives an allegation that an employee or volunteer abused a child in an educational setting must promptly complete a written report and deliver it to the school administrator. [^edn-1125-school-includes-nonpublic] [^edn-1126-teacher-must-report] [^edn-1126-employee-written-report] Where the administrator finds reasonable suspicion of abuse, the administrator must promptly forward the report to law enforcement and, depending on who made the allegation, notify the parent, give the parent a written statement of rights, or both. [^edn-1128-administrator-duties] [^edn-1128-parent-allegation] [^edn-1128-other-person-allegation]

Article 23-B defines a school to include a nonpublic school, and its educational setting covers school buildings and grounds, school transportation, field trips, on- and off-site activities and any other place where an employee or volunteer allegedly had direct contact with a child. [^edn-1125-school-includes-nonpublic] [^edn-1125-educational-setting] The report goes on the State form, and the employee personally delivers a copy to the administrator of the school where the abuse allegedly occurred. [^edn-1126-employee-written-report] A school bus driver reports instead to the driver's supervisor. [^edn-1126-bus-driver] When the accused person is the superintendent or the administrator, the report goes to another administrator the school designates. [^edn-1126-administrator-accused]

What the administrator does next depends on who made the allegation, and each route applies only where there is reasonable suspicion that abuse occurred. [^edn-1128-administrator-duties] When the child made the allegation, the administrator promptly notifies the parent that an allegation has been made, gives the parent the written statement of parental rights, responsibilities and procedures, and forwards the report to law enforcement. [^edn-1128-administrator-duties] When a parent made it, the administrator gives that parent the written statement and forwards the report. [^edn-1128-parent-allegation] When anyone else made it, the administrator notifies the parent, gives the written statement, asks the person reporting for the source and basis of the allegation, and forwards the report. [^edn-1128-other-person-allegation] On each route, an administrator who receives a written report also gives a copy to the superintendent, and reporting to law enforcement may not be delayed because the superintendent cannot be reached. [^edn-1128-administrator-duties] [^edn-1128-parent-allegation] [^edn-1128-other-person-allegation] The Commissioner's regulation lists what the written statement to the parent must cover. [^nycrr-100-2-hh-parent-statement]

The State Education Department's summary adds that when an allegation reaches a nonpublic school from law enforcement or child protective services, the school building administrator completes the report form and makes the reasonable-suspicion determination. [^nysed-23b-nonpublic-administrator]

An administrator may not agree to withhold an allegation against an employee or volunteer from law enforcement, the superintendent or the Commissioner in exchange for the accused person's resignation or voluntary suspension; each violation is a class E felony with a civil penalty of up to twenty thousand dollars. [^edn-1133-no-silent-resignation]

## Are teachers at a New York nonpublic school mandated child abuse reporters? {#mandated-reporter-training}

**Short answer.** Social Services Law § 413 requires a school official, a category that expressly lists school teachers, to report when the teacher has reasonable cause to suspect that a child coming before the teacher in a professional capacity is abused or maltreated. [^sos-413-reasonable-cause] [^sos-413-school-official-reporter]

The list of school officials names school teachers, guidance counselors, psychologists, social workers, nurses and administrators, and then adds other school personnel required to hold a teaching or administrative license or certificate. [^sos-413-school-official-reporter] On the better reading, the license-or-certificate qualifier attaches to that residual category of other personnel rather than to the teachers named before it, so an uncertified teacher at a nonpublic school is a mandated reporter. [^sos-413-school-official-reporter] The State Education Department reads the training legislation the same way, stating that it expanded the staff who need mandated reporter training to include religious and independent school staff. [^nysed-training-independent-schools] The conservative course is for a school's policy to treat every teacher and administrator as a mandated reporter. [^sos-413-school-official-reporter]

A staff member who must report makes the report and then immediately notifies the person in charge of the school or that person's designee, and the school may not require prior approval or prior notification as a condition of reporting. [^sos-413-report-and-notify] [^sos-413-no-prior-approval] The reporting duty is the individual's own, so an internal reporting channel cannot be made a precondition to the individual's report. [^sos-413-no-prior-approval] This differs from the Article 23-B route described in the [question on allegations against employees](#child-abuse-allegation-duties), which runs through the school administrator to law enforcement. [^sos-413-report-and-notify] The school must give current and new mandated-reporter employees written information explaining the reporting requirements. [^sos-413-written-information] Training is covered in the [question on abuse-reporting training](#abuse-reporting-training).

## What child abuse identification and reporting training must New York nonpublic school staff complete, and how often? {#abuse-reporting-training}

**Short answer.** Education Law § 1132(3) requires teachers and administrators hired by a nonpublic school on or after July 1, 2019, and school bus drivers, to complete two hours of approved coursework or training on identifying and reporting child abuse and maltreatment. [^edn-1132-nonpublic-training]

Separately, the Commissioner's regulation requires each school, including a nonpublic school, to establish and run on an ongoing basis a training program on the Article 23-B procedures for current and new teachers, administrators and persons employed in equivalent titles in a nonpublic school, among other listed staff. [^nycrr-100-2-hh-23b-training-program] The State Education Department's guidance says the child-abuse-in-an-educational-setting training should be completed annually, while the mandated reporter training need be completed only once. [^nysed-training-frequency] The regulation also requires each school to give every teacher and other school official an annual written explanation of Article 23-B reporting, including its immunity provisions. [^nycrr-100-2-hh-annual-explanation]

The mandated reporter coursework itself has been updated by later amendments to Social Services Law § 413, and the Department states that all certified educators must have completion of the second updated workshop reported to its TEACH system by November 17, 2026. [^nysed-training-november-2026-deadline] That certification-based deadline is distinct from the § 1132(3) hiring-based requirement for nonpublic school teachers and administrators, so an uncertified nonpublic teacher's obligation comes from § 1132(3) and the school's own Article 23-B program. [^edn-1132-nonpublic-training] [^nycrr-100-2-hh-23b-training-program]

## Can a New York nonpublic school fingerprint prospective employees, and how does the Clean Slate Act affect its background checks? {#background-checks-fingerprinting}

**Short answer.** New York law authorizes, but does not require, a nonpublic elementary or secondary school to fingerprint prospective employees and seek a State Education Department clearance based on their criminal history. [^edn-305-30-nonpublic-fingerprinting] [^edn-3035-clearance-notice]

The Commissioner of Education notifies the school whether the prospective employee is cleared, and must make that determination under Executive Law § 296(16) and Correction Law Article 23-A. [^edn-3035-clearance-notice] Executive Law § 296(16) makes it unlawful to inquire about or act adversely on a conviction sealed under Criminal Procedure Law § 160.57, the Clean Slate provision, except where the record is accessed under listed paragraphs of § 160.57(1)(d). [^exc-296-16-sealed-convictions] One of those paragraphs allows access by entities authorized by state law to obtain a fingerprint-based criminal history check about a person's fitness to be responsible for the safety and well-being of children. [^cpl-160-57-children-fingerprint-access]

On that text, a sealed conviction can reach a nonpublic school only through an authorized fingerprint-based check, and a direct question to an applicant about a sealed conviction remains unlawful. [^exc-296-16-sealed-convictions] [^cpl-160-57-children-fingerprint-access]

## Must a New York nonpublic school adopt and publish a student anti-bullying and harassment policy? {#anti-bullying-policy}

**Short answer.** Education Law § 21 requires every nonpublic elementary or secondary school in New York to adopt a written, plain-language and age-appropriate anti-bullying and harassment policy, publish it on the school's website or parent and student portal, and share it annually with employees, parents and students. [^edn-21-policy-required]

The policy must at least explain how bullying and harassment are defined, how incidents are reported, investigated and documented, and how the victim is told the outcome of the investigation in an age-appropriate way. [^edn-21-policy-required] A school employee who witnesses or receives a report of bullying or harassment must notify the principal or head of school, or a designee, orally within one school day and in writing within two school days after the oral report. [^edn-21-employee-reports] The principal or head of school, or a designee, must then investigate promptly, communicate findings to the victim and take steps reasonably calculated to stop the conduct and protect the student, including against retaliation. [^edn-21-investigation]

Education Law § 20(5), which supplies the definitions for § 21, defines a school as any nonpublic elementary or secondary school, so the requirement reaches independent and religious schools.

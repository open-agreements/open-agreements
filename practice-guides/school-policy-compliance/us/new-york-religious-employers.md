# School Policy Compliance in New York: Discrimination Law and Religious Employers[^about]

How New York and federal discrimination law applies to an independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school: which laws cover it, whether an EEO policy is required, religious preference, the ministerial exception, off-duty conduct rules and the reproductive health handbook notice.

## Which federal and New York discrimination laws cover a private school, and at what employee count? {#discrimination-law-coverage}

**Short answer.** The New York State Human Rights Law covers every employer in the state regardless of size, while Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act reach employers with fifteen or more employees and the ADEA reaches employers with twenty or more. [^exc-292-employer-coverage] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen] [^usc-12111-ada-employer] [^usc-2000gg-pwfa-employer] [^usc-630-adea-employer]

As of the November 19, 2026 review of the official text, the Human Rights Law's employment provision protects age, race, creed, color, national origin, citizenship or immigration status, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, military status, sex, disability, predisposing genetic characteristics, familial status, marital status and status as a victim of domestic violence. [^exc-296-1-a-protected-classes] The federal headcount counts employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen]

## Does New York or federal law require an independent school to adopt a written equal employment opportunity policy? {#eeo-policy-required}

**Short answer.** Neither Title VII nor the New York State Human Rights Law requires a private school to adopt a general written equal employment opportunity policy. Title VII instead requires a covered employer to post the EEOC's notice, [^usc-2000e-10-posting-eeo] and New York requires every employer to adopt a written sexual harassment prevention policy. [^lab-201-g-policy-eeo] The State Human Rights Law bars discrimination by every employer in the state, [^exc-292-employer-all] and it separately bars a recruitment statement, advertisement or application form that expresses a limitation based on a protected characteristic unless it rests on a bona fide occupational qualification. [^exc-296-1-d-advertisements]

An equal employment opportunity statement in a school's handbook or job postings is therefore a description of obligations the school already has, not a separately mandated document. A religious school's statement can also describe the religious preference that Executive Law § 296(11) permits, as explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^exc-296-11-religious-preference-eeo]

## May a religious school in New York prefer co-religionists or require employees to support its religious mission? {#religious-preference-and-mission}

**Short answer.** A New York school that qualifies as a religious educational institution under Title VII, or as an organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with a religious organization under Executive Law § 296(11), may prefer members of its religion, but neither exemption permits discrimination because of race, color, sex or national origin. [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer] [^usc-2000e-2-e-2-religious-school] [^exc-296-11-religious-preference] [^eeoc-religious-employers-other-bases]

Section 702 of Title VII exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion, and § 703(e)(2) separately permits a school that is substantially owned, supported, controlled or managed by a religion, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a religion, to hire employees of that religion. [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer] [^usc-2000e-2-e-2-religious-school] Executive Law § 296(11) reaches a religious institution itself and a charitable or educational organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with a religious organization, and it also permits action calculated to promote the religious principles for which the organization is maintained. [^exc-296-11-religious-preference]

The EEOC's Compliance Manual states that religious organizations remain subject to Title VII's prohibitions on race, color, sex and national origin discrimination and to the ADEA and ADA. [^eeoc-religious-employers-other-bases] The Manual also states that the exemption applies only to organizations whose purpose and character are primarily religious, judged on all the facts, so the inquiry looks at how the school actually operates as well as at its governing documents. [^eeoc-religious-organization-test] Section 703(e)(2) applies its own test, asking whether the school is substantially owned, supported, controlled or managed by a religion or directs its curriculum toward propagating one. [^usc-2000e-2-e-2-religious-school] The New York Court of Appeals reads § 296(11) narrowly: in *Scheiber v. St. John's University* it held that the exemption does not let a religious employer discriminate on the basis of religion generally, and instead excludes from discrimination a preference for persons of the same faith that the institution makes to carry out its religious mission. [^scheiber-296-11-narrow] *Scheiber* concerned a preference in hiring, and the text does not say how far the clause permitting action to promote religious principles reaches a requirement that employees support the mission when the requirement bears on another protected characteristic; the conservative course is to tie any mission-support requirement to religious belief and conduct and to treat it as subject to the ordinary discrimination rules on every other ground. [^exc-296-11-religious-preference] [^eeoc-religious-employers-other-bases]

## Which religious school teachers and staff fall within the ministerial exception to discrimination claims? {#ministerial-exception-teachers}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception bars employment discrimination claims by employees whom a religious school entrusts with educating students in its faith, and the test turns on what the employee does. [^olg-what-employee-does] [^hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized]

In *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, the Supreme Court recognized the exception, reasoning that requiring a church to accept or retain an unwanted minister intrudes on more than an employment decision. [^hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized] The Court balanced society's interest in enforcing discrimination statutes against a religious group's interest in choosing who will teach its faith. [^hosanna-tabor-interest-balance] In *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru*, the Court said that educating young people in their faith lies at the core of a private religious school's mission. [^olg-what-employee-does]

The Second Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in New York, applied the exception in *Fratello v. Archdiocese of New York* to a Catholic elementary school's lay principal who sued under Title VII and Executive Law § 296, [^fratello-claims-title-vii-and-296] holding that because she held herself out as a spiritual leader of the school and performed important religious functions, the exception barred her employment-discrimination claims. [^fratello-principal-minister] In *Sander v. Westchester Reform Temple*, decided December 16, 2025, the New York Court of Appeals applied the exception to a claim under Labor Law § 201-d by a teacher whose offer letter established that her core teaching responsibilities were religious. [^sander-ministerial-201-d] The Fourth Circuit's 2024 decision in *Billard v. Charlotte Catholic High School*, which does not bind New York courts, applied the exception to a longtime English and drama teacher, [^billard-english-drama-teacher] concluding that he played a vital role as a messenger of the school's faith. [^billard-ministerial-holding]

The EEOC's Compliance Manual describes the exception as reaching lay employees and even employees who are not co-religionists. [^eeoc-ministerial-lay-employees] Because the test asks what the employee does, a teacher whose duties include teaching the faith is closer to the exception than an employee whose role has no religious function. [^olg-what-employee-does]

## Can a New York religious school discipline or dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that conflicts with its religious teachings? {#off-duty-conduct-religious-school}

**Short answer.** New York Labor Law § 201-d bars employers from acting against employees because of lawful off-duty political activities, recreational activities and use of consumable products, so a religious school's off-duty conduct rule is most secure for employees within the ministerial exception. [^lab-201-d-off-duty-religious] [^sander-ministerial-201-d-conduct]

In *Sander v. Westchester Reform Temple*, the Court of Appeals did not decide how far § 201-d's protection of recreational activities reaches, and instead held that the ministerial exception barred a religious teacher's § 201-d claim. [^sander-ministerial-201-d-conduct] The Fourth Circuit's *Billard* decision, which does not bind New York courts, upheld a Catholic school's decision under a diocesan policy against conduct contrary to the moral teachings of the Catholic faith, on the ground that the teacher fell within the ministerial exception. [^billard-moral-teachings-policy] [^billard-ministerial-holding-conduct]

For staff without a religious role, the ministerial exception does not apply, and the school relies on the statutory exemptions instead. Section 201-d itself exempts a Title VII-exempt religious educational institution only for speech on religious matters to employees who work on its religious activities. [^lab-201-d-religious-speech-exemption] Labor Law § 203-e, which bars discrimination based on an employee's reproductive health decisions, contains no religious-employer exception, as the [question on the reproductive health notice](#reproductive-health-notice-religious-school) explains. [^lab-203-e-prohibition-conduct] Courts also differ on how far Title VII's religious-employer exemption reaches: the Fourth Circuit held in October 2026 that it does not cover a religiously motivated decision challenged as sex discrimination, a decision that does not bind New York courts. [^doe-crs-702-not-sex] For staff outside the ministerial exception, the conservative course is to treat an off-duty conduct rule as subject to the ordinary federal and New York discrimination laws and their statutory exemptions.

## Must a religious school's employee handbook in New York include the Labor Law § 203-e reproductive health notice? {#reproductive-health-notice-religious-school}

**Short answer.** A religious school that provides a handbook is subject to the § 203-e(6) notice requirement, because the Second Circuit in 2025 vacated the injunction that had barred its enforcement. [^lab-203-e-notice-religious-school] [^compasscare-vacates-notice-injunction] The court remanded for the district court to decide whether any plaintiff had plausibly alleged an expressive-association claim under *Slattery v. Hochul*. [^compasscare-vacates-notice-injunction]

Section 203-e bars an employer from discriminating or retaliating against an employee because of the employee's or a dependent's reproductive health decision making. [^lab-203-e-prohibition] In *Slattery v. Hochul*, the Second Circuit concluded that § 203-e, as a crisis pregnancy center alleged it, forced the center to employ people who act against its mission, [^slattery-203-e-expressive-association] and *CompassCare v. Hochul* read *Slattery* to mean that an employer may have an associational-rights claim if the statute forces it to employ such people. [^compasscare-describes-slattery]

As of the November 19, 2026 review, the most recent appellate record cited here is the Second Circuit's January 2, 2025 decision in *CompassCare*, which vacated the injunction against the notice provision and addressed only whether the employers' allegations stated a plausible claim, not whether any employer is exempt. [^compasscare-vacates-notice-injunction] A possible as-applied claim about particular employment decisions is a different question from the duty to print the notice. *Slattery* recognizes a possible as-applied claim only for an employer that shows the statute forces it to employ people who act against its very mission, so the conservative course for a religious school with a handbook is to include the § 203-e(6) notice. [^slattery-203-e-expressive-association] [^lab-203-e-notice-religious-school]

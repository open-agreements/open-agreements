# School Policy Compliance in Virginia: Employee Handbook Notices and Workplace Rules[^about]

What a Virginia independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's employee handbook and workplace policies must reflect: required postings and pay statements, harassment liability, confidentiality clauses on sexual harassment claims, noncompetes, personal social media accounts, medical cannabis oil, pay ranges and salary history, pay discussion, whistleblower retaliation and NLRB work-rule standards.

## What written notices, postings and pay statements must a Virginia independent school give its employees? {#handbook-required-notices}

**Short answer.** A Virginia school must post a copy or approved summary of the state's noncompete statute and any Department of Social Services earned income tax credit notice where its other required employee notices are posted, and must give each employee a written pay statement on each regular pay date. [^va-hb-noncompete-posting] [^va-hb-eitc-notice] [^va-hb-pay-statement]

| Requirement | What the law requires | When or where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covenants not to compete, Va. Code § 40.1-28.7:8(G) | A copy of the section or a summary approved by the Department of Labor and Industry | Posted where other required employee notices are posted; a written warning for a first violation, then penalties of up to $250 and up to $1,000 [^va-hb-noncompete-posting] |
| Earned income tax credit, Va. Code § 40.1-28.7:3 | Any Department of Social Services notice that employees may be eligible for federal and state earned income tax credits | Posted where other required employee notices are posted [^va-hb-eitc-notice] |
| Pay statement, Va. Code § 40.1-29(D) | A paystub or online accounting showing the employer's name and address, hours worked for hourly employees and for salaried employees below the federal standard salary level, the rate of pay, gross wages and each deduction | On each regular pay date, with records kept for three years [^va-hb-pay-statement] |
| Veterans benefits poster, Va. Code § 40.1-28.7:11 | The Department's poster on benefits and services for veterans | Optional: any employer may request and display it [^va-hb-veterans-poster] |
| Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-10, for employers with fifteen or more employees | A posted notice prepared or approved by the EEOC | Posted where notices are customarily posted [^va-hb-title-vii-posting] [^va-hb-title-vii-employer] |

The posting duties in sections 40.1-28.7:8 and 40.1-28.7:3 fall on every employer, [^va-hb-noncompete-posting] [^va-hb-eitc-notice] and Title 40.1 defines an employer to include any association or corporation doing business in Virginia that employs another for wages, which reaches an independent or religious school. [^va-hb-employer-defined] The pay statement duty in section 40.1-29 uses the employer definition borrowed from federal wage law rather than the Title 40.1 definition. [^va-hb-wage-employer-defined]

Virginia's current paid sick leave article does not reach school staff: it defines a covered employee as a home health worker who provides personal care, respite or companion services to an individual receiving consumer-directed services under the state plan for medical assistance. [^va-hb-sick-leave-current-scope] [^va-hb-sick-leave-home-health-worker] A broader paid sick leave article takes effect July 1, 2027 under the enactment clauses of 2026 Va. Acts chapters 1128 and 1129, covering employers of at least 50 employees from that date, at least 25 from January 1, 2028 and at least one from January 1, 2029. [^va-hb-sick-leave-2027-employer] [^va-hb-psl-ch1128] [^va-hb-psl-ch1129] That article directs the Commissioner of Labor and Industry to adopt regulations requiring employers to notify employees of their rights in writing and through a required posting, so the notice content will come from those regulations rather than from the statute itself. [^va-hb-sick-leave-2027-notice]

## Does a Virginia school's anti-harassment policy affect its liability when an employee claims harassment by a supervisor? {#anti-harassment-policy}

**Short answer.** Under *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, an employer sued under Title VII for a supervisor's hostile environment, with no tangible employment action, may defend by proving it took reasonable care to prevent and correct harassment and the employee unreasonably failed to use its procedures. [^va-hb-faragher-defense]

*Faragher* held that proof of an anti-harassment policy with a complaint procedure is not required in every case as a matter of law, but that the need for a stated policy suited to the employment circumstances may be addressed when the employer litigates the first element of the defense. [^va-hb-faragher-policy] The defense is unavailable when the supervisor's harassment culminates in a tangible employment action such as discharge, demotion or undesirable reassignment. [^va-hb-faragher-tangible] Title VII reaches an employer with fifteen or more employees in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding year. [^va-hb-title-vii-employer-harassment]

The Virginia Human Rights Act reaches smaller schools: its employment section defines an employer as a person employing five or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year, and it bars discrimination in compensation, terms, conditions or privileges of employment because of sex, among other grounds. [^va-hb-vhra-employer] [^va-hb-vhra-prohibition] A court that finds unlawful discrimination under the Act may award compensatory and punitive damages and attorney fees. [^va-hb-vhra-remedies] Section 2.2-3905 states no harassment-specific defense of its own. [^va-hb-vhra-prohibition]

The Act's religious exceptions are framed around religion-based hiring. One provides that it is not an unlawful discriminatory practice for an elementary or secondary school that is owned, supported, controlled or managed in whole or in substantial part by a particular religion or religious organization, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a particular religion, to hire and employ employees of a particular religion. [^va-hb-vhra-religious-school] The other provides that the section does not apply to the employment of individuals of a particular religion by a religious corporation, association, educational institution or society to perform work associated with its activities. [^va-hb-vhra-religious-employer]

## Can a Virginia school require an employee to sign a confidentiality or nondisparagement clause that covers sexual harassment or sexual assault claims? {#confidentiality-of-harassment-claims}

**Short answer.** A Virginia employer may not require an employee or applicant, as a condition of employment, to sign or renew a nondisclosure, confidentiality or nondisparagement provision that has the purpose or effect of concealing the details of a sexual assault or sexual harassment claim. [^va-hb-nda-condition-of-employment] Any such provision is void and unenforceable. [^va-hb-nda-condition-of-employment]

The statute does not define employer, so the Title 40.1 definition applies, and it reaches any association or corporation doing business in Virginia that employs another for wages. [^va-hb-employer-defined-nda] Its prohibition is tied to provisions required as a condition of employment, and it preserves every other ground at law or in equity for holding such a provision unenforceable. [^va-hb-nda-condition-of-employment] [^va-hb-nda-other-grounds]

Federal law adds a second limit that does not depend on whether the clause was a condition of employment: under the Speak Out Act, a nondisclosure or nondisparagement clause agreed to before a sexual assault or sexual harassment dispute arises is not judicially enforceable in that dispute when the conduct is alleged to violate federal, tribal or state law. [^va-hb-speak-out-act] The Act defines a sexual harassment dispute as one relating to conduct alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable federal, tribal or state law, and it does not prohibit an employer and employee from protecting trade secrets or proprietary information. [^va-hb-speak-out-definitions] [^va-hb-speak-out-act] A confidentiality clause in a standard handbook acknowledgment or offer letter is therefore exposed under both laws to the extent it reaches the details of a sexual harassment or sexual assault claim. [^va-hb-nda-condition-of-employment] [^va-hb-speak-out-act]

## Can a Virginia school require teachers or staff to sign a noncompete, and what makes one unenforceable? {#noncompete-agreements}

**Short answer.** A Virginia employer may not enter into, enforce or threaten to enforce a covenant not to compete with a low-wage employee, or with a health care professional unless the covenant was entered into or renewed before July 1, 2026. [^va-hb-noncompete-ban] [^va-hb-hb627-application] Low-wage employees include every employee entitled to federal overtime pay and every employee whose average weekly earnings fall below the Commonwealth's average weekly wage. [^va-hb-noncompete-ban] [^va-hb-noncompete-low-wage]

For any other employee, a covenant not to compete entered into, amended or renewed on or after July 1, 2026 is unenforceable if the employer discharges the employee without cause and without providing severance benefits or other monetary payment, and the payment must be disclosed when the covenant is signed. [^va-hb-noncompete-severance] [^va-hb-sb170-application] A health care professional is anyone licensed, registered or certified by the Board of Medicine, Nursing, Counseling, Optometry, Psychology or Social Work, which can include a school nurse or licensed counselor. [^va-hb-noncompete-low-wage] The low-wage category also includes interns, students, apprentices and trainees, with or without pay, and independent contractors paid an hourly rate below the state median hourly wage, and it excludes an employee whose earnings come wholly or predominantly from sales commissions, incentives or bonuses. [^va-hb-noncompete-low-wage] The statute's definition of a covenant not to compete does not restrict an employee from serving a customer or client of the employer if the employee does not initiate contact or solicit that customer or client. [^va-hb-noncompete-definition] Nondisclosure agreements protecting trade secrets and proprietary or confidential information are outside the statute's limits. [^va-hb-noncompete-nda]

An employee may sue within two years of the latest of signing, learning of the covenant, termination or the employer's first step to enforce it, and the court may void the covenant and award liquidated damages, lost compensation and attorney fees; an employer that violates the ban or the severance rule, as determined by the Commissioner, is subject to a civil penalty of $10,000 for each violation. [^va-hb-noncompete-remedies] [^va-hb-noncompete-penalty] Section 40.1-28.7:8 does not define "cause"[^va-hb-noncompete-severance] or "severance benefits or other monetary payment"[^va-hb-noncompete-severance], and it sets no minimum payment, so the conservative course is to state a specific payment in the covenant when it is signed and to treat any discharge that is not plainly for cause as triggering it. [^va-hb-noncompete-severance] [^va-hb-noncompete-definition] Neither rule reaches every existing covenant, because each 2026 act that added one carries its own application clause, which is not codified in the section. [^va-hb-sb170-application] [^va-hb-hb627-application] Chapter 883 added the severance rule, [^va-hb-sb170-severance] and its second enactment clause provides that nothing in the act shall "invalidate, alter, or otherwise affect any contract, covenant, or agreement entered into, amended, or renewed prior to July 1, 2026"[^va-hb-sb170-application], so the severance rule does not make a covenant entered into, amended or renewed before that date unenforceable. [^va-hb-sb170-application] Chapters 1113 and 1114 added health care professionals to the ban, [^va-hb-hb627-hcp-ban] and their application clauses protect "contracts, covenants, or agreements entered into or renewed prior to July 1, 2026"[^va-hb-hb627-application], without the word amended that chapter 883 uses. [^va-hb-hb627-application] [^va-hb-sb128-application] None of the three acts says how it treats a covenant first signed before July 1, 2026 that is amended or renewed on or after that date, so the conservative course is to treat a covenant amended or renewed on or after July 1, 2026 as subject to the rule that act added. [^va-hb-sb170-application] [^va-hb-hb627-application]

## May a Virginia school require employees or applicants to give access to their personal social media accounts? {#personal-social-media-accounts}

**Short answer.** A Virginia employer may not require a current or prospective employee to disclose the username and password to a personal social media account, or to add an employee, supervisor or administrator to the account's contacts. [^va-hb-social-media-prohibition] [^va-hb-social-media-exceptions] The statute preserves compliance with other law and an employer's existing rights or obligations to request credentials for a formal investigation of suspected violations of law or written policy. [^va-hb-social-media-prohibition] [^va-hb-social-media-exceptions]

The investigation exception applies only when the employee's account activity is reasonably believed to be relevant to a formal investigation or related proceeding by the employer of allegations that the employee violated federal, state or local law or the employer's written policies, and the credentials may be used only for that investigation or proceeding. [^va-hb-social-media-exceptions] A social media account in this statute is a personal account, and the definition excludes accounts opened at the employer's request, accounts the employer provides such as its email system, accounts set up on the employer's behalf, and accounts set up to impersonate the employer. [^va-hb-social-media-definitions] The employer may view publicly available information about a current or prospective employee. [^va-hb-social-media-public]

An employer that inadvertently receives an employee's login information through an employer-provided device or network monitoring is not liable for having it but may not use it to access the account, [^va-hb-social-media-inadvertent] and the employer may not penalize a current employee or refuse to hire an applicant for exercising these rights. [^va-hb-social-media-no-retaliation] The statute's definition of employer adds state and local government units to the general Title 40.1 definition, so it reaches a private or religious school. [^va-hb-social-media-definitions]

## Can a Virginia school discipline an employee for cannabis use or a positive cannabis test? {#off-duty-cannabis-use}

**Short answer.** Virginia bars an employer from discharging, disciplining or discriminating against an employee for lawful use of cannabis oil under a practitioner's valid written certification for a diagnosed condition or disease. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-protection] [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-exceptions] The statute preserves action for work impairment, bans on possession during work hours, compliance with federal law and federal contracts or funding, and a THC-test exemption for defense industrial base employers, and it sets no general drug-testing rule. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-protection] [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-exceptions]

The protection is limited to the use the section describes: lawful use of cannabis oil under a practitioner's written certification for the treatment or symptoms of the employee's diagnosed condition or disease. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-protection] The section does not by its terms protect other off-duty cannabis use. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-protection] It does not set rules for when or how an employer may drug-test; its only reference to testing is the exception allowing a defense industrial base sector employer, as defined by the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, to refuse to hire or retain a person who tests positive for THC above 50 ng/ml in urine or 10 pg/mg in hair. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-exceptions]

Even for a certified user, an employer may take adverse action for any work impairment caused by cannabis oil and may prohibit possession during work hours, and the section does not require an act that would violate federal law or cost the employer a federal contract or federal funding. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-exceptions] The section uses the Title 40.1 definition of employer and adds public employers to it, and it excludes law-enforcement officers from the definition of employee, so it reaches a private or religious school. [^va-hb-cannabis-oil-definitions] [^va-hb-employer-defined-cannabis]

Virginia's statutes barring a condition of employment that an employee abstain from tobacco use off duty reach only employees of and applicants to the Commonwealth, and of localities and political subdivisions; they do not reach a private school. [^va-hb-tobacco-state-employees] [^va-hb-tobacco-local-employees]

## Must a Virginia school's job postings include a pay range, and may it ask applicants about their salary history? {#pay-range-job-postings}

**Short answer.** A Virginia employer must disclose the wage, salary or a good-faith wage or salary range in each public and internal posting for a job, promotion, transfer or other employment opportunity, and may not seek or rely on an applicant's wage or salary history. [^va-hb-pay-range-prohibitions] [^va-hb-pay-range-voluntary] Salary history an applicant volunteers unprompted may be confirmed and relied on only to support pay above the initial offer. [^va-hb-pay-range-prohibitions] [^va-hb-pay-range-voluntary]

The range is the minimum and maximum pay for the position, set in good faith by reference to any applicable pay scale, any previously set range for the position, the actual pay of people in equivalent positions or the budgeted amount, and a good-faith analysis considers the breadth of the range. [^va-hb-pay-range-definitions] [^va-hb-pay-range-prohibitions] An employer may not refuse to interview, hire, employ or promote, or otherwise retaliate against, a person for declining to give salary history or for asking for a range. [^va-hb-pay-range-prohibitions] An applicant may still volunteer salary history, including to negotiate after an initial offer, and an employer that receives it unprompted may rely on it, and seek to confirm it, only to support pay higher than its initial offer and only to the extent the higher pay does not violate Virginia's equal pay statute or federal law. [^va-hb-pay-range-voluntary]

The Attorney General may sue for civil penalties of up to $1,000 for a first violation and up to $5,000 for each later violation. [^va-hb-pay-range-ag] An aggrieved applicant or employee may sue within one year for actual damages, but before suing over a posting a prospective employee must give the employer an opportunity to correct it, and no posting claim lies if the employer corrects the posting in its original locations within 15 business days of written notice. [^va-hb-pay-range-private-action] The section does not define employer, so the Title 40.1 definition applies, and that definition contains no employee-count threshold. [^va-hb-employer-defined-pay]

The section was added by 2026 Acts of Assembly chapters 996 and 1063, identical acts of the 2026 regular session whose only enactment clause adds the section, so neither sets an effective date of its own. [^va-hb-pay-range-ch996] [^va-hb-pay-range-ch1063] Under the Constitution of Virginia, a law enacted at a regular session takes effect on the first day of July following adjournment unless the bill specifies an earlier emergency date or a later date, so the section took effect July 1, 2026. [^va-hb-const-effective-date]

## Can a Virginia school's handbook forbid employees from discussing their pay with one another? {#pay-discussion-rules}

**Short answer.** A Virginia employer may not discharge or retaliate against an employee for asking about, discussing or disclosing the employee's own or another employee's wages, or for filing a complaint with the Department of Labor and Industry. [^va-hb-wage-discussion] The protection does not cover certain disclosures by employees who have access to others' compensation information as an essential job function. [^va-hb-wage-discussion]

The exception covers an employee whose essential job functions give access to the compensation information of other employees or applicants and who discloses that pay to people who do not otherwise have access to it, and it does not apply to a disclosure in response to a formal complaint or charge, in furtherance of an investigation, proceeding, hearing or action, including the employer's own investigation, or consistent with a legal duty to furnish information. [^va-hb-wage-discussion] A handbook rule making wage discussion a disciplinary offense would, if enforced against an employee outside that exception, be the retaliatory action the section prohibits. [^va-hb-wage-discussion]

The Commissioner may impose a civil penalty of up to $100 for each violation, considering the size of the employer's business and the gravity of the violation, and may petition a circuit court for injunctive relief. [^va-hb-wage-discussion-penalty] The section relies on the Title 40.1 definition of employer, which reaches a private or religious school. [^va-hb-employer-defined-wage-discussion]

## What does Virginia's whistleblower law protect, and must a Virginia school post or distribute a whistleblower notice? {#whistleblower-retaliation}

**Short answer.** Virginia bars an employer from retaliating against an employee who, personally or through someone acting for the employee, in good faith reports a violation of federal or state law or regulation to a supervisor, governmental body or law-enforcement official. [^va-hb-whistleblower-protection] [^va-hb-whistleblower-limits] [^va-hb-whistleblower-remedy] The same protection covers an employee asked by a governmental body or law-enforcement official to participate in an investigation, hearing or inquiry; one who provides information or testimony in a government inquiry into an alleged violation by the employer; and one who refuses to commit a criminal act that would expose the employee to criminal liability, or refuses an unlawful order after telling the employer why. The statute itself contains no posting or notice requirement. [^va-hb-whistleblower-protection] [^va-hb-whistleblower-limits] [^va-hb-whistleblower-remedy]

The protection does not authorize disclosure of data protected by law or privilege, does not cover statements the employee knows are false or makes in reckless disregard of the truth, and does not permit disclosures that would violate law or impair common-law confidentiality protections. [^va-hb-whistleblower-limits] An employee may sue within one year of the retaliatory action for an injunction, reinstatement, lost wages and benefits with interest, and attorney fees and costs. [^va-hb-whistleblower-remedy] The section uses the Title 40.1 definition of employer, which reaches a private or religious school. [^va-hb-employer-defined-whistleblower]

Section 40.1-27.3 contains no posting or distribution requirement; its text consists of the protected conduct, its limits and the civil action. [^va-hb-whistleblower-protection] [^va-hb-whistleblower-limits] [^va-hb-whistleblower-remedy] The protection covers a good-faith report made either to a supervisor or directly to a governmental body or law-enforcement official, and the section does not condition protection for an outside report on a prior internal report, so a handbook's internal reporting channel operates alongside the statutory right rather than as a precondition to it. [^va-hb-whistleblower-protection] Virginia's separate Fraud and Abuse Whistle Blower Protection Act covers only employees of governmental agencies, which it defines as state agencies, localities and local or regional governmental authorities, and local school divisions, so it does not reach a private school. [^va-hb-fawbpa-scope]

## Do NLRB standards for handbook work rules apply to a nonprofit independent school in Virginia? {#nlrb-handbook-rules}

**Short answer.** The National Labor Relations Board applies a $1 million annual minimum to private and nonprofit schools, and its 2023 *Stericycle* standard presumes unlawful a work rule, challenged as facially unlawful, that the General Counsel proves reasonably tends to chill employees' exercise of their rights. [^va-hb-nlrb-jurisdiction-schools] [^va-hb-nlrb-stericycle] [^va-hb-nlrb-stericycle-facial]

The Board states that it will not assert jurisdiction over employees of a religious organization who are involved in effectuating its religious purpose, such as teachers in church-operated schools. [^va-hb-nlrb-jurisdiction-religious] The standard governs work rules challenged as facially unlawful under Section 8(a)(1) of the National Labor Relations Act. [^va-hb-nlrb-stericycle-facial] Under *Stericycle*, an employer can rebut the presumption by proving that the rule advances a legitimate and substantial business interest that a more narrowly tailored rule could not advance. [^va-hb-nlrb-stericycle] In *NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago*, the Court declined to construe the Act to reach teachers in church-operated schools absent a clear expression of congressional intent, because doing so would raise questions under the Religion Clauses. [^va-hb-catholic-bishop]

As of the November 23, 2026 review, *Stericycle* remains the Board's standard. [^va-hb-nlrb-stericycle] In *Tesla, Inc.*, 375 NLRB No. 63 (Oct. 28, 2026), Chairman Murphy and Member Macy said they would be open to reconsidering *Stericycle* in a future appropriate case while finding it not at issue there, so the standard may change. [^va-hb-nlrb-tesla] A secular nonprofit school that meets the $1 million minimum is within the Board's jurisdiction, and its handbook rules on confidentiality, social media, pay discussion and workplace conduct are measured against that standard. [^va-hb-nlrb-jurisdiction-schools] [^va-hb-nlrb-stericycle] *Catholic Bishop* addressed teachers in church-operated schools, so it does not by itself remove a religious school's non-teaching staff from the Act. [^va-hb-catholic-bishop]

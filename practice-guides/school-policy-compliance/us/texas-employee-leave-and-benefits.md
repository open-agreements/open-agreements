# School Policy Compliance in Texas: Employee Leave, Benefits and Tuition Remission[^about]

The leave and benefit rules a Texas independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's policies must reflect: paid leave and its payout under the Payday Law, pregnancy and lactation accommodation, the FMLA, unemployment coverage for church-run and nonprofit schools, elective workers' compensation, jury, voting, military and evacuation leave, tuition remission and deductions from final pay.

## Must a Texas private school give teachers and staff paid sick leave, vacation or parental leave? {#paid-sick-and-vacation-leave}

**Short answer.** No Texas statute requires a private school to give its employees paid sick leave, vacation or parental leave. The Texas Workforce Commission states that vacation, sick and parental leave are not required under Texas law unless the leave would be a reasonable accommodation under disability- or pregnancy-related laws. [^tx-twc-leave-not-required]

In a search of every current Labor Code chapter, including amendments through the 2025 second called session, for the terms sick leave, paid leave, family leave, medical leave and parental leave, the references to sick leave appear only in wage-definition, professional employer organization, unemployment and public-employee workers' compensation provisions, and none of them requires a private employer to grant leave. [^tx-twc-leave-not-required] Unless another statute expressly authorizes it, a city or county cannot add a leave mandate: Labor Code § 1.005, added in 2023, voids a municipal or county ordinance, order or rule regulating conduct in a field the Labor Code occupies, and that field includes employment leave and employment benefits that exceed or conflict with federal or state law for employers other than a municipality or county. [^tx-lab-1-005-preemption] Before that enactment, *Texas Association of Business v. City of Austin* held that the Texas Minimum Wage Act preempted Austin's paid sick leave ordinance because the ordinance established a wage, making the ordinance unconstitutional. [^tx-tab-v-austin-holding]

Where a school chooses to grant paid leave, its written terms govern. The Payday Law treats vacation, holiday, sick leave and parental leave pay as wages when owed under a written agreement with the employer or a written policy of the employer, [^tx-lab-61-001-wages] and the Commission notes that the Payday Law will enforce leave pay according to the terms of the written policy. [^tx-twc-leave-written-policy] The Payday Law defines an employer as a person who employs one or more employees, and its exclusion for governmental entities covers the United States, Texas and its political subdivisions, not private schools. [^tx-lab-61-001-employer] [^tx-lab-61-003-government]

The leave statutes Texas enacts for teachers are written for public schools. Education Code § 22.003 provides a state minimum personal leave program of five days a year, with no limit on accumulation and transferable among districts, for school district employees, [^tx-ed-22-003-personal-leave] and its assault leave belongs to an employee of a school district who is physically assaulted while performing regular duties. [^tx-ed-22-003-assault-leave] The Education Code applies to educational institutions supported in whole or in part by state tax funds unless the Code excludes them, [^tx-ed-1-001-leave-scope] and the 2025 education savings account statute states that an education service provider receiving program money may not be considered a state actor on the basis of receiving it. [^tx-ed-29-368-autonomy] Because § 22.003 names school district employees as its beneficiaries, neither point brings a private school's staff within it. [^tx-ed-22-003-personal-leave]

## Must a Texas private school pay out a departing teacher's unused vacation or sick leave, and when is final pay due? {#leave-payout-and-final-pay}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school owes a departing employee pay for unused vacation or sick leave only if a written agreement or written policy promises it, because the Payday Law counts leave pay as wages only when owed under such a writing. [^tx-lab-61-001-wages-payout] [^tx-twc-leave-payout]

The Texas Workforce Commission states that the wording of the policy or agreement controls the payout and that, if no such policy exists, the employer does not owe it. [^tx-twc-leave-payout] Final pay is due by the sixth day after a discharge, and by the next regularly scheduled payday when the employee leaves other than by discharge, such as by resigning. [^tx-lab-61-014-final-pay]

An employee who is not paid as the Payday Law requires may file a wage claim with the Commission no later than the 180th day after the wages became due, a deadline the statute makes jurisdictional. [^tx-lab-61-051-wage-claim] If the Commission finds that the employer acted in bad faith in not paying wages, it may assess an administrative penalty in addition to ordering payment, [^tx-lab-61-053-bad-faith] capped at the lesser of the wages in question or $1,000. [^tx-lab-61-053-cap]

## What pregnancy and lactation accommodations must a Texas private school provide to its employees? {#pregnancy-and-lactation-accommodation}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school with fifteen or more employees must reasonably accommodate a qualified employee's known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions under the federal Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship. [^usc-2000gg-1-pwfa-accommodation-tx] [^usc-2000gg-pwfa-covered-entity-tx]

That Act is subject to Title VII's religious-employment provision in 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-1(a). [^usc-2000gg-5-religious-tx] In *Texas v. Blanche*, decided November 20, 2026, the en banc Fifth Circuit held that the Act's passage was unconstitutional because the House counted proxies toward its quorum, [^texas-v-blanche-pwfa-tx] but the only relief before it was the district court's injunction against enforcing the Act against the State of Texas. [^texas-v-blanche-texas-only-tx] The court said its decision extends no further than affirming that relief, so it does not by its terms bar enforcement against a private school, though its holding leaves the Act's enforceability in the Fifth Circuit uncertain. [^texas-v-blanche-relief-tx] Texas's own employment discrimination statute, Labor Code chapter 21, reaches a private employer with fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^tx-lab-21-002-employer] It treats discrimination because of sex as including pregnancy, childbirth or a related medical condition, and requires that a woman affected by them be treated for all employment purposes, including fringe benefits, in the same manner as others similar in their ability or inability to work. [^tx-lab-21-106-pregnancy]

Chapter 21's religious-organization section has two parts. Subsection (a) permits a religious educational institution, or an educational organization operated, supervised or controlled in whole or in substantial part by a religious corporation, association or society, to limit employment or give a preference to members of the same religion. [^tx-lab-21-109-religious] Subsection (b) states that Subchapter B does not apply to a religious corporation, association or society's employment of an individual of a particular religion to perform work connected with its religious activities, [^tx-lab-21-109-religious] and Subchapter B is where the chapter's prohibition on employer discrimination because of sex appears. [^tx-lab-21-051-subchapter-b] How these provisions and the federal religious-employer rules bear on a religious school's employment decisions is addressed in the [Texas guide on discrimination law and religious employers](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/texas-religious-employers).

For lactation, the federal PUMP Act provision requires reasonable break time each time an employee needs to express breast milk, for one year after the child's birth, and a private place other than a bathroom. [^usc-218d-pump-break-lactation-tx] The Fair Labor Standards Act reaches a private school's employees because it treats the operation of a preschool, elementary or secondary school as a covered enterprise whether the school is public or private and whether or not it operates for profit. [^usc-203-s-school-enterprise-tx] An employer with fewer than fifty employees is exempt if compliance would impose an undue hardship by causing significant difficulty or expense in relation to the size, financial resources, nature or structure of its business. [^usc-218d-small-employer-hardship-tx] Texas's statute on expressing breast milk at work, Government Code chapter 619, binds only a public employer, which it defines as a county, municipality or other political subdivision, including a school district, or a state agency or institution, [^tx-gov-619-001-public-employer] so its break-time and private-space duties do not reach a private school. [^tx-gov-619-004-duties] A business, including a private school, may instead use the designation *mother-friendly* in its promotional materials if it adopts a worksite breast-feeding policy addressing schedule flexibility, private locations, a clean water source and sink, and hygienic milk storage. [^tx-hs-165-003-mother-friendly]

## Does the federal FMLA apply to a small Texas private school, and does Texas add family leave of its own? {#fmla-and-family-leave}

**Short answer.** The federal Family and Medical Leave Act covers a Texas private elementary or secondary school regardless of its size, but an employee is eligible only if the school employs at least fifty employees within seventy-five miles of the employee's worksite. [^cfr-825-600-school-coverage-tx] [^usc-2611-fmla-75-mile-tx]

An eligible employee is entitled to a total of twelve workweeks of leave in a twelve-month period for the birth or placement of a child, to care for a spouse, child or parent with a serious health condition, or for the employee's own serious health condition, among other listed reasons. [^usc-2612-fmla-twelve-weeks-tx] For other private employers the Act applies only at fifty or more employees in twenty or more workweeks, a threshold the school regulation displaces. [^usc-2611-fmla-employer-tx] [^cfr-825-600-school-coverage-tx] An eligible employee must also have worked for the employer for at least twelve months and 1,250 hours in the preceding twelve months, [^cfr-825-110-eligibility-tx] and the federal regulations set special rules for instructional employees, such as teachers and coaches, on intermittent leave, reduced schedules and leave near the end of an academic term. [^cfr-825-600-instructional-employees-tx]

Texas adds no family or medical leave entitlement for private-sector employees: the Texas Workforce Commission states that sick and parental leave are not required under Texas law unless the leave would be a reasonable accommodation under disability- or pregnancy-related laws. [^tx-twc-leave-not-required-fmla]

## Must a Texas church-affiliated or nonprofit private school pay state unemployment tax for its staff? {#unemployment-coverage-religious-schools}

**Short answer.** Texas unemployment coverage excludes service for a church or for a primarily religious organization that a church operates, supervises, controls or principally supports, but other 501(c)(3) schools are covered once they employ four or more individuals in twenty or more calendar weeks. [^tx-lab-201-066-religious-service] [^tx-lab-201-023-nonprofit]

Under the Texas Unemployment Compensation Act, a nonprofit organization is an employer if it is described in Internal Revenue Code § 501(c)(3), is exempt from income tax under § 501(a), and employed at least four individuals for a portion of at least one day during twenty or more different calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^tx-lab-201-023-nonprofit] The Act's definition of employment does not include service in the employ of a church, a convention or association of churches, or an organization that is operated primarily for religious purposes and that is operated, supervised, controlled or principally supported by a church or a convention or association of churches. [^tx-lab-201-066-religious-service] The organization exclusion has two parts, a primarily religious purpose and church operation, supervision, control or principal support, so a religiously affiliated school that meets only one of them falls outside its words. [^tx-lab-201-066-religious-service] The same section excludes service by an ordained, commissioned or licensed minister in the exercise of the ministry and by a member of a religious order as the order requires. [^tx-lab-201-066-religious-service]

These exclusions describe automatic coverage. An employing unit may elect in writing, for at least two calendar years, to have services that are not employment treated as employment for all purposes of the Act, effective on the Commission's written approval. [^tx-lab-206-003-election] A covered 501(c)(3) school may elect to reimburse the Commission for benefits paid instead of paying contributions, by an election made within forty-five days after the notice that it is subject to the Act and effective for at least two calendar years. [^tx-lab-205-002-reimbursement]

## Must a Texas private school carry workers' compensation insurance, and what changes if it does not? {#workers-compensation-coverage}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school may choose whether to carry workers' compensation insurance, because the Labor Code makes coverage elective for employers other than public employers, except as otherwise provided by law. [^tx-lab-406-002-elective]

An employer that does not obtain coverage must notify the Division of Workers' Compensation in writing that it elects not to, as the commissioner's rules prescribe. [^tx-lab-406-004-division-notice] Every employer, covered or not, must tell each new employee at hiring whether it has coverage, post a notice of its coverage status at conspicuous locations, and notify employees within fifteen days after coverage, or its termination or cancellation, takes effect. [^tx-lab-406-005-employee-notice]

A school without coverage that is sued by an employee for a work injury cannot defend on the grounds that the employee was contributorily negligent, assumed the risk or was injured by a fellow employee's negligence, though it may defend on the ground that the employee intended the injury or was intoxicated, and the employee must prove the employer's negligence. [^tx-lab-406-033-defenses] An employee's agreement, made before an injury, to waive that cause of action is void and unenforceable. [^tx-lab-406-033-waiver]

## Can a Texas private school fire or dock an employee for jury service or for taking time off to vote? {#jury-and-voting-leave}

**Short answer.** Texas law bars a private school from terminating a permanent employee for jury or grand jury service, and makes it an offense to knowingly refuse an employee time off to vote unless the polls are open two consecutive hours outside the employee's working hours. [^tx-cprc-122-001-private-employer] [^tx-elec-276-004-voting]

Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 122.001 currently appears in two versions, each amended by a different 2019 act. The H.B. 504 version bars a private employer from terminating a permanent employee because the employee serves as a juror or grand juror, [^tx-cprc-122-001-private-employer] and the S.B. 370 version bars any employer from discharging, threatening to discharge, intimidating or coercing a permanent employee because of jury service, or attendance or scheduled attendance in connection with it, in any court in the United States. [^tx-cprc-122-001-any-court] Under both, an employee who gives the employer actual notice of intent to return as soon as practical after release is entitled to return to the same employment. [^tx-cprc-122-001-private-employer] [^tx-cprc-122-001-any-court] A violation entitles the employee to reinstatement, damages of between one and five years' compensation and reasonable attorney's fees, in an action brought within two years after the jury service, [^tx-cprc-122-002-remedies] and it is a Class B misdemeanor. [^tx-cprc-122-0021-penalty] The employer has a defense if its circumstances changed during the service so that reemployment was impossible or unreasonable, but it must prove the termination was for reasons other than the jury service. [^tx-cprc-122-003-defense] The Texas Workforce Commission states that Texas law does not require jury duty leave to be paid, except for salaried exempt employees. [^tx-twc-jury-pay]

For voting, the offense also covers subjecting or threatening an employee with a penalty for attending the polls on election day or during early voting, and *penalty* means a loss or reduction of wages or another benefit of employment; the offense is a Class C misdemeanor. [^tx-elec-276-004-voting] Applying that definition, the Texas Workforce Commission states that an employee who has not already voted early is entitled to paid time off to vote on election day unless the employee has at least two consecutive hours to vote outside working hours. [^tx-twc-voting-paid]

## Must a Texas private school hold a job open for an employee's military duty or a hurricane evacuation? {#military-and-evacuation-leave}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school may not fire a state military forces member for ordered training or duty, or an employee who leaves work for an ordered evacuation, and federal law bars denying retention or any employment benefit because of uniformed service. [^tx-gov-437-204-state-military] [^tx-lab-22-002-evacuation] [^usc-4311-userra-discrimination-tx]

Government Code § 437.204 protects a member of the state military forces of Texas or any other state, entitles the employee to return to the same employment without loss of time, efficiency rating, vacation time or any benefit of employment, and requires the employee to give written or actual notice of intent to return as soon as practicable after release; a violation is an unlawful employment practice that may be brought to the Texas Workforce Commission civil rights division. [^tx-gov-437-204-state-military] A member of the Texas military forces ordered to state active duty or state training is entitled to the same benefits and protections that federal law gives persons performing service in the uniformed services. [^tx-gov-437-213-state-duty]

The federal Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act defines an employer as any person, institution, organization or other entity that pays salary or wages for work performed, with no minimum number of employees. [^usc-4303-userra-employer-tx] An employee whose absence is necessitated by uniformed service is entitled to reemployment if the employee gave advance written or verbal notice, the cumulative length of service absences from that employer does not exceed five years, and the employee reports to or applies for reemployment with the employer as the Act provides, subject to the Act's exceptions. [^usc-4312-userra-reemployment-tx]

For a hurricane or other disaster, Labor Code chapter 22 bars an employer of one or more employees from discharging or otherwise discriminating against an employee who leaves the workplace to participate in a general public evacuation ordered under an emergency evacuation order. [^tx-lab-22-001-employer] [^tx-lab-22-002-evacuation] A violating employer is liable for lost wages and employer-provided benefits, and a discharged employee is entitled to reinstatement with commensurate pay. [^tx-lab-22-003-remedies] The protection does not apply to emergency services personnel whose employer provides adequate emergency shelter, or to a person necessary for the safety and well-being of the public, including restoring vital services. [^tx-lab-22-004-exemptions]

## Is tuition remission for employees' children at a Texas K–12 private school excluded from federal income tax? {#employee-tuition-remission}

**Short answer.** Internal Revenue Code § 117(d) excludes from gross income an educational organization's tuition reduction for below-graduate education of an employee or the employee's dependent child, but a highly compensated employee's reduction qualifies only if offered on nondiscriminatory terms. [^irc-117-d-qualified-reduction-tx] [^irc-132-h-dependent-child-tx] [^irc-117-d-3-nondiscrimination-tx]

The exclusion is limited to tuition reductions given by an organization described in § 170(b)(1)(A)(ii), which is an educational organization that normally maintains a regular faculty and curriculum and an enrolled student body at the place where it teaches. [^irc-117-d-qualified-reduction-tx] [^irc-170-b-educational-organization-tx] A K–12 independent school that meets that description falls within the definition, and the education may be at the employer's own school or at another such organization. [^irc-117-d-qualified-reduction-tx]

A reduction that does not qualify, such as one offered only to highly compensated employees, is not excluded by § 117(d) and falls within gross income as compensation for services, including fringe benefits. [^irc-117-d-3-nondiscrimination-tx] [^irc-61-compensation-fringe-tx] The highly compensated employee test uses the definition in Code § 414(q), [^irc-117-d-3-nondiscrimination-tx] which covers a 5-percent owner in the current or preceding year and an employee whose preceding-year compensation exceeded an inflation-adjusted threshold, limited, if the employer so elects, to the top-paid group of employees. [^irc-414-q-hce-definition-tx] IRS Notice 2025-67, which sets the 2026 amounts, states that the threshold remains $160,000. [^irs-notice-2025-67-hce-tx] IRS Publication 970 describes a qualified tuition reduction as one for education below the graduate level, including primary and secondary school. [^irs-pub-970-primary-secondary-tx] The Treasury regulation describes a § 170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational organization as one whose primary function is the presentation of formal instruction, with a regular faculty, curriculum and enrolled student body, so a standalone pre-kindergarten or child care provider falls within it only if formal instruction, rather than custodial care, is its primary function. [^cfr-1-170a-9-c-1-formal-instruction-tx]

No separate state income tax question arises in Texas, because the Texas Constitution bars the legislature from imposing a tax on the net incomes of individuals. [^tx-const-art-8-24a-income-tax]

## Can a Texas private school deduct a signing bonus, tuition or training repayment from a teacher's final paycheck? {#repayment-from-final-pay}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school may deduct a repayment from wages, including final pay, only under a court order, a state or federal law authorizing it, or the employee's written authorization for a lawful purpose. [^tx-lab-61-018-deductions]

The Texas Workforce Commission's rule requires a written authorization to be specific as to the lawful purpose for which the employee accepted the responsibility or liability, to give the employee a reasonable expectation of the amount to be withheld, and to indicate clearly that the deduction will come from wages. [^tx-tac-821-28-specificity] A signed acknowledgment of a handbook or policy manual can serve as the authorization only if it meets those requirements, specifically informs the employee of the deduction, and states that the employee agrees to abide by or be bound to the authorization. [^tx-tac-821-28-handbook]

A written authorization does not displace federal wage law. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act regulations, wages count as paid only if paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear, and an employer-required cost borne by the employee violates the Act in any workweek in which it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages the Act requires. [^cfr-531-35-free-and-clear-tx] An employee who is not paid as the Payday Law requires may file a wage claim with the Commission no later than the 180th day after the wages became due. [^tx-lab-61-051-wage-claim-deduction] The Payday Law governs deductions from wages; whether a separate repayment promise is enforceable as a contract claim against the former employee is addressed in the [Texas stay-or-pay guide](/practice-guides/stay-or-pay/us/texas).

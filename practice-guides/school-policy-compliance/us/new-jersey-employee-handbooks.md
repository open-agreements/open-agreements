# School Policy Compliance in New Jersey: Employee Handbook Notices and Workplace Rules[^about]

What a New Jersey independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's employee handbook and workplace policies must reflect: required notices, anti-harassment policies, confidentiality clauses on discrimination claims, vehicle tracking, personal accounts, off-duty cannabis use, pay transparency, whistleblower notice and NLRB work-rule standards.

## What written notices and postings must a New Jersey independent school give its employees, and can the handbook carry them? {#handbook-required-notices}

**Short answer.** A New Jersey school must post and give each employee the earned sick leave notice, must display and, with ten or more employees, annually distribute its whistleblower notice, and, with fifty or more employees, must post and annually distribute the gender equity notice. [^nj-esl-notice] [^nj-cepa-notice] [^nj-cepa-notice-small-employer] [^nj-gender-equity-posting] [^nj-gender-equity-written-copy]

| Requirement | What the law requires | When or where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earned sick leave, N.J.S.A. 34:11D-7 | The Commissioner's notice of earned sick leave rights, posted and given to each employee in writing | Within 30 days after the form issued, at hiring, and on first request [^nj-esl-notice] |
| Whistleblower protection, N.J.S.A. 34:19-7 | Notice of employees' protections, obligations, rights and procedures, in English, Spanish and, at the employer's discretion, any other language spoken by a majority of its employees, naming the person designated to receive written notifications | Displayed conspicuously and distributed annually, with annual distribution excused below ten employees [^nj-cepa-notice] [^nj-cepa-notice-small-employer] |
| Gender equity in pay, N.J.S.A. 34:11-56.12 | The Commissioner's notice of the right to be free of gender inequity or bias in pay and other terms, with a signed acknowledgment | Posted, and given in writing at hiring, annually by December 31 and on first request, for employers with fifty or more employees [^nj-gender-equity-posting] [^nj-gender-equity-written-copy] |
| Rate of pay and payday, N.J.S.A. 34:11-4.6 | Notice of the rate of pay and the regular payday | At hiring, and before any change [^nj-wpl-hiring-notice] |
| Law Against Discrimination, N.J.A.C. 13:8-1.2 | The Division on Civil Rights' official employment poster, displayed and given to each employee in writing, by email, as printed material such as an attachment to an employee manual, or on an employee website | Displayed where easily visible to employees and applicants, and given annually by December 31 and on first request [^nj-dcr-employment-poster] [^nj-dcr-poster-written-copy] |
| Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-10, for employers with fifteen or more employees | A posted notice prepared or approved by the EEOC | Posted where notices are customarily posted [^usc-2000e-10-posting-handbook] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-handbook] |

The gender equity statute names a handbook as one acceptable delivery method: the written copy may be given through printed material, including an attachment to an employee manual or policy book, and the employee must sign and return an acknowledgment within thirty days. [^nj-gender-equity-written-copy] The whistleblower statute applies to every employer, including any association or corporation, with the annual distribution requirement excused only for an employer with fewer than ten employees. [^nj-cepa-employer-defined] [^nj-cepa-notice-small-employer] The Earned Sick Leave Law defines an employer to include any educational institution, nonprofit agency or corporation that employs employees in New Jersey, which reaches an independent or religious school. [^nj-esl-employer-defined] The other entries are postings or individual notices that a handbook can reproduce or sit beside, and each statute fixes its own timing.

## Does a New Jersey school's anti-harassment policy affect its liability when an employee claims sexual harassment by a supervisor? {#anti-harassment-policy}

**Short answer.** Under *Aguas v. State*, an employer facing a supervisor sexual harassment claim alleging a hostile work environment under the Law Against Discrimination, with no tangible employment action taken, may assert an affirmative defense to vicarious liability based on its anti-harassment policy, unless the policy is ineffective or unenforced. [^aguas-adopts-defense] [^aguas-ineffective-policy]

The defense to vicarious liability requires the employer to show that it exercised reasonable care to prevent and promptly correct harassment and that the employee unreasonably failed to use the preventive or corrective opportunities it provided. [^aguas-two-prongs] For a direct negligence claim against the employer, *Gaines v. Bellino* identified the factors that bear on the employer's care: formal policies prohibiting harassment, formal and informal complaint structures, anti-harassment training that is mandatory for supervisors and managers and available to all employees, effective monitoring of the policies and complaint structures, and an unequivocal commitment from the highest levels of the employer, shown by consistent practice. [^gaines-five-factors] *Gaines* treats the efficacy of the employer's remedial program as highly pertinent to its defense. [^gaines-remedial-program-efficacy]

The Law Against Discrimination reaches every employer, defined to include associations, organizations and corporations, unless specifically exempt under another section of the Act. [^nj-lad-employer-defined] The religious-employer provisions of that Act are addressed in the [guide on discrimination law and religious employers](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/new-jersey-religious-employers).

## Can a New Jersey school's employment agreement or settlement require an employee to keep a discrimination or harassment claim confidential? {#confidentiality-of-discrimination-claims}

**Short answer.** New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination makes a provision in an employment contract or settlement agreement that has the purpose or effect of concealing details of a discrimination, retaliation or harassment claim unenforceable against the employee who is a party to it. [^nj-lad-12-8-concealment]

The provision also becomes unenforceable against the employer if the employee publicly reveals enough detail that the employer is reasonably identifiable, and every settlement of such a claim must carry a bold, prominently placed notice saying so. [^nj-lad-12-8-employer-and-notice] The statute does not bar an agreement not to compete or an agreement not to disclose proprietary information, limited to non-public trade secrets, business plans and customer information. [^nj-lad-12-8-carve-outs] The rule applies to contracts and agreements entered into, renewed, modified or amended on or after March 18, 2019. [^nj-pl-2019-c39-effective]

*Savage v. Township of Neptune* arose from a suit by a police sergeant accusing the police department where she worked, among others, of discrimination, retaliation and sexual harassment. [^savage-facts-suit] After a television reporter interviewed the sergeant and she commented about the matter, the defendants moved to enforce the parties' settlement agreement, and the New Jersey Supreme Court held that the Law Against Discrimination protects her statements. [^savage-facts-interview] The label on a clause does not control: the Court held that the meaning of section 12.8 turns on what the law says rather than on labels, [^savage-section-12-8-labels] and held a settlement's non-disparagement clause against public policy and unenforceable because it would conceal details of the employee's claims. [^savage-non-disparagement] A party that enforces or attempts to enforce a provision barred by these sections is liable for the employee's reasonable attorney fees and costs. [^nj-lad-fees-handbook]

The same law makes unenforceable an employment-contract provision that waives a substantive or procedural right or remedy relating to a discrimination, retaliation or harassment claim, and bars prospective waiver of any Law Against Discrimination right. [^nj-lad-12-7-waiver] The Appellate Division held in *Antonucci v. Curvature Newco, Inc.* that the Federal Arbitration Act pre-empts that waiver provision when it is applied to prevent arbitration under an agreement governed by the federal Act, so section 12.7 does not by itself invalidate an arbitration clause in such an agreement. [^antonucci-faa-preemption]

## Must a New Jersey school give written notice before using a tracking device on a vehicle an employee drives? {#vehicle-tracking-notice}

**Short answer.** A New Jersey employer that knowingly uses a tracking device in a vehicle used by an employee without giving the employee written notice is subject to a civil penalty of up to $1,000 for the first violation and up to $2,500 for each later violation. [^nj-tracking-notice-penalty]

The statute defines a tracking device as one designed or intended solely to track the movement of a vehicle, person or device, and excludes devices used to document employee expense reimbursement. [^nj-tracking-definitions] Its definition of employer excludes the Department of Corrections, the State Parole Board, county correctional facilities, State and local government entities and public transportation systems, so a private or religious school is within it. [^nj-tracking-definitions] The notice duty attaches to tracking devices in vehicles; this question does not address monitoring of email, telephone or internet use.

## May a New Jersey school require employees or applicants to give access to their personal social media accounts? {#personal-social-media-accounts}

**Short answer.** A New Jersey employer may not require or request a current or prospective employee to provide or disclose a user name or password, or otherwise give the employer access to a personal account through an electronic communications device, subject to exceptions the statute lists. [^nj-social-media-prohibition] The exceptions preserve compliance with other law, policies on employer-issued devices and on accounts or services the employer provides or the employee uses for business, specified investigations based on specific information, and use of public-domain information. [^nj-social-media-exceptions]

A personal account is one on a social networking website used exclusively for personal communications unrelated to the employer's business, and the definition excludes any account created, maintained, used or accessed for the employer's business purposes. [^nj-social-media-personal-account] The investigation exception is limited to two purposes, each triggered by specific information the employer has received: ensuring compliance with applicable laws, regulatory requirements or prohibitions against work-related misconduct based on activity on a personal account, and investigating an unauthorized transfer of the employer's proprietary information, confidential information or financial data to a personal account. [^nj-social-media-exceptions] Neither exception authorizes a general demand for personal-account credentials outside those limits. [^nj-social-media-prohibition] [^nj-social-media-exceptions] An employer may not retaliate or discriminate against an individual for refusing a prohibited request or for reporting, testifying about or opposing a violation, [^nj-social-media-no-retaliation] and a violation carries a civil penalty of up to $1,000 for the first violation and $2,500 for each later violation. [^nj-social-media-penalty] The statute's definition of employer excludes only corrections agencies and law enforcement agencies, so it reaches a private or religious school. [^nj-social-media-personal-account]

## Can a New Jersey school discipline an employee for off-duty cannabis use or a positive cannabis test? {#off-duty-cannabis-use}

**Short answer.** New Jersey bars an employer from refusing to hire, discharging or acting adversely against an employee because the person does or does not use cannabis, or solely because of cannabinoid metabolites from lawful use, while preserving drug-free workplace rules and drug testing on listed grounds. [^nj-creamma-no-adverse-action] [^nj-creamma-drug-free-workplace] [^nj-creamma-test-grounds]

The statute permits a drug test on reasonable suspicion of cannabis use while the employee is performing work responsibilities, on observable signs of intoxication related to cannabis use, after a work-related accident the employer investigates, randomly, before employment, or as regular screening to determine use during prescribed work hours. [^nj-creamma-test-grounds] The test must combine scientifically reliable objective testing, such as blood, urine or saliva, with a physical evaluation by an individual holding the necessary certification, and the employer may then use the results in deciding discipline. [^nj-creamma-test-method] The statute calls that credential a Workplace Impairment Recognition Evaluator certification and directs the Cannabis Regulatory Commission, in consultation with the Department of Labor and Workforce Development, to prescribe its standards in regulation; that text is as amended by P.L.2025, c.325, approved January 20, 2026 and effective on the ninetieth day after enactment. [^nj-creamma-evaluator-certification] [^nj-pl-2025-c325-effective] An employer may keep a drug- and alcohol-free workplace and prohibit use, possession or intoxication during work hours, and need not permit or accommodate cannabis in the workplace. [^nj-creamma-drug-free-workplace] An employer subject to a federal contract that can show a provable adverse impact may revise its prohibitions to conform to federal law. [^nj-creamma-federal-contract]

The Commission's separate September 9, 2022 guidance, issued under the earlier name Workplace Impairment Recognition Expert (WIRE), states that it serves until the Commission formulates and approves standards for WIRE certifications, [^crc-guidance-interim] and lets an employer designate an interim staff member to assist with determinations of suspected cannabis use during work hours. [^crc-guidance-interim-designee] The guidance also states that a test showing cannabinoid metabolites alone is insufficient to support an adverse employment action, while such a test combined with evidence-based documentation of impairment during work hours may be sufficient. [^crc-guidance-metabolites] As of the November 22, 2026 review, the most recent Commission record cited here is that 2022 guidance. Read together, the statute and guidance support action on documented impairment or on-duty use, but not on a positive test for lawful off-duty use alone. [^nj-creamma-no-adverse-action] [^crc-guidance-metabolites]

Tobacco is treated separately: an employer may not take adverse action because a person does or does not smoke or use tobacco products unless it has a rational basis reasonably related to the employment. [^nj-tobacco-off-duty]

## Must a New Jersey school's job postings include a pay range? {#pay-range-job-postings}

**Short answer.** Since June 1, 2025, a New Jersey employer with ten or more employees over twenty calendar weeks must disclose in each posting for a new job or transfer opportunity the hourly wage or salary, or a range, and a general description of benefits and other compensation programs. [^nj-pay-transparency-disclosure] [^nj-pay-transparency-employer] [^njdol-pay-transparency-effective]

The duty covers postings advertised externally or internally, and an employer may still raise the wages, benefits and compensation at the time of an offer. [^nj-pay-transparency-disclosure] An employer must also make reasonable efforts to make known to all current employees in the affected department or departments a promotion opportunity it advertises, before deciding the promotion, although a promotion awarded on the basis of years of experience or performance, or made on an emergent basis, is not subject to that notice. [^nj-pay-transparency-promotion] The statute defines an employer to include any corporation or association with ten or more employees over twenty calendar weeks that does business, employs persons or takes applications for employment in New Jersey, which reaches a nonprofit school of that size. [^nj-pay-transparency-employer] Civil penalties are up to $300 for a first violation and $600 for each later violation, and all postings for one opening count as one violation. [^nj-pay-transparency-penalties]

## What whistleblower notice must a New Jersey school post and distribute, and which employers does the Conscientious Employee Protection Act cover? {#whistleblower-protection-notice}

**Short answer.** The Conscientious Employee Protection Act defines an employer to include any individual, partnership, association or corporation and requires an employer to display notice of employees' whistleblower protections, obligations, rights and procedures and, unless it has fewer than ten employees, to distribute that notice to all employees annually. [^nj-cepa-employer-defined-wb] [^nj-cepa-notice-wb] [^nj-cepa-notice-small-employer-wb]

The notice must be in English and Spanish and, at the employer's discretion, any other language spoken by a majority of its employees, and must name the person or persons the employer has designated to receive employees' written notifications of suspected violations. [^nj-cepa-notice-wb] An employer with fewer than ten employees must still display the notice but is excused from the annual distribution. [^nj-cepa-notice-small-employer-wb] The statute protects any individual who performs services for and under the control and direction of an employer for wages or other remuneration. [^nj-cepa-employee-defined]

The designated person matters because protection for a disclosure to a public body generally depends on the employee first bringing the activity to a supervisor's attention by written notice and giving the employer a reasonable opportunity to correct it, unless the employee is reasonably certain a supervisor already knows or reasonably fears physical harm in an emergency. [^nj-cepa-written-notice-prerequisite] A supervisor includes the person the employer designates on the required notice. [^nj-cepa-supervisor-defined]

## Do NLRB standards for handbook work rules apply to a nonprofit independent school in New Jersey? {#nlrb-handbook-rules}

**Short answer.** The National Labor Relations Board applies a $1 million annual minimum to private and nonprofit schools, and where it has jurisdiction its 2023 *Stericycle* standard presumes unlawful a work rule that reasonably tends to chill employees' exercise of labor-law rights. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools] [^nlrb-stericycle-standard]

The Board states that it will not assert jurisdiction over employees of a religious organization who are involved in effectuating its religious purpose, such as teachers in church-operated schools. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-religious] Under *Stericycle*, an employer can rebut the presumption by proving that the rule advances a legitimate and substantial business interest that a more narrowly tailored rule could not advance. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard] *NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago* arose from the Board's exercise of jurisdiction over lay faculty members at two groups of Catholic high schools. [^catholic-bishop-facts] In that case the Supreme Court declined to construe the Act to reach teachers in church-operated schools absent a clear expression of congressional intent, because doing so would raise questions under the Religion Clauses. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools]

As of the November 22, 2026 review, the most recent Board record cited here is the Board's August 2, 2023 announcement of the *Stericycle* standard. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard] A secular nonprofit school that meets the $1 million minimum is within the Board's jurisdiction, and its handbook rules on confidentiality, social media and workplace conduct are measured against that standard. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools] *Catholic Bishop* addressed teachers in church-operated schools, so it does not by itself remove a religious school's non-teaching staff from the Act. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard] [^catholic-bishop-church-schools]

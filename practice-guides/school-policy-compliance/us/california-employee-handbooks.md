# School Policy Compliance in California: Employee Handbook Notices and Workplace Rules[^about]

What a California independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's employee handbook and workplace policies must reflect: required notices, harassment prevention and training, confidentiality clauses on harassment claims, vehicle tracking, personal social media, off-duty cannabis use, pay transparency, whistleblower notice and NLRB work-rule standards.

## What written notices and postings must a California independent school give its employees, and can the handbook carry them? {#handbook-required-notices}

**Short answer.** A California school must give its nonexempt employees, apart from those under qualifying collective bargaining agreements, a written pay notice at hire, post the paid sick leave poster and a list of whistleblower rights, and give every employee an annual stand-alone Workplace Know Your Rights notice. [^ca-lab-2810-5-notice] [^ca-lab-2810-5-exclusions] [^ca-lab-247-poster] [^ca-lab-1102-8-list-notices] [^ca-lab-1553-notice]

| Requirement | What the law requires | When or where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pay notice at hire, Lab. Code § 2810.5 | Written notice of pay rates, allowances, regular payday, employer identity and contact details, the workers' compensation carrier, and paid sick leave rights; public employees, overtime-exempt employees and employees under a valid collective bargaining agreement that expressly sets wages, hours and working conditions, pays premium rates for all overtime and pays at least 30 percent above the state minimum wage are excluded | At hiring, with written notice of changes within seven calendar days unless a timely wage statement or another required writing reflects them [^ca-lab-2810-5-notice] [^ca-lab-2810-5-exclusions] |
| Paid sick leave, Lab. Code § 247 | A poster stating the right to accrue, request and use paid sick days, the amount, the terms of use and the bar on retaliation | Displayed in a conspicuous place in each workplace [^ca-lab-247-poster] |
| Whistleblower rights, Lab. Code § 1102.8 | A list of employees' rights and responsibilities under the whistleblower laws, including the state whistleblower hotline number, in lettering larger than 14-point type | Prominently displayed; posting the State model list complies [^ca-lab-1102-8-list-notices] |
| Workplace Know Your Rights, Lab. Code § 1553 | A stand-alone written notice describing rights to workers' compensation, to notice of immigration inspections, against unfair immigration-related practices, to organize, and when interacting with law enforcement at the workplace | By February 1, 2026 and annually after that, and to each new employee at hire [^ca-lab-1553-notice] |
| Emergency contact, Lab. Code § 1555 | An opportunity to name an emergency contact to be told if the employee is arrested or detained at the worksite | By March 30, 2026 for existing employees, and at hiring after that [^ca-lab-1555-emergency-contact] |
| Harassment poster and information sheet, Gov. Code § 12950 | The Civil Rights Department's discrimination poster and transgender rights poster, and its sexual harassment information sheet or equivalent information | Posted in a prominent and accessible location, and delivered to each employee, for example with pay [^ca-gov-12950-posters] [^ca-gov-12950-information-sheet] |
| Mandated reporter statement, Penal Code §§ 11165.7, 11166.5 | Every private school employee is a mandated reporter and signs, on the employer's form, a statement acknowledging the reporting duty, with copies of the reporting statutes | Before employment begins, and private school training annually from July 1, 2026 [^ca-pen-11165-7-private-school] [^ca-pen-11166-5-statement] [^ca-pen-11165-7-annual-training] |
| Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-10, for employers with fifteen or more employees | A posted notice prepared or approved by the EEOC | Posted where notices are customarily posted [^usc-2000e-10-posting-ca-hb] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-ca-hb] |

The Workplace Know Your Rights notice must be a stand-alone written notice delivered in the way the employer normally communicates employment information, such as personal service, email or text message if it can reasonably be expected to reach the employee within one business day, and it must also go annually to the employee's authorized representative, if any; a section of a handbook alone does not match the statute's terms. [^ca-lab-1553-notice] The employer must keep records of compliance for three years, including the date each notice is provided or sent. [^ca-lab-1553-records] The mandated reporter statement is signed by the employee and retained by the employer, which bears the cost of printing and filing it. [^ca-pen-11166-5-statement]

None of the Labor Code sections in the table excludes nonprofit or religious employers; the paid sick leave article, for example, defines an employer as any person employing another under any appointment or contract of hire. [^ca-lab-245-5-employer] The Penal Code names private school employees expressly. [^ca-pen-11165-7-private-school] The § 12950 poster and information-sheet duties fall under the general definition in the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA), which covers a person regularly employing five or more persons and excludes a religious association or corporation not organized for private profit. [^ca-gov-12926-employer-notices] A nonprofit public benefit corporation formed by or affiliated with a religion that operates a school as its sole or primary activity may restrict employment to members of that religion but is subject to FEHA in all other respects. [^ca-gov-12926-2-f-notices]

## Does a California school's anti-harassment policy and training affect its liability when an employee claims sexual harassment by a supervisor? {#harassment-policy-and-training}

**Short answer.** Under *State Department of Health Services v. Superior Court*, a California employer is strictly liable under FEHA for a supervisor's sexual harassment, but its prevention measures and complaint procedures can reduce the damages the employee recovers. [^ca-mcginnis-strict-liability] [^ca-mcginnis-three-elements]

The avoidable consequences defense has three elements: the employer took reasonable steps to prevent and correct workplace sexual harassment, the employee unreasonably failed to use the preventive and corrective measures the employer provided, and reasonable use of those procedures would have prevented at least some of the harm. [^ca-mcginnis-three-elements] An employee's failure to report harassment is not a defense on the merits and at most reduces the damages recoverable. [^ca-mcginnis-not-merits-defense] FEHA itself requires an entity to take all reasonable steps to prevent harassment, and makes harassment by a coworker unlawful if the entity knew or should have known of it and failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective action. [^ca-gov-12940-j1-reasonable-steps]

Government Code § 12950.1 requires an employer with five or more employees to provide at least two hours of interactive sexual harassment training to supervisory employees and one hour to nonsupervisory employees in California every two years, with new employees and new supervisors trained within six months. [^ca-gov-12950-1-training] Seasonal, temporary or other employees hired to work for less than six months must be trained within 30 calendar days after hire or within 100 hours worked, whichever comes first. [^ca-gov-12950-1-short-term] The training must include prevention of abusive conduct and harassment based on gender identity, gender expression and sexual orientation. [^ca-gov-12950-1-components] A claim that the training did not reach a particular individual does not by itself create liability, and compliance does not insulate the employer from liability. [^ca-gov-12950-1-no-insulation] Beginning January 1, 2028, AB 1803 (Stats. 2026, ch. 547) adds anti-hate speech training as a required component: practical guidance on recognizing, reporting and confronting workplace speech that vilifies, humiliates or incites hatred based on the characteristics protected by § 12940(a); a training that covers unlawful harassment and abusive conduct based on those characteristics satisfies the requirement. [^ca-ab-1803-anti-hate-speech]

Coverage turns on how the school is organized. FEHA's harassment prohibition applies to an employer of one or more persons, but for that subdivision it excludes a religious association or corporation not organized for private profit, except as provided in Government Code § 12926.2. [^ca-gov-12940-j4-employer] [^ca-gov-12926-2-f-harassment] In *Henry v. Red Hill Evangelical Lutheran Church of Tustin*, the Court of Appeal held that a church organized as a religious corporation not for private profit, whose school had no legal status apart from the church, was not an employer subject to FEHA's prohibitions, and noted that no evidence showed the school was a nonprofit public benefit corporation. [^ca-henry-not-employer] [^ca-henry-no-separate-status] A religiously affiliated nonprofit public benefit corporation that operates a school as its sole or primary activity is subject to FEHA apart from religious hiring preferences. [^ca-gov-12926-2-f-harassment] The religious-employer provisions of FEHA are addressed in the [guide on discrimination law and religious employers](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/california-religious-employers).

The training statute uses its own definition of employer, for that section only, as any person regularly employing five or more persons or regularly receiving the services of five or more contractors, and that definition does not repeat the religious-corporation exclusion found in Government Code § 12926(d) and § 12940(j)(4)(B). [^ca-gov-12950-1-employer] [^ca-gov-12926-employer-harassment] [^ca-gov-12940-j4-employer] On the statutory text, a religious corporation school with five or more employees or contractors is therefore within the training duty even though it falls outside FEHA's general definition of employer; a November 23, 2026 CourtListener search of California Supreme Court and Court of Appeal opinions found none that cite § 12950.1 and mention a religious employer, and the conservative course for such a school is to provide the training on the statutory schedule. [^ca-gov-12950-1-employer] AB 1803 re-enacts the section's own definition of employer without adding a religious exclusion. [^ca-ab-1803-employer-unchanged]

## Can a California school's employment, separation or settlement agreement require an employee to keep harassment or discrimination claims confidential? {#confidentiality-of-harassment-claims}

**Short answer.** Under Government Code § 12964.5, a California employer may not require an employee, as a condition of employment or for a raise or bonus, to sign a nondisparagement agreement that denies the right to disclose information about unlawful acts in the workplace, and such an agreement is unenforceable. [^ca-gov-12964-5-condition]

A nondisparagement or other provision restricting disclosure of workplace conditions must include, in substantial form, the statutory sentence: "Nothing in this agreement prevents you from discussing or disclosing information about unlawful acts in the workplace, such as harassment or discrimination or any other conduct that you have reason to believe is unlawful."[^ca-gov-12964-5-condition] [^ca-gov-12964-5-condition] A separation agreement may not prohibit disclosure of information about unlawful acts in the workplace, must carry the same sentence in any nondisparagement provision, may still contain an otherwise lawful general release, and must be offered with notice of the right to consult an attorney and at least five business days to do so. [^ca-gov-12964-5-separation] The section does not apply to a negotiated settlement of a FEHA claim already filed in court, with an agency, in an alternative dispute resolution forum or through the employer's internal complaint process, and it permits confidentiality of a severance amount and protection of trade secrets and confidential information that do not involve unlawful acts. [^ca-gov-12964-5-exceptions]

For a claim filed in a civil action or a complaint filed in an administrative action, Code of Civil Procedure § 1001 prohibits a settlement provision that restricts disclosure of factual information about sexual assault not governed by § 1002(a), sexual harassment as defined in Civil Code § 51.9, or workplace harassment or discrimination, failure to prevent it, or retaliation as described in Government Code § 12940(a), (h), (i), (j) and (k). [^ca-ccp-1001-prohibition] Apart from the claimant-identity provision, such a term in an agreement entered into on or after January 1, 2019 is void, but the 2021 amendments that broadened the workplace prong apply only to agreements entered into on or after January 1, 2022. [^ca-ccp-1001-exceptions] [^ca-ccp-1001-sb331-dates] At the claimant's request, a settlement may shield the claimant's identity unless a government agency or public official is a party, and a provision keeping the settlement amount confidential remains permitted. [^ca-ccp-1001-exceptions]

Section 12964.5 defines an unlawful employment practice within FEHA, so it reaches the employers within FEHA's general definition: a person regularly employing five or more persons, excluding a religious association or corporation not organized for private profit. [^ca-gov-12926-employer-nda] Section 1001's workplace prong describes the covered claims by reference to FEHA's harassment, discrimination and retaliation provisions, so its text does not settle whether that prong reaches a settlement with a religious corporation that FEHA excludes from its definition of employer, while the sexual assault and Civil Code § 51.9 sexual harassment prongs carry no such cross-reference; a November 23, 2026 CourtListener search of California Supreme Court and Court of Appeal opinions found none that cite Code of Civil Procedure § 1001 and mention a religious employer, and the conservative course is to draft any settlement of such a claim as though § 1001 applies. [^ca-ccp-1001-prohibition] [^ca-gov-12926-employer-nda]

Arbitration is treated separately. Labor Code § 432.6 bars requiring an applicant or employee, as a condition of employment, to waive any right, forum or procedure for a violation of FEHA or the Labor Code, while stating that it does not invalidate an arbitration agreement otherwise enforceable under the Federal Arbitration Act. [^ca-lab-432-6-waiver] [^usc-9-2-arbitration-ca-hb] In *Chamber of Commerce of the United States v. Bonta*, the Ninth Circuit held that the Federal Arbitration Act preempts that statute as a whole to the extent it applies to arbitration agreements. [^ca-chamber-bonta-preemption] The federal Act makes a written arbitration provision in a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce valid, irrevocable and enforceable, save on grounds that exist for revoking any contract or as provided in its chapter 4. [^usc-9-2-arbitration-ca-hb]

## Can a California school use a GPS or other electronic tracking device on a vehicle an employee drives? {#vehicle-tracking}

**Short answer.** California Penal Code § 637.7 makes it a misdemeanor for any person or entity to use an electronic tracking device to determine a person's location or movement, except when the vehicle's registered owner, lessor or lessee consents to its use on that vehicle. [^ca-pen-637-7]

The statute defines an electronic tracking device as any device attached to a vehicle or other movable thing that reveals its location or movement by transmitting electronic signals, and it also excepts lawful use by a law enforcement agency. [^ca-pen-637-7] Because the exception turns on the consent of the registered owner, lessor or lessee, a school that owns or leases a vehicle supplies that consent itself, while for a vehicle the employee owns or leases, the consent that the statute recognizes is the employee's. [^ca-pen-637-7] The prohibition applies to any person or entity in the State, which includes a private or religious school. [^ca-pen-637-7] This question addresses devices attached to vehicles and other movable things; it does not address monitoring of email, telephone or internet use.

## May a California school require employees or applicants to give access to their personal social media? {#personal-social-media-accounts}

**Short answer.** California Labor Code § 980 bars an employer from requiring or requesting an employee or applicant to disclose a username or password for personal social media, to access it in the employer's presence, or to divulge it, subject to exceptions for misconduct investigations and employer-issued devices. [^ca-lab-980-prohibition] [^ca-lab-980-exceptions]

The employer may still request personal social media reasonably believed to be relevant to an investigation of employee misconduct or violation of law, if it is used solely for that investigation or a related proceeding, and may require a username or password to access an employer-issued electronic device. [^ca-lab-980-exceptions] Neither exception authorizes a general demand for personal-account credentials. [^ca-lab-980-prohibition] [^ca-lab-980-exceptions]

Social media is defined broadly, to include electronic services, accounts and content such as videos, photographs, blogs, podcasts, instant and text messages, email and online profiles. [^ca-lab-980-prohibition] The employer may not discharge, discipline, threaten or otherwise retaliate against an employee or applicant for refusing a request that violates the section, although it may take adverse action otherwise permitted by law. [^ca-lab-980-retaliation] The section applies to an employer without a nonprofit or religious exclusion in its text, so it reaches a private or religious school. [^ca-lab-980-prohibition]

## Can a California school refuse to hire or discipline an employee for off-duty cannabis use or a positive cannabis test? {#off-duty-cannabis-use}

**Short answer.** Since January 1, 2024, Government Code § 12954 has made it unlawful for a covered California employer to discriminate against a person for off-the-job cannabis use away from the workplace or for a drug test finding nonpsychoactive cannabis metabolites, subject to exemptions the statute lists. [^ca-gov-12954-off-duty] [^ca-gov-12954-operative]

Subdivision (a) does not apply to an employee in the building and construction trades; the section does not apply to applicants or employees hired for positions that require a federal background investigation or security clearance under the Department of Defense's regulations or equivalent agency rules; and it does not preempt state or federal laws requiring applicants or employees to be tested for controlled substances, or governing how they are tested, as a condition of employment, federal funding, federal licensing-related benefits or a federal contract. [^ca-gov-12954-off-duty] [^ca-gov-12954-exemptions] An employer may still act on scientifically valid preemployment drug screening conducted through methods that do not screen for nonpsychoactive cannabis metabolites. [^ca-gov-12954-off-duty]

The statute also makes it unlawful to request information from an applicant about prior cannabis use, and information about prior use obtained from criminal history is covered unless state or federal law permits the employer to consider it. [^ca-gov-12954-applicant-inquiry] The section does not permit an employee to possess, be impaired by or use cannabis on the job, and it does not affect an employer's right to maintain a drug- and alcohol-free workplace. [^ca-gov-12954-workplace] Read together, these provisions let an employer act on possession, impairment or use on the job, but not on off-the-job use away from the workplace or on an employer-required test result showing nonpsychoactive cannabis metabolites. [^ca-gov-12954-off-duty] [^ca-gov-12954-workplace]

The Civil Rights Department treats these protections as part of FEHA and applies them only to employers with five or more employees. [^ca-hb-crd-cannabis-feha] [^ca-hb-crd-cannabis-five] FEHA's definitions apply in connection with its unlawful practices unless a different meaning clearly appears from the context, [^ca-hb-henry-definitions] and its general definition of employer is a person regularly employing five or more persons, excluding a religious association or corporation not organized for private profit. [^ca-gov-12926-employer-cannabis] On that text, a school run by a nonprofit religious association or corporation appears to fall outside § 12954, though in our review of California appellate decisions on CourtListener we found none applying § 12954 to a religious employer; the conservative course for such a school is to avoid adverse action based only on off-duty use or a nonpsychoactive-metabolite result and to rely on the impairment and drug-free-workplace provisions instead. [^ca-gov-12926-employer-cannabis] [^ca-gov-12954-workplace] A religiously affiliated nonprofit public benefit corporation that operates a school as its sole or primary activity is subject to FEHA in all respects other than religious hiring preferences, which brings it within § 12954. [^ca-gov-12926-2-f-cannabis]

## Must a California school's job postings include a pay range? {#pay-range-job-postings}

**Short answer.** A California employer with 15 or more employees must include the pay scale for a position in any job posting, and every employer must provide the pay scale to an applicant on reasonable request. [^ca-lab-432-3-pay-scale]

An employer must also give a current employee, on request, the pay scale for the employee's position, and an employer with 15 or more employees that uses a third party to post a job must give the pay scale to that third party, which must include it in the posting. [^ca-lab-432-3-pay-scale] The pay scale is a good faith estimate of the salary or hourly wage range the employer reasonably expects to pay for the position on hire. [^ca-lab-432-3-definitions] The employer must keep records of each employee's job title and wage rate history for the duration of employment plus three years. [^ca-lab-432-3-pay-scale]

The same section bars an employer from relying on an applicant's salary history in deciding whether to hire or what to pay, and from seeking that history, orally or in writing, personally or through an agent. [^ca-lab-432-3-salary-history] An applicant may still volunteer salary history without prompting, and the employer may then consider that voluntarily disclosed information in setting the applicant's salary. [^ca-lab-432-3-voluntary] The section applies to all employers, including state and local government employers, so it reaches a private or religious school. [^ca-lab-432-3-all-employers] The Labor Commissioner may order a civil penalty of $100 to $10,000 per violation, and for a first violation of the pay scale provisions no penalty is assessed if the employer shows that all job postings for open positions have been updated to include the pay scale. [^ca-lab-432-3-penalties]

## What whistleblower notice must a California school post, and can its policies limit reports to government agencies? {#whistleblower-protection-notice}

**Short answer.** California Labor Code § 1102.8 requires an employer to display prominently, in lettering larger than 14-point type, a list of employees' rights and responsibilities under the whistleblower laws, including the state whistleblower hotline number. [^ca-lab-1102-8-list]

Posting the model list the Labor Commissioner prepares satisfies that requirement. [^ca-lab-1102-8-list] Labor Code § 1102.5 separately bars an employer from making, adopting or enforcing any rule, regulation or policy that prevents an employee from disclosing information to a government or law enforcement agency, a person with authority over the employee, another employee with authority to investigate or correct the violation, or a public body, if the employee has reasonable cause to believe the information discloses a violation of law. [^ca-lab-1102-5-policy] The employer may not retaliate against an employee for such a disclosure, or because it believes the employee disclosed or may disclose such information, regardless of whether disclosing it is part of the employee's job duties, [^ca-lab-1102-5-disclosure-retaliation] or for refusing to participate in an activity that would violate a statute, rule or regulation. [^ca-lab-1102-5-retaliation]

A handbook confidentiality rule is measured against that standard, subject to an exception for rules that implement the lawyer-client privilege, the physician-patient privilege or trade secret protection. [^ca-lab-1102-5-exception] In addition to other remedies, an employer is liable for a civil penalty of up to $10,000 per employee for each violation, awarded to the employee retaliated against. [^ca-lab-1102-5-penalty] Neither section contains an exclusion for nonprofit or religious employers, so both reach a private or religious school. [^ca-lab-1102-5-policy] [^ca-lab-1102-8-list]

## Do NLRB standards for handbook work rules apply to a nonprofit independent school in California? {#nlrb-handbook-rules}

**Short answer.** The National Labor Relations Board applies a $1 million annual minimum to private and nonprofit schools, and where it has jurisdiction its 2023 *Stericycle* standard presumes unlawful a work rule that reasonably tends to chill employees' exercise of labor-law rights. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools-ca-hb] [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-ca-hb]

The Board states that it will not assert jurisdiction over employees of a religious organization who are involved in effectuating its religious purpose, such as teachers in church-operated schools. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-religious-ca-hb] Under *Stericycle*, an employer can rebut the presumption by proving that the rule advances a legitimate and substantial business interest that a more narrowly tailored rule could not advance. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-ca-hb] In *NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago*, the Court declined to construe the Act to reach teachers in church-operated schools absent a clear expression of congressional intent, because doing so would raise questions under the Religion Clauses. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools-ca-hb] *Catholic Bishop* addressed teachers in church-operated schools, so it does not by itself remove a religious school's non-teaching staff from the Act. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools-ca-hb] [^nlrb-jurisdiction-religious-ca-hb]

Separation agreements are also measured against the Act: in *McLaren Macomb*, the Board held that simply offering employees a severance agreement requiring them to broadly give up their rights under Section 7 of the Act violates Section 8(a)(1). [^nlrb-mclaren-macomb-ca-hb]

As of the November 23, 2026 review, the most recent official record cited here is General Counsel Memorandum GC 26-04 of August 26, 2026, which states that the General Counsel is arguing to the Board to overturn *Stericycle* and to overrule *McLaren Macomb* in pending cases. [^nlrb-gc-26-04-positions-ca-hb] The same memorandum directs the Regions to continue to investigate and prosecute cases according to extant Board law, so *Stericycle* and *McLaren Macomb* remain the standards the Regions apply. [^nlrb-gc-26-04-extant-law-ca-hb] Because the Board may change the standard, the conservative course is to draft handbook confidentiality, social media and conduct rules, and separation-agreement terms, so they satisfy *Stericycle* and *McLaren Macomb* as they stand. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-ca-hb] [^nlrb-mclaren-macomb-ca-hb]

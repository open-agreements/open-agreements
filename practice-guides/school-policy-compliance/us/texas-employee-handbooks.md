# School Policy Compliance in Texas: Employee Handbook Notices and Workplace Rules[^about]

What a Texas independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's employee handbook and workplace policies must reflect: required postings, the 2021 sexual harassment statute, confidentiality and arbitration of harassment claims, drug testing, paydays and final pay, firearms in parking lots, retaliation for reporting, at-will disclaimers and NLRB work-rule standards.

## What written notices and postings must a Texas independent school give its employees, and can the handbook carry them? {#handbook-required-notices}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school must post notices of its designated paydays and of whether it has workers' compensation insurance coverage, and it must tell each new employee at hiring whether that coverage exists. [^tx-payday-posting] [^tx-wc-notice]

| Requirement | What the law requires | When or where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paydays, Tex. Lab. Code § 61.012 | Notices indicating the paydays the employer has designated, at least monthly for overtime-exempt employees and at least twice a month for others | Posted in conspicuous places in the workplace [^tx-payday-posting] [^tx-payday-frequency-notices] |
| Workers' compensation, Tex. Lab. Code § 406.005 | Notice of whether the employer has workers' compensation insurance coverage, revised when the information changes | Told to each new employee at hiring, posted at conspicuous locations, and given to each employee within 15 days after coverage, or its termination or cancellation, takes effect [^tx-wc-notice] |
| Unemployment benefits, Tex. Lab. Code § 208.001(b); 40 Tex. Admin. Code § 815.1(14) | The Texas Workforce Commission's printed notice of general information about filing an unemployment claim | Displayed in a manner reasonably calculated to be encountered by all employees, and given individually to an employee at separation [^tx-ui-notice] [^tx-ui-separation-notice] |
| Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-10, for employers in an industry affecting commerce with fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year | A posted notice prepared or approved by the EEOC | Posted where notices are customarily posted [^usc-2000e-10-posting-txh] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-txh] |

The Payday Law reaches a private or religious school: it defines an employer as a person who employs one or more employees, [^tx-payday-employer-defined] and it excludes only the United States, the state and the state's political subdivisions, which leaves a public school district outside it. [^tx-payday-government-excluded] Workers' compensation coverage is elective for a private employer, but the notice duty in section 406.005 applies whether or not the employer has coverage. [^tx-wc-elective] [^tx-wc-notice] The unemployment notice runs to employers under the Texas Unemployment Compensation Act, which excludes from its definition of employment service for a church, a convention or association of churches, or an organization operated primarily for religious purposes and operated, supervised, controlled or principally supported by a church, so a church-operated school may employ no one in covered employment. [^tx-ui-religious-service] The Commission's rule lets the separation notice go by paper, mail, email, text or other means reasonably calculated to reach the individual. [^tx-ui-separation-notice] Each entry other than the separation notice is a workplace posting, which a handbook can reproduce but does not replace.

## Does a Texas school's anti-harassment policy and complaint process affect its liability when an employee is sexually harassed? {#anti-harassment-policy}

**Short answer.** A Texas employer with one or more employees commits an unlawful employment practice when sexual harassment of an employee occurs that it or its agents or supervisors knew or should have known about and it fails to take immediate and appropriate corrective action. [^tx-sb45-employer] [^tx-sb45-unlawful-practice]

That rule, added by Senate Bill 45 effective September 1, 2021, reaches an employer of any size, while the Act's general definition reaches a private employer only at fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^tx-sb45-unlawful-practice] [^tx-tchra-employer-fifteen] The 2021 definition also covers a person who acts directly in the interests of an employer in relation to an employee. [^tx-sb45-employer] The Act's religious-organization provision permits a religious educational institution to limit employment or give a preference to members of the same religion and removes religious work from subchapter B; by its terms it does not address the sexual harassment subchapter. [^tx-tchra-religious-org] A complaint alleging sexual harassment must be filed within 300 days after the harassment occurred, rather than the 180 days that applies to other complaints. [^tx-tchra-harassment-300-days] Coverage of small and religious schools under the 2021 statute is addressed in the [guide on discrimination law and religious employers](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/texas-religious-employers).

The statute measures the employer by what it and its agents or supervisors knew or should have known and by whether its corrective action was immediate and appropriate, so the reporting channels and response steps a handbook sets out bear directly on both elements. [^tx-sb45-unlawful-practice] In *Fossil Group, Inc. v. Harris*, the Texas Supreme Court noted that sections 21.141 and 21.142 do not apply to claims based on conduct before September 1, 2021, [^tx-fossil-sb45-prospective] applied the earlier negligence standard of actual or constructive knowledge and failure to take prompt remedial action, [^tx-fossil-negligence-standard] and expressly left open whether its understanding of the term *supervisor* carries over to the 2021 sections. [^tx-fossil-supervisor-reserved]

Under Title VII, which reaches employers in an industry affecting commerce with fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks, *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton* allows an employer facing a supervisor-harassment claim with no tangible employment action to defend by showing it exercised reasonable care to prevent and promptly correct harassment and that the employee unreasonably failed to use the preventive or corrective opportunities provided. [^faragher-defense-elements-txh] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-harassment-txh] *Faragher* treats a stated anti-harassment policy with a complaint procedure as relevant to the first element, though not required in every case as a matter of law, [^faragher-policy-first-element-txh] and makes no defense available when the harassment culminates in a tangible employment action such as discharge or demotion. [^faragher-tangible-action-txh] Before the 2021 statute, the Texas Supreme Court described the same two-part defense for a sexual harassment claim under the Act in *Waffle House, Inc. v. Williams*. [^tx-waffle-house-defense]

## Can a Texas school's employment agreement or handbook require an employee to keep a sexual harassment claim confidential or to arbitrate it? {#confidentiality-and-arbitration-of-harassment-claims}

**Short answer.** Under the federal Speak Out Act, a nondisclosure or nondisparagement clause agreed to before a sexual assault or sexual harassment dispute arises is not judicially enforceable where the conduct is alleged to violate federal, tribal or state law. [^speak-out-act-enforceability-txh]

A sexual harassment dispute is one relating to conduct alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable federal, tribal or state law, and a nondisparagement clause is one requiring a party not to make a negative statement about another party relating to the contract, agreement, claim or case. [^speak-out-act-definitions-txh] The Act leaves an employer and employee free to protect trade secrets and proprietary information, and leaves in place state laws at least as protective of the right to speak. [^speak-out-act-enforceability-txh] Because the limit applies to clauses agreed to before the dispute arises, a confidentiality clause in a handbook acknowledgment, offer letter or employment agreement signed in advance is within it, while a clause negotiated after the dispute arises is outside its terms. [^speak-out-act-enforceability-txh]

The Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act separately makes a predispute arbitration agreement or joint-action waiver unenforceable, at the election of the person alleging the conduct, for a case filed under federal, tribal or state law that relates to a sexual harassment or sexual assault dispute. [^efaa-no-enforceability-txh] Whether that Act applies, and whether the agreement is enforceable, is decided by a court under federal law rather than by an arbitrator, even if the agreement delegates those questions to the arbitrator. [^efaa-no-enforceability-txh]

## Can a Texas private school require employee drug tests and discipline an employee for marijuana or low-THC cannabis use? {#drug-testing-and-marijuana}

**Short answer.** A Texas court of appeals upheld a private employer's random drug-testing program for at-will employees in *Jennings v. Minco Technology Labs, Inc.*, and Labor Code § 21.120 permits a policy against employing current users of Schedule I or II controlled substances, subject to listed exceptions. [^tx-jennings-consent] [^tx-tchra-controlled-substance]

Section 21.120 does not reach use or possession of a drug taken under the supervision of a licensed health care professional, or any other use or possession authorized by the federal Controlled Substances Act or any other federal or state law, and it does not shelter a policy adopted or applied with intent to discriminate because of race, color, sex, national origin, religion, age or disability. [^tx-tchra-controlled-substance] In *Jennings*, the court held that an at-will employee could consent to a future urine test or reject continued employment on the modified terms, but could not dictate the terms of her employment. [^tx-jennings-consent] Section 21.120 is a defense under the Texas Commission on Human Rights Act, whose general definition of employer requires, for a private employer, fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks. [^tx-tchra-employer-fifteen-drug]

The Texas Compassionate-Use Act, Health and Safety Code chapter 487 as amended by House Bill 46 in 2025, licenses dispensing organizations to dispense low-THC cannabis to patients for whom it is prescribed under Occupations Code chapter 169. [^tx-compassionate-use-definitions] In a search of Texas state and federal opinions on CourtListener for this review, we found no decision on whether a prescribed patient's use is "authorized by the Controlled Substances Act or any other federal or state law"[^tx-tchra-controlled-substance] within section 21.120's exception, so a policy that treats a prescribed low-THC cannabis patient the same as an illegal user rests on an unresolved reading. [^tx-tchra-controlled-substance]

Federal scheduling has also changed. Effective April 28, 2026, a Justice Department final order placed in Schedule III FDA-approved drug products containing marijuana-derived THC and marijuana subject to a state medical marijuana license. [^dea-schedule-iii-marijuana-2026-txh] Section 21.120 shelters only a policy aimed at current users of Schedule I and II substances, so it no longer shelters a policy as applied to marijuana that the order moved to Schedule III; whether low-THC cannabis dispensed under a Texas license falls within the order depends on its federal classification, which this guide does not resolve. [^tx-tchra-controlled-substance] [^dea-schedule-iii-marijuana-2026-txh]

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, which applies to employers with fifteen or more employees, an employee or applicant currently engaging in the illegal use of drugs is not a qualified individual with a disability when the employer acts on the basis of that use. [^ada-employer-fifteen-txh] [^ada-illegal-drug-use-txh] That exclusion does not reach an individual who has completed, or is participating in, a supervised rehabilitation program and no longer uses illegal drugs, or who is erroneously regarded as using them, though the employer may use reasonable policies, including drug testing, to ensure that a rehabilitated individual is no longer using. [^ada-rehabilitated-safe-harbor-txh] The same section lets the employer prohibit illegal drug use and alcohol use at the workplace and hold a drug-using employee to the same performance standards as others, [^ada-workplace-prohibition-txh] and it provides that a test for illegal drug use is not a medical examination. [^ada-drug-test-not-medical-txh]

## How often must a Texas private school pay employees, and when is final pay due after a resignation or discharge? {#paydays-and-final-pay}

**Short answer.** Under the Texas Payday Law, a private employer must pay overtime-exempt employees at least once a month and other employees at least twice a month, and it must pay a discharged employee in full by the sixth day after discharge. [^tx-paydays-frequency] [^tx-final-pay]

An employee who leaves other than by discharge must be paid in full by the next regularly scheduled payday. [^tx-final-pay] The employer may withhold or divert part of an employee's wages only under a court order, when state or federal law authorizes it, or with the employee's written authorization for a lawful purpose. [^tx-payday-wage-deductions] Vacation, holiday, sick leave, parental leave and severance pay count as wages only when owed under a written agreement or a written policy of the employer, so the handbook's leave and payout language determines whether unused leave is owed at separation. [^tx-wages-defined] Bonuses are due according to the terms of the agreement between the employee and employer or an applicable collective bargaining agreement and must be paid on the timetable that applies to other wages. [^tx-payday-bonuses-due]

The Payday Law reaches a private or religious school because it excludes only the United States, the state and the state's political subdivisions, so a public school district is outside it. [^tx-payday-government-excluded-pay] An employee who is not paid as the chapter requires may file a wage claim with the Texas Workforce Commission within 180 days after the wages became due, and that deadline is jurisdictional. [^tx-wage-claim-deadline]

## Can a Texas private school prohibit employees from keeping a handgun in their locked cars in the school parking lot? {#firearms-in-employee-vehicles}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school may bar employees from storing firearms in their locked cars in its parking areas, because the Labor Code's parking-lot protection for licensed and lawful gun owners expressly does not apply to a private school as defined by Education Code § 22.081. [^tx-parking-lot-rule] [^tx-parking-lot-exceptions]

The general rule bars a public or private employer from prohibiting an employee who holds a license to carry a handgun, otherwise lawfully possesses a firearm, or lawfully possesses ammunition from transporting or storing it in a locked, privately owned vehicle in a parking area the employer provides for employees. [^tx-parking-lot-rule] The exceptions remove from that rule a school district, an open-enrollment charter school and a private school as defined by section 22.081 of the Education Code. [^tx-parking-lot-exceptions] Section 22.081 defines a private school as one that offers instruction in one or more grades from prekindergarten through grade 12 and is not operated by a governmental entity, which covers an independent or religious K–12 school. [^tx-private-school-defined] Any employer may also prohibit a licensed or lawful gun owner from possessing a firearm on the premises of its business, with *premises* taking the meaning given in Penal Code section 46.03. [^tx-parking-lot-premises]

Separately, the Penal Code makes it an offense to intentionally, knowingly or recklessly possess a firearm on the premises of a school, public or private, on grounds or buildings the school owns and controls while a school-sponsored activity is being conducted there, or in a school passenger vehicle, unless pursuant to written regulations or written authorization of the school. [^tx-penal-weapons-school] For that offense, *school* means an accredited primary or secondary school, and *premises* means a building or portion of a building and excludes parking lots and parking garages. [^tx-penal-weapons-definitions] For an accredited school, written regulations or written authorization are the school's own route to permitting an employee to carry a firearm inside its buildings. [^tx-penal-weapons-school] Other firearm rules on school grounds, including armed-staff programs, are addressed in the [school safety guide](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/texas-school-safety).

## Does Texas law protect a private school employee who is fired for reporting wrongdoing or refusing to break the law? {#retaliation-for-reporting}

**Short answer.** The Texas Whistleblower Act protects only public employees of state and local governmental entities, so a private school employee's protection comes from narrower rules, such as the *Sabine Pilot* exception for an employee discharged solely for refusing to perform an illegal act. [^tx-whistleblower-public-only] [^tx-sabine-pilot-exception]

The Whistleblower Act bars a state or local governmental entity from retaliating against a public employee who in good faith reports a violation of law to an appropriate law enforcement authority, and it lists a public school district among local governmental entities. [^tx-whistleblower-public-only] In *Sabine Pilot Service, Inc. v. Hauck*, the Texas Supreme Court recognized a very narrow exception to employment at will, grounded in laws that carry criminal penalties, covering only discharge for the sole reason that the employee refused to perform an illegal act, and placed on the employee the burden of proving that the refusal was the only reason. [^tx-sabine-pilot-exception]

For abuse reporting, Family Code § 261.110 bars an employer from suspending, terminating, discriminating against or taking other adverse employment action against a professional who in good faith reports child abuse or neglect to a supervisor, a facility administrator, a state regulatory agency or law enforcement, or who initiates or cooperates with a governmental investigation. [^tx-abuse-report-retaliation] The statute reaches private employers, since it allows exemplary damages "if the employer is a private employer"[^tx-abuse-report-retaliation]. [^tx-abuse-report-retaliation] Its protection runs only to a *professional*, defined as an individual licensed or certified by the state, or an employee of a facility licensed, certified or operated by the state, who has direct contact with children in the normal course of duties, a term that expressly includes teachers. [^tx-professional-defined] The protection does not extend to a person who reports, or cooperates in an investigation of, the person's own abuse or neglect of a child. [^tx-abuse-report-own-conduct] An employee who opposes a discriminatory practice or files, testifies or participates in a proceeding under the Texas Commission on Human Rights Act is protected from retaliation by an employer within that Act. [^tx-tchra-retaliation]

## Can a Texas school's employee handbook create a binding contract that limits at-will employment or commits employees to arbitration? {#handbook-contract-and-at-will}

**Short answer.** A Texas employee handbook that disclaims contractual effect and preserves at-will employment does not restrict the employer's right to discharge, as the Texas Supreme Court held in *Federal Express Corp. v. Dutschmann*. [^tx-dutschmann-disclaimer]

In *Dutschmann*, the handbook stated that employment was at will, that it was not a contract and that it could be changed at any time, and the employee signed an acknowledgment saying the same. [^tx-dutschmann-facts] Oral assurances do not change that result without more: in *Montgomery County Hospital District v. Brown*, the court held that the employer must unequivocally indicate a definite intent to be bound not to terminate except under clearly specified circumstances, and that general comments about job security, or statements that an employee will be discharged only for good cause when there is no agreement on what that term encompasses, do not meet that standard. [^tx-brown-definite-intent]

A handbook can still bind both sides when it is used to add a program by notice. In *In re Halliburton Co.*, the court held that an employer that unequivocally notifies an at-will employee of a change accepted by continued work may add a binding arbitration program, [^tx-halliburton-notice-acceptance] [^tx-halliburton-holding] and held the program not illusory because termination took effect only ten days after reasonable notice and did not reach disputes that arose before it, so the employer could not avoid its promise to arbitrate by amending or terminating the program. [^tx-halliburton-not-illusory] For a claim relating to a sexual harassment or sexual assault dispute, federal law lets the person alleging the conduct elect not to be bound by any predispute arbitration agreement. [^efaa-handbook-arbitration-txh]

## Do NLRB standards for handbook work rules apply to a nonprofit independent school in Texas? {#nlrb-handbook-rules}

**Short answer.** The National Labor Relations Board applies a $1 million annual minimum to private and nonprofit schools, and where it has jurisdiction its 2023 *Stericycle* standard presumes unlawful a work rule that reasonably tends to chill employees' exercise of labor-law rights. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools-txh] [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-txh]

The Board states that it will not assert jurisdiction over employees of a religious organization who are involved in effectuating its religious purpose, such as teachers in church-operated schools. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-religious-txh] Under *Stericycle*, an employer can rebut the presumption by proving that the rule advances a legitimate and substantial business interest that a more narrowly tailored rule could not advance. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-txh] In *NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago*, the Court declined to construe the Act to reach teachers in church-operated schools absent a clear expression of congressional intent, because doing so would raise questions under the Religion Clauses. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools-txh]

A secular nonprofit school that meets the $1 million minimum is within the Board's jurisdiction, and its handbook rules on confidentiality, social media and workplace conduct, as applied to employees the Act covers, are measured against that standard. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools-txh] [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-txh] *Catholic Bishop* addressed teachers in church-operated schools, so it does not by itself remove a religious school's non-teaching staff from the Act. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools-txh] As of the November 23, 2026 review, the most recent Board record cited here is its August 2, 2023 announcement of the *Stericycle* standard. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-txh]

Enforcement in Texas also turns on a structural challenge: in *Space Exploration Technologies Corp. v. NLRB*, the Fifth Circuit, which hears appeals from Texas federal courts, affirmed preliminary injunctions halting Board proceedings against three employers after concluding that they were likely to succeed in challenging the Board Members' removal protections. [^spacex-removal-likely-success] [^spacex-affirmed-injunctions] As of the November 23, 2026 review, the most recent Fifth Circuit record cited here on that question is the August 19, 2025 decision, which did not address the substance of any work rule. [^spacex-removal-likely-success]

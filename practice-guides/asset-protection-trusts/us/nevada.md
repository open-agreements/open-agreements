# Nevada Asset Protection Trusts: Self-Settled Spendthrift Trusts Under NRS Chapter 166[^about]

Nevada self-settled spendthrift trusts: trustee and drafting rules, creditor deadlines, support claims, spouses, LLC holdings and out-of-state courts.

## Can a person create a Nevada trust that protects his or her own assets from creditors? {#self-settled-trust-requirements}

**Short answer.** Nevada law lets a person (the settlor) create a spendthrift trust that names the settlor as a beneficiary, subject to three conditions in NRS 166.040(1)(b). [^nrs-166-040-settlor-beneficiary-conditions] The trust document must be irrevocable, must not require that any income or principal be distributed to the settlor, and must not have been intended to hinder, delay or defraud known creditors. [^nrs-166-040-settlor-beneficiary-conditions] NRS 166.040(2) qualifies the distribution condition: a writing still meets it if the settlor holds certain listed rights, including a veto over distributions, a limited power of appointment, an interest in a charitable remainder trust, an annual payout capped at trust income under 26 U.S.C. § 643(b) or a retirement plan's required minimum distribution, a qualified annuity or unitrust interest in a grantor retained trust, use of a residence held in a qualified personal residence trust, distributions only in another person's discretion, or use of trust property. [^nrs-166-040-permitted-settlor-interests]
When the settlor is a beneficiary, at least one trustee must be an individual who resides and is domiciled in Nevada, a trust company with an office in Nevada, or a bank with an office in Nevada that exercises trust powers. [^nrs-166-015-nevada-trustee-required]
The settlor may keep significant control. NRS 166.040(3) permits the settlor to remove and replace a trustee, direct trust investments and serve as a cotrustee, but not to make distributions to himself or herself without another person's consent. [^nrs-166-040-settlor-retained-powers] The settlor holds only the powers the trust instrument confers, and a side agreement or understanding with the trustee that grants the settlor more is void. [^nrs-166-045-side-agreements-void]
A lapse in administration after the trust is validly created does not undo it. In *Klabacka v. Nelson*, the Nevada Supreme Court held that breaching the formalities of a validly created self-settled spendthrift trust creates liability for the trustee rather than invalidating the trust. [^klabacka-formalities-breach-trustee-liability]

## Who counts as the settlor when someone else adds property to a Nevada spendthrift trust? {#later-contributors-settlor}

**Short answer.** Under NRS 166.018(2), a person who contributes assets to a Nevada spendthrift trust is a settlor only as to the assets that person contributed, and not to the extent the person received consideration for them. [^nrs-166-018-settlor-includes-contributors] The person who creates the trust is also a settlor, however the trust instrument describes that person. [^nrs-166-018-settlor-includes-contributors]
Read together with NRS 166.040(1), which permits a spendthrift trust for *a person other than the settlor*, this per-asset definition suggests that one trust can hold self-settled property and third-party property side by side, with each contribution tested against its own contributor. [^nrs-166-040-person-other-than-settlor] [^nrs-166-018-settlor-includes-contributors]
A separate rule addresses withdrawal rights. For purposes of NRS 163.5559, which governs claims of a settlor's creditors, a beneficiary is not treated as a settlor merely because a right to withdraw trust property lapses, is waived or is released, if the amount withdrawable in a calendar year does not exceed the greatest of the federal amounts in 26 U.S.C. § 2041(b)(2), § 2503(b) or § 2514(e). [^nrs-163-5559-lapsed-withdrawal-not-settlor]
The statute does not define *consideration received*, so how a contribution made in exchange for value, such as a reciprocal arrangement between family members, is allocated is not resolved by that definition, and no Nevada decision resolving that allocation was found in our review; the conservative course is to record each contribution, its contributor and any value given for it, and to treat a contribution made for value as self-settled by the person who gave that value. [^nrs-166-018-settlor-includes-contributors] The source note dates the definition to the 2019 legislative session, and the captured text does not say whether it applies to contributions made before then; the conservative course is not to rely on per-contributor treatment for earlier contributions. [^nrs-166-018-settlor-includes-contributors]

## How long does a creditor have to challenge a transfer into a Nevada asset protection trust? {#creditor-challenge-deadline}

**Short answer.** A creditor whose claim existed when property was transferred into a Nevada spendthrift trust must sue within two years after the transfer or six months after it discovered or reasonably should have discovered the transfer, whichever is later, while a later creditor has two years. [^nrs-166-170-two-year-six-month-periods] [^nrs-166-170-public-record-discovery]
A person is treated as discovering a transfer when a public record of it is made, including a recorded deed or a filed financing statement. [^nrs-166-170-public-record-discovery]
A timely suit is not enough by itself. The creditor must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the transfer was a fraudulent transfer under NRS chapter 112, or that it violated a legal obligation owed to that creditor under a contract or a valid court order enforceable by that creditor. [^nrs-166-170-clear-convincing-proof] Proof is creditor-specific and transfer-specific: one creditor's proof does not count for another creditor or invalidate another transfer. [^nrs-166-170-clear-convincing-proof]
Each transfer runs on its own clock. A later transfer is disregarded in deciding whether an action about an earlier transfer is timely, and any distribution is deemed to come from the most recent transfer. [^nrs-166-170-each-transfer-own-clock] Once a creditor's action about a transfer would be barred, no action of any kind, including an action to enforce a judgment, may be brought against the trustee. [^nrs-166-170-trustee-suit-barred]
Chapter 112's own limitations periods, generally four years or one year after discovery, do not apply to transfers into a chapter 166 trust. [^nrs-112-230-chapter-166-carve-out] The current Nevada Revised Statutes still title chapter 112 the Uniform Fraudulent Transfer Act. [^nrs-112-140-uniform-fraudulent-transfer-act]
A transfer of an asset that produces no public record, such as cash, securities or an LLC interest, has no statutory deemed-discovery date, so an existing creditor's six-month period runs only from when that creditor actually discovered or reasonably should have discovered the transfer. [^nrs-166-170-two-year-six-month-periods] [^nrs-166-170-public-record-discovery] No Nevada appellate decision construing that discovery standard was found in our review.
The timing of a claim that has not yet been reduced to judgment is less settled. Chapter 166 borrows chapter 112's definition of creditor, a person who has a claim, and chapter 112 defines a claim to include contingent, unliquidated and disputed rights. [^nrs-166-170-creditor-borrowed-definition] [^nrs-112-150-claim-and-creditor-defined] On that text, a person injured before a transfer is probably an existing creditor under NRS 166.170(1)(a) even before any judgment, but no Nevada decision applying the definition to this question was found in our review. [^nrs-112-150-claim-and-creditor-defined]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Funding records that omit evidence of when existing creditors learned of an unrecorded transfer can leave the discovery deadline unresolved and prevent a reliable conclusion that a later challenge is barred. [^nrs-166-170-two-year-six-month-periods] [^nrs-166-170-public-record-discovery]

## Can a former spouse or child collect support from a Nevada asset protection trust? {#support-claims-and-exception-creditors}

**Short answer.** Nevada recognizes no general child-support or spousal-support exception to the protection of a self-settled spendthrift trust, because the Nevada Supreme Court expressly rejected that exception in *Klabacka v. Nelson*, a case involving support obligations not known when the trust was created. [^klabacka-unknown-support-obligations-protected]
Chapter 166 contains no provision exempting support, tort or government claims from spendthrift protection. NRS 166.120(2) instead bars trustee payments to anyone other than the beneficiary under legal process, whether the claim arises from contract, tort or duty. [^nrs-166-120-payments-despite-legal-process]
"We further conclude the child- and spousal-support exception articulated in section 59 of the Third Restatement of Trusts is inconsistent with Nevada's statutory framework and the legislative history of NRS Chapter 166, and we expressly reject that exception here."[^klabacka-unknown-support-obligations-protected]
The protection has four limits.
**Transfer-specific challenges.** Under NRS 166.170(3), a creditor may proceed against a transfer into the trust if it proves by clear and convincing evidence that the transfer was a fraudulent transfer under NRS chapter 112, or that it violated a legal obligation owed to that creditor under a contract or a valid court order enforceable by that creditor. [^nrs-166-170-court-order-creditor-route] In *Klabacka*, the divorce decree did not qualify because it required the trustees to violate NRS 166.120, and the court noted that the record did not show a fraudulent transfer under NRS 166.170(3). [^klabacka-divorce-decree-not-enforceable-order]
**Obligations known at creation.** The *Klabacka* holding covers obligations not known when the trust was created, and the court noted that the legislative history treats child support known at creation as a debt that would not be free from attachment. [^klabacka-known-debts-legislative-history] NRS 166.040(1)(b) separately conditions a self-settled trust on its not having been intended to hinder, delay or defraud known creditors. [^nrs-166-040-known-creditors-condition] Whether a support order already in place when the trust is funded can reach the trust, through that condition or through the court-order route above, is not settled by any decision found in our review.
**Community property.** The spendthrift restraints do not protect the nonbeneficiary spouse's community property share of property inside the trust. [^klabacka-nonbeneficiary-spouse-community-share]
**Medicaid recovery.** Nevada's statute on liability of nonprobate transferees states that nothing in it exempts property from a state statute authorizing recovery of Medicaid benefits paid. [^nrs-111-779-medicaid-recovery-preserved] Whether those recovery statutes reach a chapter 166 trust after the settlor dies was outside the authorities in our review.

## Does it matter whether married spouses in Nevada use one joint asset protection trust or separate trusts? {#married-couples-joint-or-separate-trusts}

**Short answer.** When Nevada spouses choose between one joint self-settled trust and separate trusts, Nevada's rule letting the trust instrument preserve the community or separate character of transferred property applies only to an irrevocable trust benefiting both spouses. [^nrs-123-125-joint-trust-character] In either structure, a divorce court must trace trust assets to find any community property. [^klabacka-trace-assets-divide-community] The court must divide that community property equally unless it states in writing a compelling reason for an unequal division. [^nrs-125-150-equal-division-trust-property]
**One joint trust.** NRS 123.125(1) applies when both spouses are distribution beneficiaries. The trust instrument may provide that transferred property remains community or separate property during the marriage, and property distributed from such a trust keeps that character. [^nrs-123-125-joint-trust-character] A party claiming that property transferred into a trust changed from community to separate, or the reverse, must prove the change by clear and convincing evidence. [^nrs-123-125-joint-trust-character] In a divorce, the court must divide community property equally, including community property transferred into such a trust over which the court has jurisdiction, unless it states in writing a compelling reason for an unequal division. [^nrs-125-150-equal-division-trust-property]
**Separate trusts.** In *Klabacka v. Nelson*, each spouse had a self-settled spendthrift trust, and the court held the spouses' written agreements valid and unambiguous. [^klabacka-respective-trusts-written-agreements] The court held that their separate property agreement was a valid transmutation agreement that converted community property into separate property. [^klabacka-separate-property-agreement-transmutation] That result rests on the Nevada rule that property acquired during marriage is community property unless a written agreement between the spouses provides otherwise. [^nrs-123-220-written-agreement-exception] Funding either structure with community property also engages the control rules: neither spouse may give away community property without the other's consent, and a deed of community real property requires both spouses to join and acknowledge it. [^nrs-123-230-community-gift-consent-joinder]
A spouse is a beneficiary only if named or clearly referred to as one in the trust writing. [^nrs-166-080-spouse-named-beneficiary]
The tracing mandate had practical consequences in the same divorce. In *Nelson v. Eighth Judicial District Court*, decided April 1, 2021, the Nevada Supreme Court said *Klabacka* had not determined that all trust assets were separate property and directed an injunction over trust property subject to a claim of community interest. [^nelson-2021-tracing-mandate-community-claims]
One statutory question is open. NRS 123.125(1) applies by its terms only when both spouses are distribution beneficiaries, and subsection 3 says the section does not affect the character of property transferred into a trust in any other manner. [^nrs-123-125-joint-trust-character] Read together, a trust benefiting only one spouse appears to fall outside the character-preservation rule, leaving character to other rules such as a written agreement; whether subsection 2's clear-and-convincing standard still applies to such a trust is not resolved by the text or by any decision found in our review.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Funding records that do not identify the source of each contribution, separate or community, make it difficult to identify the community share inside a self-settled trust that remains exposed to division in a divorce. [^nrs-123-220-written-agreement-exception] [^klabacka-community-share-tracing-practice]

## If one spouse's will leaves property to the other spouse's Nevada asset protection trust, is that property self-settled? {#spouse-will-funds-other-spouses-trust}

**Short answer.** Nevada's settlor definition points toward treating property that a deceased spouse's will adds to the surviving spouse's spendthrift trust as the deceased spouse's contribution, not as self-settled by the survivor, though no Nevada decision applying it was found in our review. [^nrs-166-018-will-contributor-settlor] [^nrs-166-040-trust-by-will-for-another]
NRS 166.018(2) makes any person who contributes assets a settlor as to those assets, and NRS 166.040(1) allows a spendthrift trust to be created by will for a person other than the settlor. [^nrs-166-018-will-contributor-settlor] [^nrs-166-040-trust-by-will-for-another] Read together, the surviving spouse would be a beneficiary of the deceased spouse's contribution but not its settlor, which makes that portion a third-party spendthrift interest rather than a self-settled one.
Community property limits what the will can contribute. Neither spouse may leave more than one-half of the community property by will, and at death the survivor's one-half interest becomes the survivor's separate property, while only the decedent's half is subject to estate administration. [^nrs-123-230-devise-limit-one-half] [^nrs-123-250-survivor-half-separate-property] Property the survivor adds to the trust, including the survivor's half of former community property, is the survivor's own contribution and is self-settled as to the survivor. [^nrs-166-018-will-contributor-settlor]
The contributor is a settlor except to the extent of consideration received, and the statute does not say who is treated as the settlor of that portion. [^nrs-166-018-will-contributor-settlor] No Nevada decision found in our review addresses whether a court would look through to the survivor when the deceased spouse's gift was given in exchange for value from the survivor, such as under reciprocal estate plans; the conservative course is to treat any portion given for value as self-settled by the survivor.
Because the survivor created the trust and is a beneficiary, the trust as a whole still needs a qualifying Nevada trustee. [^nrs-166-015-trustee-surviving-settlor] The irrevocability, no-required-distribution and known-creditor conditions in NRS 166.040(1)(b) are framed around the writing, not each asset, so the text leaves undecided how they apply to the deceased spouse's contribution; the conservative course is to draft the survivor's trust to meet every NRS 166.040(1)(b) condition for all of its property. [^nrs-166-040-trust-by-will-for-another]
How claims against the deceased spouse's estate reach property passing under the will into the trust was outside the authorities in our review; the conservative course is to treat that property as exposed to those claims until the estate's creditors have been paid.

## Does holding trust assets or out-of-state land through an LLC change what a creditor can reach? {#llc-held-assets-and-land}

**Short answer.** Under NRS 86.401, a judgment creditor of a member of a Nevada limited-liability company may obtain only a charging order against the member's interest, even in a single-member company, and may not foreclose on that interest. [^nrs-86-401-charging-order-exclusive-remedy]
The Nevada Supreme Court held in *Weddell v. H2O, Inc.* that a charging-order creditor receives only a share of the economic interests, such as profits and distributions. [^weddell-creditor-economic-interest-only]
That charging-order limit governs collection against a member's interest. A creditor attacking the transfer into the trust uses a different set of remedies. Under NRS chapter 112, a creditor may obtain avoidance of the transfer, attachment of the asset transferred, an injunction, a receiver or other relief, and a judgment creditor may, if the court orders, levy execution on the asset transferred or its proceeds. [^nrs-112-210-avoidance-remedies] Chapter 112 also allows a judgment for the value of the asset transferred, or the amount needed to satisfy the claim if less, against the first transferee or the person for whose benefit the transfer was made. [^nrs-112-220-value-judgment-transferee] How those avoidance remedies interact with the charging-order limit when a Nevada self-settled trust holds an LLC interest is not resolved by the authorities reviewed for this guide, and no Nevada decision addressing it was found in our review.
Two further points bear on that protection.
**Alter ego.** In a 2019 order answering certified questions, the Nevada Supreme Court held that a judgment creditor may use alter ego to make a third party liable on a judgment and that the doctrine applies to LLCs. [^magliarditi-alter-ego-applies-llcs] The federal court had also asked whether alter ego applies to trusts and to spendthrift trusts. [^magliarditi-certified-trust-questions] The court declined to answer those two questions because the record did not show the nature of the trusts. [^magliarditi-trust-questions-left-open] Separately, a settlor of an irrevocable trust is not the trustee's alter ego absent clear and convincing evidence. [^nrs-163-418-settlor-alter-ego-clear-convincing]
**Land versus an LLC interest.** Under Nevada's LLC statute, a member's interest is personal property, and real property the company owns is held and conveyed in the company's name. [^nrs-86-351-member-interest-personal-property] [^nrs-86-311-company-holds-title] Apart from a separate test based on Nevada administration, chapter 166's own applicability rule treats the two differently: it reaches land by its location in Nevada, but reaches personal property either by its location in Nevada or by the trust creator's declared Nevada domicile. [^nrs-166-015-land-versus-personal-property]
Land held directly in the trust may be judged under the law of the state where it sits. In *United States v. Huckaby*, a federal court in California addressed land the settlors had transferred directly into their trust. [^huckaby-land-transferred-directly] It treated whether a creditor could reach that land as a question for the law of the situs under Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws section 280, as the court quoted it, and applied California law because the land was in California. [^huckaby-situs-law-governs-land-in-trust] [^huckaby-land-in-california-law-applied]
Read together, these sources suggest that a trust holding a Nevada LLC interest instead of out-of-state land holds personal property, which chapter 166 reaches through the creator's declared Nevada domicile as well as through Nevada administration, and to which the land rule the *Huckaby* court quoted does not by its terms speak. *Huckaby* did not involve an LLC, and no decision applying that distinction to a Nevada trust was found in our review. [^huckaby-land-transferred-directly] NRS 86.401 is part of Nevada's LLC statute, and this guide's sources do not address charging orders against an LLC formed under another state's law. [^nrs-86-401-charging-order-exclusive-remedy]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> An operating agreement or planning memorandum that describes the NRS 86.401 charging-order limit as shielding the company's own assets can misstate their exposure once a court finds the company to be the debtor's alter ego. [^nrs-86-401-charging-order-exclusive-remedy] [^magliarditi-alter-ego-applies-llcs]

## Will courts outside Nevada respect a Nevada asset protection trust? {#courts-outside-nevada}

**Short answer.** Courts outside Nevada have applied their own law instead of a trust's Nevada designation in two kinds of disputes found in our review, a creditor's claim against land held in the trust and divorce. [^huckaby-california-law-liens-enforceable] [^dahl-marital-division-utah-law]
Nevada's statute is written for Nevada courts. Chapter 166 governs the construction, operation and enforcement of spendthrift trusts *in this State* when the trust has a listed Nevada connection, such as Nevada property, a declared Nevada domicile, or a qualified trustee performing administration in Nevada. [^nrs-166-015-governs-in-this-state] NRS 166.120(2) requires an action to reach a beneficiary's interest to be brought in a specified Nevada proceeding and gives the court exclusive jurisdiction. [^nrs-166-120-nevada-court-exclusive-jurisdiction] Whether a court in another state, or a federal court, must defer to that exclusive-jurisdiction provision was not decided by any authority found in our review.
**Land in another state: *United States v. Huckaby*.** The trust's settlors were also its trustees and its sole lifetime beneficiaries. [^huckaby-settlors-trustees-beneficiaries] The defendants argued that Nevada law governed because the trust was designated a Nevada spendthrift trust by its terms. [^huckaby-nevada-designation-argued] The federal court in California agreed that the trust is construed under Nevada law, citing Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws section 277 as it quoted it, but held that whether a creditor can reach the land is a separate question governed by the law of the situs under section 280, again as the court quoted it. [^huckaby-nevada-construction-agreed] [^huckaby-situs-rule-quoted-restatement] Applying California law, it held the judgment liens enforceable against the land to the extent of the debtor's interest and concluded that the trust did not protect the property. [^huckaby-california-law-liens-enforceable] [^huckaby-trust-no-protection-from-lien] It did not reach the government's alternative argument that the land was transferred about a month after an IRS levy was served. [^huckaby-timing-argument-not-reached] The order of March 3, 2026 is a federal trial-court ruling that binds no other court, and it did not consider a trust with an independent Nevada trustee or assets other than land.
**Divorce: Utah decisions.** In *Dahl v. Dahl*, the Utah Supreme Court said Utah generally enforces a trust's choice-of-law clause unless doing so would undermine a strong Utah public policy. [^dahl-utah-policy-overrides-choice-of-law] It declined to apply Nevada law to the trust's revocability because doing so would prevent an equitable division of the marital estate. [^dahl-revocability-nevada-law-not-applied] [^dahl-marital-division-utah-law] In *Hillam v. Hillam*, the Utah Court of Appeals applied Utah law to the enforcement of a trust whose agreement selected the Spendthrift Trust Act of Nevada, except for questions about the trust's creation. [^hillam-nevada-clause-utah-law-enforcement] *Hillam* did not decide the former spouse's creditor-based claim to the trust assets, which it held unpreserved. [^hillam-creditor-argument-unpreserved] Both decisions bind only Utah courts and are nonbinding guidance elsewhere.
**Federal bankruptcy.** The Bankruptcy Code lets a bankruptcy trustee avoid a debtor's transfer to a self-settled trust of which the debtor is a beneficiary if the transfer was made within 10 years before the bankruptcy petition with actual intent to hinder, delay or defraud a creditor. [^usc-548e-ten-year-self-settled-trust] That ten-year period comes from federal law, not chapter 166. Read together, this suggests that a transfer beyond the reach of NRS 166.170's two-year and six-month periods can still be avoided in bankruptcy if the federal elements are proved, though no Nevada decision applying the two provisions together was found in our review. [^usc-548e-ten-year-self-settled-trust] [^nrs-166-170-deadlines-bankruptcy-comparison]

# Wage and Hour Law in Puerto Rico[^about]

How Puerto Rico sets a $10.50 minimum wage, requires overtime after eight hours in a day or 40 in a week, mandates a meal period, and sets final-pay, 15-day pay-frequency, deduction, tip-credit, worker-classification and wage-claim rules.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Puerto Rico's minimum wage is $10.50 an hour, the rate Act 47-2021 set for July 1, 2024 unless the Minimum Wage Evaluation Commission varies it by mandatory decree. [^pr-minimum-wage-ten-fifty] The Department of Labor and Human Resources' 2026 minimum wage assessment confirms that the $10.50 rate has taken effect. [^pr-minimum-wage-2026-assessment] The Puerto Rico rate prevails only while it is higher than the federal minimum wage, [^pr-federal-minimum-wage-applies] which is $7.25 an hour for employees covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act. [^pr-federal-minimum-wage-rate]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The Commission may raise the minimum wage by decree, but it may never set a rate below the federal minimum wage. [^pr-commission-decree-floor] When Puerto Rico's minimum wage applies, federal law and regulations still govern how the minimum wage is paid, what counts as hours worked and which employees and occupations are exempt. [^pr-federal-rules-on-hours-and-exemptions]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Puerto Rico generally requires overtime after eight hours in a calendar day or an employer-noticed alternate 24-hour cycle, and after 40 hours in a workweek, with a ten-hour daily threshold under a written alternate weekly schedule. [^pr-daily-weekly-overtime] [^pr-alternate-weekly-schedule] An employer must pay each overtime hour at no less than one and one-half times the rate agreed for regular hours, and employees hired before the 2017 Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act who were entitled to a higher rate keep it. [^pr-overtime-time-and-a-half] The employer may give an employee written notice, at least five days before it starts, of an alternate 24-hour cycle for counting the eight-hour day, provided there are at least eight hours between consecutive shifts. [^pr-daily-weekly-overtime]

Hours worked during the legally required weekly day of rest, and hours worked while an establishment must by law be closed to the public, are also overtime hours, although Sunday hours are not overtime merely because a law closes the establishment on Sunday. [^pr-overtime-rest-day-closing] An employer and an employee may agree in writing to an alternate weekly schedule of no more than 40 hours, with days of up to ten hours; hours over ten in a day are then paid at time and a half. [^pr-alternate-weekly-schedule]

Except in the situations that Article 6 of the hours law authorizes, the right to overtime pay cannot be waived, and any clause by which an employee agrees to give it up is void. [^pr-overtime-not-waivable] The overtime rules do not apply to administrators, executives and professionals, or to traveling agents, peddlers and outside salespersons, as the Secretary of Labor and Human Resources defines those terms by regulation, nor to union officers or organizers acting in that role. [^pr-overtime-exemptions] Employees who are exempt from overtime under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act are also outside the Puerto Rico overtime rules. [^pr-overtime-flsa-exempt] For an employee covered by the federal Act, federal law separately requires one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. [^pr-federal-overtime]

Act 41-2022, signed in June 2022, would have tightened labor rules that the 2017 labor reform had loosened, [^pr-act-41-signed] but the federal district court overseeing Puerto Rico's debt restructuring nullified it, and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit affirmed that order in August 2023. [^pr-act-41-nullification-affirmed] The overtime and meal-period rules in this guide therefore follow the statutes as amended in 2017.

Federal overtime exemptions and hours-worked rules are discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Puerto Rico generally requires a meal period when an employee works more than six hours in a day. [^pr-meal-period-five-hours] An employer that requires or permits an employee to work more than five consecutive hours without a meal period must pay for the time worked at a premium rate, although the meal period may be skipped when the employee works no more than six hours in the day. [^pr-meal-period-five-hours] An employer that has an employee work during the meal period must pay for that period, or any part of it, at one and one-half times the agreed regular rate, and employees entitled to a higher rate before the 2017 labor reform keep it. [^pr-meal-period-premium] The meal period must begin no earlier than the end of the second consecutive hour of work and no later than the start of the sixth, and an employee who works more than ten hours in a day must get a second meal period unless the day does not exceed twelve hours and the employee took the first meal period. [^pr-meal-period-timing-second]

The meal period ordinarily must last at least one hour. [^pr-meal-period-one-hour] It may generally be shortened to no less than 30 minutes by written agreement between the employer and the employee. [^pr-meal-period-thirty-minutes] For croupiers, nurses, security guards and others the Secretary authorizes, a written agreement may shorten it to 20 minutes without the Secretary's approval. [^pr-meal-period-twenty-minutes]

An employee who returns to work after maternity leave is entitled to one hour within each full-time workday to breastfeed or express milk, which may be split into two 30-minute or three 20-minute periods. [^pr-lactation-period] A part-time employee whose workday exceeds four hours gets 30 minutes for each four consecutive hours of work. [^pr-lactation-part-time] An employer that is a small business under U.S. Small Business Administration standards must provide at least half an hour in each full-time workday. [^pr-lactation-small-business] For an employer covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, short rest periods that are given must be counted as hours worked, [^pr-federal-rest-periods-paid] while a meal period may be unpaid only if the employee is completely relieved from duty. [^pr-federal-meal-relieved]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** When an employee covered by the wage payment law is let go or has to leave work on any day of the week, the employer must pay for the days worked no later than the next official payday. [^pr-pay-interval-final-pay] [^pr-final-pay-covered-employees] That law does not cover executives, administrators and professionals as the Minimum Wage Board defined them. [^pr-final-pay-covered-employees] The rule is the same whether the employee is discharged or quits. The employer must also pay the employee all accrued vacation leave, even if the employee has accrued less than a year. [^pr-vacation-payout-separation] No Puerto Rico statute reviewed for this guide imposes a separate daily penalty for late final pay. Instead, an employee paid less than the law, a collective agreement or an individual employment contract requires may sue for the unpaid wages, vacation or other benefits plus an equal amount, with costs, interest and attorney's fees. [^pr-wage-claim-equal-amount]

Any violation of the wage payment law, including late payment of wages, is also a misdemeanor. [^pr-wage-payment-misdemeanor] Act 47-2021 sets a five-year period, running from the end of employment, for wage claims under that Act or under any contract or law, and wage claims made before its approval keep the period previously in force. [^pr-final-pay-minimum-wage-five-years] The department's compilation of the vacation and sick leave law still shows that law's earlier one-year period. [^pr-wage-claim-one-year] Severance under Puerto Rico's wrongful discharge law is a separate subject and is not covered here.

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Wages of employees covered by the wage payment law must be paid at intervals of no more than 15 days, in U.S. currency, by check or by direct deposit, electronic transfer or payroll card. [^pr-pay-interval-fifteen-days] [^pr-pay-frequency-covered-employees] That law excludes executives, administrators and professionals as the Minimum Wage Board defined them. [^pr-pay-frequency-covered-employees] The employee chooses among the payment methods the employer makes available, and an electronic payment must be effective on the payday itself. [^pr-payment-method-choice] A contract promise to pay wages in any other form is void as to that promise. [^pr-other-forms-void]

The costs of a check or electronic payroll system fall entirely on the employer. [^pr-payroll-costs-employer]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** Every employer must make an itemized pay stub available to each worker within five calendar days after payday. [^pr-pay-stub-contents] The stub must show the employer's name and address, the employee's name and position, the pay date and period covered, total regular and overtime hours, regular and overtime earnings, additions and deductions with the reason for each, and net pay. [^pr-pay-stub-contents] In addition, when wages are paid by direct deposit, electronic transfer or payroll card, the employer must give each employee a receipt showing the wages deposited or transferred after the deductions the law authorizes, on paper or electronically as the employee chooses and at no charge. [^pr-pay-stub-electronic] The employer must also keep payroll records showing the wages earned and the regular and overtime hours worked by each employee. [^pr-payroll-records] Deductions are tightly limited: except in the cases the wage payment law lists, an employer may not deduct or withhold any part of an employee's wages for any reason. [^pr-deductions-listed-only]

The first listed case is a deduction the employee authorizes in writing for dues to a hospital-service association. [^pr-deductions-listed-only] An employer that advances an employee money, up to the employee's weekly wage, may deduct it from the wages for the week of the advance, but no more than the amount advanced. [^pr-advance-deduction]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Unless a special law provides otherwise, Puerto Rico first asks whether a conclusive statutory presumption of independent-contractor status applies, and otherwise uses the common law test. [^pr-contractor-presumption] [^pr-contractor-common-law] The Department of Labor and Human Resources explains that the 2017 labor reform created a presumption that cannot be rebutted, which arises only when four required facts are all present and at least three further factors from a statutory list are also met. [^pr-contractor-presumption] [^pr-contractor-five-factors] The four required facts are that the person has or has applied for an employer identification number, has filed income tax returns claiming to own a business, signed a written contract establishing the relationship, and was contractually required to hold the licenses and permits needed for the business and the services. [^pr-contractor-presumption] The further factors include control over how and when the work is done, freedom to work for others and freedom to hire helpers. [^pr-contractor-five-factors] When the required facts are missing, the presumption does not apply, and status is decided under the common law test, giving weight to what the parties' contract says and to the degree of direct control over how the work is done. [^pr-contractor-common-law]

The hours and overtime law defines an employee as a natural person who works for an employer and receives pay for the services, and excludes independent contractors. [^pr-employee-definition]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Puerto Rico permits a tip credit toward its minimum wage, subject to the conditions stated in the Secretary of Labor and Human Resources' opinion. [^pr-tipped-employees-minimum] [^pr-tip-credit-conditions] The Secretary of Labor and Human Resources' opinion conditions the credit on informing employees in advance, on the employer being able to prove that the $2.13 direct payment plus tips reached the applicable minimum wage, and on the employees actually receiving the tips. [^pr-tip-credit-conditions] Act 47-2021 entitles tipped employees to the federal minimum cash wage for tipped workers, which together with tips must reach at least Puerto Rico's minimum wage. [^pr-tipped-employees-minimum] The Secretary of Labor and Human Resources has interpreted that rule to mean that a tipped employee is entitled to the $10.50 minimum wage, which the employer may meet with a direct payment of at least $2.13 an hour plus the tips received during the pay period. [^pr-tip-credit-cash-wage] The maximum tip credit is therefore $8.37 an hour, the $10.50 minimum wage less the $2.13 the employer must pay directly. [^pr-tip-credit-maximum]

The Secretary's opinion also states that the tip credit grows as the minimum wage rises, unless legislation or a special mandatory decree provides otherwise. [^pr-tip-credit-grows] Act 47-2021 directs the Minimum Wage Evaluation Commission to establish a base minimum wage for tipped workers that, combined with tips, meets or exceeds the minimum wage in applicable mandatory decrees. [^pr-tip-decree]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Employees may sue their employers for unpaid wages, and the Secretary of Labor and Human Resources may bring those claims on their behalf. [^pr-summary-procedure] [^pr-secretary-may-sue] An employee paid less than the law requires for regular hours, overtime or the meal period may recover the unpaid amounts plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, with costs, expenses and attorney's fees. [^pr-hours-claim-liquidated-damages] An employee paid less than the vacation and sick leave law, a collective agreement or an individual contract requires may likewise recover the difference plus an equal amount. [^pr-leave-claim-equal-amount] An employee may bring a wage claim in the Superior Part of the Court of First Instance under a summary procedure, [^pr-summary-procedure] and the Secretary may sue on the Secretary's own initiative or at an employee's request on behalf of similarly situated employees. [^pr-secretary-may-sue]

A settlement made outside court of a claim for regular-hour wages, overtime, meal-period pay or the equal liquidated-damages amount is void unless it is made before the Secretary or a designated department lawyer or official, [^pr-settlement-before-secretary] or through the department's labor-dispute mediators under the Secretary's rules. [^pr-settlement-mediators] Act 47-2021 sets a five-year period, running from the end of employment, for wage claims under that Act or under any contract or law, and wage claims made before its approval keep the period previously in force. [^pr-minimum-wage-claim-five-years] Recovery may reach back five years before suit or, for a former employee, before the separation. [^pr-minimum-wage-claim-lookback] The department's compilation of the vacation and sick leave law still shows that law's earlier one-year period [^pr-enforcement-one-year] and three-year lookback. [^pr-wage-claim-three-years] Violations of the vacation and sick leave law also carry civil liability for twice the damages caused, in addition to criminal penalties. [^pr-leave-law-double-damages]

# Wage and Hour Law in the District of Columbia[^about]

How the District of Columbia sets an inflation-indexed $18.40 minimum wage and a $10.30 tipped base wage, requires weekly overtime after 40 hours, sets no general meal or rest break rule, and sets final-pay, pay-frequency, pay-statement, worker-classification, tip-credit and wage-claim rules.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia's minimum wage is $18.40 an hour from July 1, 2026, for covered, nonexempt employees, regardless of employer size. [^dc-does-2026-minimum-wage-notice] [^dc-employee-definition-exclusions] [^dc-white-collar-newspaper-exceptions] The rate rises each July 1 in proportion to the annual average increase in the Consumer Price Index for the Washington metropolitan area, rounded to the nearest five cents. [^dc-minimum-wage-cpi-indexing] If the federal minimum wage ever exceeds the District rate, the District rate becomes the federal rate plus $1. [^dc-minimum-wage-federal-plus-one] The federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour, so the District rate is well above it. [^dc-federal-minimum-wage-rate]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The District minimum wage applies to all hours an employee works for the employer when the employee regularly spends more than half of working time in the District, or when the employment is based in the District and the employee spends a substantial amount of working time there and not more than half in any one state. [^dc-minimum-wage-coverage-fifty-percent] An employee who works at least two hours in the District for the same employer in a workweek must be paid at least the District minimum wage for each hour worked in the District. [^dc-minimum-wage-two-hours-in-district]

The Act's definition of employee excludes unpaid volunteers for educational, charitable, religious or nonprofit organizations, lay members of a religious organization engaged in religious functions, and casual babysitters in the employer's residence. [^dc-employee-definition-exclusions] The minimum wage and overtime rules do not apply to bona fide executive, administrative and professional employees and outside salespeople, as the U.S. Secretary of Labor defines those terms, or to newspaper carriers delivering to the home. [^dc-white-collar-newspaper-exceptions] Workers with disabilities must be paid at least the minimum wage unless the U.S. Department of Labor has issued a certificate under 29 U.S.C. § 214(c). [^dc-disability-certificate-minimum-wage] Security officers in office buildings have a separate, higher wage floor tied to the federal Service Contract Act rate for the guard 1 classification. [^dc-security-officer-wage-floor]

Employers must keep, for at least three years or any longer federal retention period, records of each employee's name, address and occupation, rate of pay and amount paid each pay period, and the precise times worked each day and each workweek. Precise time records are not required for employees who are not paid hourly and are exempt from the minimum wage and overtime rules. [^dc-records-three-years]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia requires overtime at no less than one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. [^dc-overtime-forty-hours-weekly] No District of Columbia statute requires daily overtime. The regular rate includes all pay for employment except the items excluded under 29 U.S.C. § 207(e)(1) to (7). [^dc-regular-rate-definition] Bona fide executive, administrative and professional employees and outside salespeople are exempt, using the federal definitions. [^dc-white-collar-overtime-exemption]

The overtime rule does not apply to seamen, railroad employees, salespeople, partsmen and mechanics primarily engaged in selling or servicing automobiles, trailers or trucks for a nonmanufacturing establishment primarily engaged in selling those vehicles to ultimate purchasers, or air carrier employees who voluntarily trade workdays to use air travel benefits. [^dc-overtime-specific-exemptions] A retail or service establishment employee is not owed overtime if the employee's regular rate is more than one and one-half times the applicable minimum wage and more than half of the employee's pay for a representative period of at least one month is commissions. [^dc-retail-commission-overtime-exception]

Working time includes time the employee is required to be on the premises, on duty or at a prescribed place, is permitted to work, must travel for the employer's business, or waits on the premises for work. It is interpreted under the federal hours-worked regulations in 29 C.F.R. Part 785, except that federal interpretations of the Portal-to-Portal Act have no effect in the District. [^dc-working-time-definition]

An employer covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act must also pay overtime at one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek unless a federal exemption applies. [^dc-federal-overtime-forty-hours]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia's wage statutes do not require employers to give adult employees meal or rest breaks. Whether a break that is given must be paid turns on the working-time definition, which the District interprets under the federal hours-worked regulations in 29 C.F.R. Part 785, except that federal interpretations of the Portal-to-Portal Act have no effect. [^dc-working-time-breaks-federal-rules] Time the employee is required to be on duty or on the premises, or is permitted to work, counts as working time. [^dc-working-time-breaks-federal-rules]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** Unless a collective agreement with a bona fide union provides otherwise, an employer that discharges an employee must pay the wages earned no later than the next working day, except that the employer has four days to check the accounts of an employee who was responsible for the employer's money. [^dc-final-pay-discharge-next-day] Also unless a collective agreement with a bona fide union provides otherwise, an employee who quits or resigns, and who does not have a written contract of employment for more than 30 days, must be paid by the next regular payday or within seven days of quitting, whichever is earlier. [^dc-final-pay-resignation-seven-days] An employer that pays late owes liquidated damages of 10% of the unpaid wages for each working day the failure continues, or treble the unpaid wages, whichever is smaller. [^dc-late-final-pay-liquidated-damages]

A working day excludes Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays. [^dc-working-day-definition]

If the amount owed is disputed in good faith, the employer must give the employee written notice of the amount it concedes and pay that amount unconditionally on time; accepting it does not release the rest of the claim. [^dc-conceded-wages-unconditional-payment]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** As a general rule, employers must pay all wages earned on regular paydays designated in advance and at least twice each calendar month, subject to two monthly-pay exceptions. [^dc-pay-frequency-twice-monthly] Bona fide administrative, executive and professional employees may be paid once a month, and an employer that has paid wages at least monthly by contract or custom may continue to do so. [^dc-pay-frequency-twice-monthly] [^dc-monthly-custom-lawful-money] No more than 10 working days may pass between the end of a pay period and its payday, unless a collective agreement with a bona fide labor organization sets a different period. [^dc-pay-frequency-twice-monthly]

Wages must be paid in U.S. money or by checks payable on demand. [^dc-monthly-custom-lawful-money]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** With each wage payment, the employer must give an itemized statement showing the date of payment, gross wages, deductions and additions (with a separate line for tips), net wages, hours worked in the pay period, and the employee's tip-declaration form separating cash and credit-card tips. [^dc-itemized-wage-statement-contents] Since January 1, 2026, the statement must also list sources of pay beyond base wages and tips, such as bonuses, commissions and amounts calculated as a percentage of service charges. [^dc-wage-statement-other-compensation-2026] At hiring, and whenever the information changes, the employer, except where § 32-1008.01 provides otherwise, must give a written notice of the employer's name and address, the rate and basis of pay, any tip, meal or lodging allowance claimed, overtime and exemptions, the tip-sharing policy, and the regular payday. [^dc-hiring-notice-contents]

Wages are defined as compensation after lawful deductions, and include bonuses, commissions, cash fringe benefits, overtime premiums and other pay promised under a contract or law. [^dc-wages-definition-lawful-deductions] An employer must keep signed or electronic acknowledgments showing that employees received the hiring notice. [^dc-notice-acknowledgment-records] If the employer does not give the notice, that failure counts against the credibility of the employer's testimony about the promised rate of pay, and the three-year limitations period for a wage claim does not begin to run until the employee has received all required statements and notices. [^dc-notice-failure-credibility-tolling]

The Mayor assesses an administrative penalty of $500 for each failure to provide an itemized wage statement or the written notice. [^dc-wage-statement-administrative-penalty]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** District of Columbia courts use the economic-reality test from the federal Fair Labor Standards Act to decide whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor under the Wage Payment and Collection Law and the Minimum Wage Revision Act. [^dc-eckington-steinke-flsa-test-adopted] [^dc-eckington-economic-reality-factors] In *Eckington House Mental Health Services, LLC v. Office of Wage-Hour* (D.C. Nov. 13, 2025), the D.C. Court of Appeals listed the factors: the alleged employer's control over the manner of the work, the worker's opportunity for profit or loss, the worker's investment in equipment or employment of workers, special skill, permanency and duration, whether the service is an integral part of the business, whether the work is part of the regular business, and whether the parties believe they are creating an employment relationship. [^dc-eckington-economic-reality-factors] No single factor decides the question. [^dc-eckington-no-factor-dispositive] Both statutes define employment broadly to reach anyone the employer suffers or permits to work. [^dc-suffer-or-permit-wage-payment] [^dc-employ-suffer-or-permit-minimum-wage]

In *Eckington House*, the court upheld the conclusion that most factors weighed in favor of classifying the worker as an employee [^dc-eckington-most-factors-employee], and explained that the employer's independent-contractor label did not on its own establish the parties' understanding or absolve the employer of potential misclassification. [^dc-eckington-label-not-controlling]

For construction services, the Workplace Fraud Act presumes an employment relationship when an individual performs work for pay, unless the employer shows the individual is an exempt person or meets all three parts of a test: freedom from control and direction, an independently established trade or business, and work outside the employer's usual course of business. [^dc-workplace-fraud-construction-only] [^dc-construction-workplace-fraud-presumption]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia allows a partial tip credit. Since July 1, 2026, the tipped minimum wage is 56% of the District minimum wage, with tips on top, provided the employee actually receives enough tips to make up the difference between the cash wage and the full minimum wage. [^dc-tipped-wage-fifty-six-percent] The Department of Employment Services announced that the tipped base wage rose from $10.00 to $10.30 an hour on July 1, 2026, and that the employer must pay the difference if weekly-averaged tips plus the base wage fall short of the full minimum wage. [^dc-does-2026-tipped-base-wage] The tipped wage is scheduled to rise to 60% of the minimum wage on July 1, 2028, and in further steps to 75% on July 1, 2034. [^dc-tipped-wage-future-steps] [^dc-tipped-wage-seventy-five-percent-2034]

Under the current statute, the tipped wage does not phase out: the last scheduled step sets it at 75% of the minimum wage, with tips on top, as of July 1, 2034. [^dc-tipped-wage-seventy-five-percent-2034]

An employer may use the tipped wage only if it has given the employee notice of the tipped-wage rules, of whether tips are shared and the tip-sharing policy, and of the percentage by which credit-card fees reduce card tips; has posted any tip-sharing policy; and lets employees keep all tips except for sharing among employees who customarily receive tips. [^dc-tip-credit-notice-conditions] An employer that pays employees the tipped wage, other than a hotel employer, must use a third-party payroll business to prepare its payroll. [^dc-tipped-third-party-payroll]

The tipped-wage schedule does not apply to District government employees or to employees performing services under District contracts. [^dc-tipped-wage-government-contract-exclusion]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia enforces its wage laws through employee lawsuits, the Mayor and the Attorney General. [^dc-civil-action-treble-damages] [^dc-mayor-enforces-wage-laws] [^dc-mayor-attorney-general-enforcement] An employee who prevails in a civil action is awarded reasonable attorney's fees and costs, and is entitled to back wages, liquidated damages equal to treble the unpaid wages, statutory penalties and other appropriate relief, including reinstatement. [^dc-civil-action-treble-damages] Under the Minimum Wage Revision Act, a court may award less than treble but not less than the unpaid wages only if the employer shows good faith, reasonable grounds for believing it was complying, and prompt payment of the full amount claimed. [^dc-good-faith-liquidated-reduction] A wage action must be brought within three years after the claim accrued, or after the last occurrence of a continuing violation. [^dc-three-year-limitations-tolling]

The limitations period is tolled while an administrative complaint is pending with the Mayor, and during any period the employer fails to give the employee actual or constructive notice of the employee's rights. [^dc-three-year-limitations-tolling] Employees may sue individually, jointly, as a collective action, as a class action, or through a labor organization. [^dc-civil-action-treble-damages]

The Attorney General may sue for restitution and injunctive or compensatory relief, and on behalf of an aggrieved employee can recover back wages and liquidated damages equal to treble those wages. [^dc-mayor-attorney-general-enforcement] For violations of the Wage Payment and Collection Law, the Mayor assesses administrative penalties of $50 for each affected employee for each day of a first violation and $100 for a later violation. [^dc-wage-payment-administrative-penalties] For violations of the Wage Payment and Collection Law, the Living Wage Act and the Sick and Safe Leave Act, general contractors are jointly and severally liable for their subcontractors' violations, and temporary staffing firms and their clients are jointly and severally liable to the staffing firm's employees; the staffing firm and client must be notified of the alleged violations at least 30 days before a claim is filed. [^dc-joint-liability-contractors-staffing] [^dc-staffing-firm-client-joint-liability]

Except where the Wage Payment and Collection Law expressly permits otherwise, its provisions cannot be set aside by private agreement. [^dc-no-private-waiver] An agreement to work for less than the minimum wage is no defense to a claim for unpaid wages. [^dc-agreement-less-than-wage-no-defense] Under the Minimum Wage Revision Act, an employer may not retaliate against an employee for complaining about a violation, taking part in a proceeding, providing information, testifying or exercising rights under the Act [^dc-retaliation-prohibited-activities], and adverse action within 90 days of protected activity is presumed retaliatory unless the employer rebuts it by clear and convincing evidence. [^dc-retaliation-ninety-day-presumption]

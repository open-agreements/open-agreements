# Wage and Hour Law in Oregon[^about]

A plain-language practice note on Oregon wage and hour law, covering the three-tier regional minimum wage and its annual inflation adjustment, weekly overtime and the industry-specific daily hour limits, mandatory paid rest periods and duty-free meal periods, final-pay deadlines and penalty wages, payday and itemized pay-statement duties, the statutory independent-contractor test, the ban on tip credits, and enforcement through the Bureau of Labor and Industries and private lawsuits.

Oregon is one of the harder states to summarize in a single number, because its minimum wage is regional and moves every July. Layered on top of that are meal and rest period rules written into administrative rule, fast final-pay deadlines with a penalty-wage remedy, and a flat ban on counting tips toward the wage floor. This note walks through each topic for both employers and employees, and pins every statement to the statute, rule, or agency page that supports it. For how these questions are framed across states, see our [wage and hour practice guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour).

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Oregon has no single statewide minimum wage — it has three regional rates, and for July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027 the Bureau of Labor and Industries lists them as $16.80 per hour in the Portland metro area, $15.55 per hour in the standard counties, and $14.55 per hour in the non-urban counties. [^boli-mw-effective-period]
Which of the three a worker gets depends on where the employer is located. ORS 653.025 builds the geography into the statute itself — the Portland metro rate sits $1.25 above the base rate, and the non-urban rate sits $1 below it. [^ors-653-025-2h][^ors-653-025-3h] The statute then directs the Commissioner of the Bureau of Labor and Industries to adopt rules for deciding where an employer is located. [^ors-653-025-4]
Those rules turn on where the work happens. If an employee performs more than half of a pay period’s work at the employer’s permanent fixed Oregon business location, the region of that business sets the rate. [^oar-839-020-0011-1a] If not, the region where the employee actually performs the work is treated as the employer’s location, and the employer must pay at least that rate for every hour in the pay period. [^oar-839-020-0011-2a] The agency puts the same idea in everyday terms for workers. [^boli-mw-location]
Since June 30, 2023, the base rate has been a figure adjusted annually for inflation, [^ors-653-025-1j] calculated each year by April 30 against the U.S. City Average Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, [^ors-653-025-5b] and the adjusted figure replaces the statutory amount on July 1. [^ors-653-025-5c]
All three Oregon rates sit well above the federal floor: the Bureau of Labor and Industries notes that the federal minimum wage remains $7.25 per hour. [^boli-federal-floor] Oregon also closes off the local-ordinance route — the state preempts the authority of local governments to set minimum wage requirements. [^ors-653-017-preemption] Three narrow exceptions remain: a local government may still set minimum wage requirements for public employers, in specifications for its own public contracts, and as a condition of direct tax abatements or subsidies it provides to private employers with 10 or more employees. [^ors-653-017-3]

The annual reset is automatic, and the statute says exactly when it lands:

"The wage amount as adjusted under this subsection becomes effective as the new Oregon minimum wage amount, replacing the minimum wage amount specified in subsection (1)(j) of this section, on July 1 of the year in which the calculation is made."[^ors-653-025-5c]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> If an employee works in more than one region during a single pay period, the employer must keep records of the locations worked — unless it simply pays the highest rate of any region worked for all hours in the work period. Skipping both the records and the higher rate is the common failure point. [^oar-839-020-0011-2c]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Oregon’s general overtime rule is weekly, not daily: for covered, nonexempt employees, work over 40 hours in a week must be paid at not less than one and one-half times the regular rate, computed without commissions, overrides, spiffs, bonuses, tips, or similar benefits. [^oar-839-020-0030-1] Two groups sit outside that 40-hour line. Agricultural workers have their own declining schedule: an employer may not work them beyond 55 hours a week in 2023 and 2024, 48 hours in 2025 and 2026, or 40 hours from 2027 onward. [^ors-653-272-1] An agricultural worker may exceed the applicable cap only if the employer pays one and one-half times the regular rate for every hour over it. [^ors-653-272-2] So for a 2026 Oregon farm payroll the overtime line is 48 hours, and for calendar year 2027 and after it is 40. Oregon also imposes daily hour limits and daily overtime in named industries rather than across the workforce, and those rules are set out below.
The workweek itself is the employer’s to set, but not to manipulate — the start of the workweek may be changed only if the change is meant to be permanent and is not designed to evade the overtime rule. [^oar-839-020-0030-workweek-change]
Where Oregon does go further than the federal baseline is in specific industries, through maximum-hour statutes rather than a general daily overtime rule.
In mills, factories, and other manufacturing establishments, an employer generally may not require or permit work beyond 10 hours in a day or 55 hours in a workweek. [^ors-652-020-2a] An employee may work up to three hours past the daily limit, and those overtime hours must be paid at one and one-half times the regular rate. [^ors-652-020-3] The same statute also restricts back-to-back scheduling: after a shift of eight or more hours, a manufacturing employee generally may not be required to start the next shift less than 10 hours later, subject to an emergency exception. [^ors-652-020-5a]
Sawmills, planing mills, shingle mills, and logging camps have tighter limits — eight hours in a day, exclusive of about an hour, or 48 hours in a workweek. [^ors-652-020-2d]
In both the manufacturing and the cannery regimes the employer must calculate the overtime on a daily basis and on a weekly basis and pay the greater of the two amounts when the employee passes both thresholds in the same workweek. [^ors-652-020-3c][^ors-653-265-3] Paying only the weekly figure when the daily figure is larger is an underpayment.
Canneries, driers, and packing plants run on the 10-hour and 55-hour limits, [^ors-653-265-2a] with a route to longer days: an employer may permit work past 10 hours in a day if it pays one and one-half times the regular rate for hourly employees, or one and one-half times the regular price for piece workers. [^ors-653-265-2d]

The cannery statute writes its daily premium as a condition on working past the cap:

"An employer may permit an employee described in paragraph (a) of this subsection to work more than 10 hours in any one day if the employer compensates the employee as follows: (A) One and one-half times the employee’s regular rate of pay for each hour the employee works over 10 hours in any one day if the employee is an hourly employee"[^ors-653-265-2d]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Oregon requires both meal periods and rest periods, and it sets them by administrative rule rather than by statute. For each work period of at least six but not more than eight hours, an employer must provide a meal period of at least 30 continuous minutes during which the employee is relieved of all duties. [^oar-839-020-0050-2a] No meal period is required for a work period of less than six hours, [^oar-839-020-0050-2c-short] and for work periods longer than eight hours the number of meal periods comes from Appendix A of the rule. [^oar-839-020-0050-2c-appendix]
Timing matters. For a work period of seven hours or less, the meal period must start after the second hour worked and finish before the fifth hour begins. [^oar-839-020-0050-2d]
The meal period is unpaid only when the employee is genuinely free of work. If the employee is not relieved of all duties for 30 continuous minutes, the employer must pay for the entire 30-minute meal period. [^oar-839-020-0050-2b]
Rest periods, by contrast, are paid. An employer must provide at least ten continuous minutes of duty-free rest for each four-hour segment or major part of one worked, with no deduction from pay. [^oar-839-020-0050-6a] As the nature of the work allows, the rest period should fall roughly in the middle of each such segment, [^oar-839-020-0050-6a-a] and rest periods must be given in addition to, and taken separately from, the meal period. [^oar-839-020-0050-6a-b]

The pay consequence of an interrupted meal is stated flatly in the rule:

"Except as otherwise provided in this rule, if an employee is not relieved of all duties for 30 continuous minutes during the meal period, the employer must pay the employee for the entire 30-minute meal period."[^oar-839-020-0050-2b]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** Oregon sets short, specific deadlines for final wages, and the deadline depends on how the job ended. When an employer discharges an employee, or employment ends by mutual agreement, all earned and unpaid wages are due no later than the end of the first business day after the discharge or termination. [^ors-652-140-1]
When an employee quits after giving at least 48 hours’ notice — excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and holidays — the wages are due immediately. [^ors-652-140-2a] Without that notice, they are due within five days excluding weekends and holidays, or at the next regularly scheduled payday, whichever comes first. [^ors-652-140-2b] Where that employee is regularly required to submit time records, the employer pays its estimate of the wages due within five days and the balance within five days after the records arrive. [^ors-652-140-2c]
If the termination falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or holiday, payment is due by the end of the first business day afterward, with a second-business-day rule for employment related to activities authorized under ORS chapter 565. [^ors-652-140-3]
One carve-out is worth checking before applying any of this: the section does not apply to employment covered by a collective bargaining agreement that itself provides for payment of wages on termination. [^ors-652-140-5]
Late payment triggers penalty wages, covered in the next section.

## What is the penalty for paying late? {#late-pay-penalty}

**Short answer.** Oregon backs its final-pay deadlines with penalty wages: if an employer willfully fails to pay wages owed to a separated employee, the wages continue from the due date at the same hourly rate for eight hours per day until paid or until an action is filed. [^ors-652-150-1] The clock is capped — penalty wages cannot run more than 30 days from the due date. [^ors-652-150-30-day-cap]
A written notice of nonpayment changes the math. If the employee, or someone on the employee’s behalf, submits written notice of nonpayment, the penalty may not exceed 100 percent of the unpaid wages unless the employer fails to pay in full within 12 days after receiving that notice. [^ors-652-150-2a]
There is a defense. An employer may avoid liability for this penalty by showing financial inability to pay the wages at the time they accrued. [^ors-652-150-5]
An employer that pays less than the minimum wage owed under the ORS 653.010 to 653.261 scheme is liable to the affected employee for the full amount of wages, less anything actually paid, and for the civil penalties provided in ORS 652.150. [^ors-653-055-1]

The 12-day window after written notice is the practical pressure point:

"If the employee or a person on behalf of the employee submits a written notice of nonpayment, the penalty may not exceed 100 percent of the employee’s unpaid wages or compensation unless the employer fails to pay the full amount of the employee’s unpaid wages or compensation within 12 days after receiving the notice."[^ors-652-150-2a]

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Oregon does not name a calendar frequency such as weekly or semi-monthly; instead, every employer must establish and maintain a regular payday and pay all employees the wages due and owing on that date. [^ors-652-120-1] The outer limit is a gap: a payday may not extend beyond 35 days from the time employees started work, or from the last regular payday. [^ors-652-120-2] Paying more often is expressly allowed. [^ors-652-120-3]
Oregon’s pay-stub requirements are unusually detailed. Employers must give each employee a written itemized statement of earnings each time the employee receives a compensation payment, except for employees specifically exempt under ORS 653.020. [^oar-839-020-0012-1] The statement must be written and sufficiently itemized. [^ors-652-610-1b]
The itemized list includes the rate or rates of pay, whether pay is by hour, shift, day, week, salary, piece, or commission, gross wages, net wages, and the amount and purpose of each deduction for the pay period. [^ors-652-610-1b] It must also show any allowances claimed as part of the minimum wage. [^ors-652-610-1b]
Deductions are restricted. An employer may not withhold, deduct, or divert any portion of wages unless a statutory condition is met, [^ors-652-610-3] one of which is that the deduction is voluntarily authorized in writing by the employee, is for the employee’s benefit, and is recorded in the employer’s books. [^ors-652-610-3] Failing to pay the amount as required constitutes an unlawful deduction. [^ors-652-610-4]

The statute sets an outer limit on the gap between one payday and the next:

"Payday may not extend beyond a period of 35 days from the time that the employees entered upon their work, or from the date of the last regular payday."[^ors-652-120-2]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Oregon uses a broad statutory definition of employment for its wage and hour chapter and a separate multi-factor contractor test in ORS 670.600, and the two are not written to cover the same ground. Under the wage and hour chapter, to employ includes to suffer or permit to work, with an exclusion for genuinely voluntary or donated services. [^ors-653-010-employ] An employer is any person who employs another person, including the state and its political subdivisions. [^ors-653-010-employer]
The statutory contractor test lives in ORS 670.600, and its opening words matter: it defines independent contractor as used in ORS chapters 316, 656, 657, 671 and 701 — a person who provides services for remuneration and who meets the conditions that follow. [^ors-670-600-2]
Oregon's wage statutes borrow that contractor definition in one narrow place, and the boundary is worth knowing. ORS 652.197 makes an owner or direct contractor jointly liable for unpaid wages owed to workers on a construction project, and an owner or direct contractor may not escape that liability by calling the worker an independent contractor unless the worker qualifies as one under ORS 670.600. [^ors-652-197-5a] In an action under that section a person performing labor within the scope of a construction contract is presumed to be an employee, and the presumption is rebuttable. [^ors-652-197-5b] By its terms that borrowing operates within ORS 652.197, which is about liability for wages owed on a construction project. The Legislative Assembly has also shown what it takes to reach chapter 653 expressly: ORS 670.610 provides that notwithstanding ORS 670.600, and for purposes of ORS chapter 653, a referee in a youth or adult recreational soccer match is an independent contractor. [^ors-670-610] A one-occupation override written that way only makes sense if ORS 670.600 does not otherwise govern chapter 653.
Within its own scope, the definition reaches a person who provides services for remuneration and who satisfies each of the conditions the subsection then lists. [^ors-670-600-2] The person must be free from direction and control over the means and manner of providing the services, subject only to the right to specify desired results. [^ors-670-600-2] The person must be customarily engaged in an independently established business, except as provided in subsection (4). [^ors-670-600-2] The person must be licensed under ORS chapter 671 or 701 if a license is required for the services. [^ors-670-600-2] And the person must be responsible for obtaining any other licenses or certificates necessary to provide the services. [^ors-670-600-2d]
That last element is where the multi-factor counting happens: a person is considered customarily engaged in an independently established business if any three of the listed requirements are met. [^ors-670-600-3] One of them is serving two or more different customers within a 12-month period, or routinely engaging in advertising, solicitation, or other marketing reasonably calculated to obtain new contracts for similar services. [^ors-670-600-3c]
There is an agricultural carve-out: the independently-established-business element does not apply to a person who files a Schedule F as part of an income tax return and provides farm labor or farm services reportable on Schedule C. [^ors-670-600-4]

The statute is explicit about how many of the requirements have to be met:

"For purposes of subsection (2)(b) of this section, a person is considered to be customarily engaged in an independently established business if any three of the following requirements are met: (a) The person maintains a business location: (A) That is separate from the business or work location of the person for whom the services are provided; or (B) That is in a portion of the person’s residence and that portion is used primarily for the business."[^ors-670-600-3]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Oregon does not allow any tip credit — employers, including those regulated under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, may not count tips toward the minimum wage required by ORS 653.010 to 653.261. [^ors-653-035-3] Tipped workers in Oregon must receive the full applicable regional minimum wage in wages, with tips on top. The Bureau of Labor and Industries states the same rule in plain terms for workers. [^boli-tip-credit]
Tip pooling is treated separately from the tip credit. According to the Bureau of Labor and Industries, an employer may require workers to pool tips, but management and the house cannot share in the pool. [^boli-tip-pool]
One deduction that is allowed does not involve tips at all: an employer may deduct from the minimum wage the fair market value of lodging, meals, or other facilities or services furnished for the employee’s private benefit. [^ors-653-035-1]

The statutory ban is unqualified, and it reaches employers already covered by federal law:

"Employers, including employers regulated under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, may not include any amount received by employees as tips in determining the amount of the minimum wage required to be paid by ORS 653.010 to 653.261."[^ors-653-035-3]

## How is it enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Oregon wage claims can go two ways — through the state labor agency or through court — and the statutes provide for both. The Commissioner of the Bureau of Labor and Industries must enforce ORS 652.310 to 652.414. [^ors-652-330-1] Where a civil action could be brought to collect a wage claim, the commissioner may instead provide an administrative proceeding to determine the claim’s validity and enforce collection. [^ors-652-330-1d] That administrative route is subject to the employer’s right to elect a trial in a court of law. [^ors-652-330-1d-election]
Employees can sue. An employer that pays less than the wages owed under ORS 653.010 to 653.261 or 653.272 is liable to the affected employee. [^ors-653-055-1-enf] A private agreement to work for less than the required rate is no defense to that action. [^ors-653-055-2] Separately, an employee has a private cause of action where an employer violates the maximum-hours provisions by requiring work more than three hours past the daily limit or beyond the weekly limit. [^ors-652-020-8a]
In an action under ORS 653.055, the court may award reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing party. [^ors-653-055-4] In a wage-collection action where the wages went unpaid for 48 hours excluding weekends and holidays after becoming due, the court must include reasonable attorney fees at trial and on appeal in a judgment for the plaintiff, unless one of two exceptions applies. [^ors-652-200-2] Those exceptions are — a willful violation of the employment contract by the employee, or a finding that the plaintiff’s attorney unreasonably failed to give the employer written notice of the wage claim before filing the action. [^ors-652-200-2]
Retaliation is separately prohibited. An employer may not discharge or otherwise discriminate against an employee on the grounds listed in ORS 652.355, [^ors-652-355-1] including because the employee made a wage claim or discussed, inquired about, or consulted an attorney or agency about one. [^ors-652-355-1] A parallel prohibition sits in the wage and hour chapter, [^ors-653-060-1] covering an employee who has testified or is about to testify in such proceedings. [^ors-653-060-1c]
On timing, which period applies turns on what is being claimed. An action for overtime or premium pay, or for penalties or liquidated damages for failing to pay it, must be commenced within two years. [^ors-12-110-3] An action on a statutory penalty or forfeiture given to the aggrieved party must be commenced within three years. [^ors-12-100-2] An action on a contract, or on a liability created by statute that is not a penalty, must be commenced within six years. [^ors-12-080] Because a single unpaid-wage dispute can carry all three kinds of relief, how the claim is characterized decides the deadline.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Before filing a wage-collection suit, send the employer written notice of the wage claim. Under ORS 652.200(2) a court may withhold the otherwise mandatory attorney fee award if it finds the plaintiff’s attorney unreasonably failed to give that pre-suit notice. [^ors-652-200-2]

# Wage and Hour Law in South Carolina[^about]

How South Carolina leaves the minimum wage, overtime, adult breaks and tip credits to federal law, bars local minimum wages and benefit mandates, and sets the Payment of Wages Act's written-notice, pay-statement, deduction and 48-hour-or-next-payday final pay rules, with treble damages and a three-year limit for unpaid wages.

## Does South Carolina have its own minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** South Carolina has no generally applicable state minimum wage statute. Employers covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act must pay at least the federal minimum wage of $7.25 an hour. [^sc-federal-minimum-wage-seven-twenty-five] A South Carolina city, county, school district or other political subdivision may not require a minimum wage higher than the federal rate. [^sc-local-minimum-wage-preemption]

The local preemption statute does not stop a political subdivision from setting wage rates in contracts to which it is a party. [^sc-local-minimum-wage-preemption] A separate statute bars political subdivisions from requiring private employers to provide employee benefits, a term that includes paid sick leave, paid vacation leave and paid holidays. [^sc-local-employee-benefit-preemption] No South Carolina statute requires private employers to provide paid sick leave.

Employers, community rehabilitation programs and hospital patient care workers at regional centers may not use the federal subminimum wage certificate program to pay employees with disabilities less than the federal minimum wage. [^sc-no-subminimum-wage-disability]

Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## Does South Carolina law require overtime pay? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** South Carolina has no statute requiring private employers to pay overtime, daily or weekly. The federal Fair Labor Standards Act requires a covered employer to pay at least one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek unless a federal exemption applies. [^sc-federal-overtime-forty-hours]

## Must South Carolina employers give meal or rest breaks? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** South Carolina has no general statute requiring meal or rest breaks for adult employees. Employers must allow break time to express breast milk unless doing so would create an undue hardship, [^sc-lactation-break-time] and employers with 15 or more employees may owe longer or more frequent breaks as a pregnancy accommodation. [^sc-pregnancy-accommodation-duty] [^sc-pregnancy-accommodation-breaks] [^sc-human-affairs-employer-fifteen] The Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation says no South Carolina law requires breaks or a lunch period. [^sc-llr-faq-no-breaks-final-pay] For employers covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, short rest periods are customarily paid and must be counted as hours worked, [^sc-federal-short-rest-breaks-paid] while a meal period may be unpaid only if the employee is completely relieved from duty. [^sc-federal-meal-period-relieved-duty]

Every employer with one or more employees, including the State and its political subdivisions, must give an employee reasonable unpaid break time, or let her use paid break or meal time, each day to express breast milk, and must make reasonable efforts to provide a private place near the work area other than a toilet stall. [^sc-lactation-break-time] The employer need not provide the break time if it would create an undue hardship on the employer's operations. [^sc-lactation-break-time] An employer with 15 or more employees must also make reasonable accommodations for medical needs arising from pregnancy, childbirth or related conditions unless it can show undue hardship, [^sc-pregnancy-accommodation-duty] [^sc-human-affairs-employer-fifteen] and those accommodations may include more frequent or longer break periods and more frequent bathroom breaks. [^sc-pregnancy-accommodation-breaks] Federal law separately requires covered employers to give reasonable break time to express breast milk for one year after the child's birth and a private place other than a bathroom. [^sc-federal-pumping-break-one-year] An employer with fewer than 50 employees is exempt from the federal requirement only if compliance would impose an undue hardship. [^sc-federal-pumping-small-employer-hardship]

## When is a final paycheck due in South Carolina? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** When an employer separates an employee from the payroll for any reason, it must pay all wages due within 48 hours of the separation or by the next regular payday, which may not be more than 30 days later. [^sc-final-pay-forty-eight-hours] The Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation reads the rule the same way whether the employee quits or is fired. [^sc-llr-faq-final-pay-quit-or-fired]

Wages for this purpose include vacation, holiday and sick leave payments that are due under an employer policy or employment contract, but not funds placed in pension or profit-sharing plans. [^sc-wages-definition-vacation-sick-leave] Whether unused vacation or sick time must be paid out therefore depends on the employer's policy or the contract. [^sc-wages-definition-vacation-sick-leave]

If the employer and employee dispute the wages, the employer must give written notice of the amount it concedes is due and pay that amount without condition within the statutory deadline, and the employee's acceptance does not release the rest of the claim. [^sc-dispute-pay-conceded-amount] An employee who is not paid as the final pay statute requires may sue for up to three times the unpaid wages, as described under [wage claim enforcement](#enforcement). [^sc-final-pay-treble-damages]

## How often must South Carolina employers pay wages, and what may they deduct? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** South Carolina sets no minimum pay frequency, but every employer must pay all wages due at the time and place it designated in its written notice to the employee. [^sc-pay-at-designated-time-place] At hiring, an employer other than a domestic employer in a private home or an employer with fewer than five employees throughout the preceding twelve months must tell each employee in writing, or by posting the terms conspicuously at or near the workplace, the normal hours and wages agreed on, the time and place of payment and the deductions that will be made, and must give at least seven calendar days' written notice before changing those terms, other than a wage increase. [^sc-written-notice-records-pay-statement] [^sc-act-applies-all-employers-notice-exemptions]
An employer may not withhold or divert any part of an employee's wages unless state or federal law requires or permits it or the employer has given the employee written notice of the amount and terms of the deduction under the hiring-notice rule. [^sc-deductions-need-law-or-written-notice]

The written-notice, recordkeeping and pay-statement section does not apply to employers of domestic labor in private homes or to employers with fewer than five employees at all times during the preceding twelve months, though the rest of the Payment of Wages Act applies to all South Carolina employers. [^sc-act-applies-all-employers-notice-exemptions] The Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation says an employer with five or more employees may lower a rate of pay only after giving the employee written notice at least seven calendar days in advance. [^sc-llr-faq-pay-decrease-notice]

Wages must be paid in lawful United States money or by a negotiable warrant or check dated on the payday, or by deposit to the employee's account at a federally insured financial institution doing business in South Carolina. [^sc-medium-of-payment] [^sc-direct-deposit-statement-free-withdrawal] No provision of the Payment of Wages Act may be set aside by a private agreement. [^sc-no-private-agreement-waiver]

## What must a South Carolina pay statement show? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** Except for domestic employers in private homes and employers with fewer than five employees throughout the preceding twelve months, South Carolina employers must give each employee an itemized statement of gross pay and deductions for each pay period. [^sc-itemized-statement-records] [^sc-pay-statement-exemptions] No South Carolina statute lists other required contents such as hours worked or rates of pay. [^sc-itemized-statement-records]

The employer must also keep, for three years, records of the names and addresses of all employees and of the wages paid each payday and the deductions made. [^sc-itemized-statement-records] Employers of domestic labor in private homes and employers with fewer than five employees throughout the preceding twelve months are exempt from these pay-statement and recordkeeping rules, [^sc-pay-statement-exemptions] but an employee paid by direct deposit must still receive a statement of earnings and withholdings. [^sc-direct-deposit-earnings-statement]

## How does South Carolina decide who is an employee rather than an independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** South Carolina's Payment of Wages Act does not define employee or set its own test for telling employees from independent contractors. [^sc-wage-act-definitions-no-employee] In workers' compensation cases, the South Carolina Supreme Court decides employee status under the common-law right-of-control test, which asks whether the purported employer had the right to control the worker in performing the work. [^sc-shatto-right-of-control] The court examines four factors: direct evidence of the right or exercise of control, the furnishing of equipment, the method of payment and the right to fire, [^sc-shatto-four-factors] and weighs them evenhandedly in both directions. [^sc-shatto-four-factors-evenhanded]

In our review, we found no reported South Carolina appellate decision adopting a different employee test under the Payment of Wages Act or applying the workers' compensation test to that Act. Federal classification rules are discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## Can South Carolina employers count tips toward the minimum wage? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** South Carolina has no tip-credit statute, because it has no state minimum wage. Employers covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act may pay a tipped employee a cash wage of at least $2.13 an hour and count tips toward the rest of the federal minimum wage, [^sc-federal-tipped-cash-wage-two-thirteen] but only if the employee has been told about the federal tip-credit provisions and keeps all tips received, apart from a pool among employees who customarily and regularly receive tips. [^sc-federal-tip-credit-notice-retention]

A South Carolina political subdivision may not require a minimum wage above the federal rate, except in contracts to which it is a party, and the statute defines wage by reference to the federal provision that contains the tip credit. [^sc-local-wage-uses-federal-definition] [^sc-federal-tip-credit-notice-retention] No South Carolina statute addresses tip pooling.

## How are unpaid wages recovered in South Carolina, and what penalties apply? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee who is not paid wages due under the Payment of Wages Act's payment or final pay rules may sue for an amount equal to three times the full unpaid wages, plus costs and reasonable attorney's fees as the court allows. [^sc-treble-damages-three-year-limit] The suit must be filed within three years after the wages became due. [^sc-treble-damages-three-year-limit] The South Carolina Supreme Court has held that treble damages are discretionary rather than mandatory, so a court may decline to award them where the employer withheld wages in a bona fide dispute. [^sc-rice-treble-damages-discretionary] [^sc-rice-treble-not-mandatory]

On an employee's written complaint, the Director of the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation may investigate and, if a violation exists, try to resolve it through mediation and conciliation. [^sc-llr-investigation-mediation] The Director gives a written warning for a first violation of the hiring-notice, recordkeeping and pay-statement rules and assesses a civil penalty of up to $100 for each later violation, and assesses a civil penalty of up to $100 for each violation of the payment and deduction rules, with each failure to pay a separate offense. [^sc-civil-penalties-hundred-dollars] The Director's inspectors may enter at reasonable times, question employers and employees, and inspect time and payroll records. [^sc-llr-right-of-entry]

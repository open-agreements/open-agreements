# Wage and Hour Law in Wisconsin[^about]

How Wisconsin law sets the minimum wage, weekly overtime, meal and rest periods, final-pay deadlines, pay frequency, pay-statement and deduction rules, worker classification, the tip credit, and wage-claim enforcement.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Wisconsin employers must pay most employees at least $7.25 per hour, with a lower statutory rate for opportunity employees and a lower cash rate for tipped employees that applies only when payroll records show that weekly tips plus wages reach the applicable minimum. [^wi-minimum-wage-rate] [^wi-opportunity-employee-wage] [^wi-tipped-employee-minimum-rates]
Every wage a Wisconsin employer pays or agrees to pay an employee must be at least the applicable statutory minimum wage, except as a separate licensing provision of the minimum wage law provides. [^wi-minimum-wage-pay-requirement]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The minimum wage for a minor employee is also $7.25 per hour. [^wi-minor-minimum-wage] An opportunity employee is a person under 20 years of age who is in the first 90 consecutive days of employment with the employer [^wi-opportunity-employee-definition], and the minimum wage for an opportunity employee is $5.90 per hour. [^wi-opportunity-employee-wage] The minimum wage for an agricultural employee is $7.25 per hour. [^wi-agricultural-minimum-wage] A counselor at a seasonal recreational or educational camp, including a day camp, has a weekly minimum wage of $350 if meals and lodging are not furnished, $265 if only meals are furnished, and $210 if both meals and lodging are furnished. [^wi-camp-counselor-weekly-wage] The minimum wage for a golf caddy is $10.50 for caddying 18 holes and $5.90 for caddying 9 holes. [^wi-golf-caddy-minimum-wage]

Under the Department of Workforce Development (DWD) minimum wage rules, the rates apply to all employees at private employments, including nonprofit organizations and indentured apprentices, whether paid on a time, piece rate, commission or other basis. [^wi-minimum-wage-private-coverage]

The minimum wage statute directs the department to set by rule the minimum wage for an employee or worker with a disability covered by a license under the minimum wage law, a student learner, and a student employed by an independent college or university for less than 20 hours per week. [^wi-department-special-minimum-rates] The minimum wage statute also directs the department to exempt by rule casual employment in and around an employer's home on an irregular or intermittent basis for not more than 15 hours per week; companionship by a person who resides in the home of an employer who, due to advanced age or physical or mental disability, cannot care for his or her own needs and who spends not more than 15 hours per week on general household work; and an elementary or secondary school student performing student work-like activities in the student's school. [^wi-department-minimum-wage-exemptions] The department's minimum rates do not apply to casual employment in or around a home in work usual to the employer's home that is not connected with or part of the employer's business, trade or profession. [^wi-casual-home-employment-exclusion]

The department may not establish a different minimum wage for men and women. [^wi-no-gender-specific-minimum]

No Wisconsin city, village, town or county may enact and administer a minimum wage ordinance, and any such ordinance in effect on June 16, 2005, is void. [^wi-local-ordinance-preemption] The legislature declared a uniform statewide minimum wage a matter of statewide concern. [^wi-statewide-uniform-minimum]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Under the Department of Workforce Development's overtime rule, Wisconsin private employers in manufactories, mechanical or mercantile establishments, restaurants, hotels and other listed establishments must pay nonexempt employees time and one-half the regular rate for hours over 40 a week. [^wi-overtime-forty-hours] [^wi-overtime-chapter-coverage] [^wi-overtime-exemptions-white-collar]
For employees of the state and local governments, the department's overtime pay, exemption and records provisions do not apply, and the federal Fair Labor Standards Act and the U.S. Department of Labor's regulations for state and local government employees govern instead. [^wi-public-employees-scope] [^wi-public-employees-federal-rules]
Overtime is generally measured by the week. [^wi-overtime-definition-weekly] Hospitals and other institutions primarily engaged in the care of the sick, the aged, the mentally ill or persons with developmental disabilities who reside on the premises may, by an agreement made before the work is performed, use a 14-day period instead, paying time and one-half for hours over eight in a day and over 80 in the 14 days. [^wi-hospital-fourteen-day-period] The Department of Workforce Development's street trades rule requires minors 16 and 17 years of age to receive overtime as provided under the employer's pay plan for other workers, and never less than time and one-half their regular rate for hours over 10 a day or over 40 a week, whichever is greater. [^wi-street-trades-minor-overtime] The department's general rule on minors' hours of labor contains no daily overtime requirement; its current text addresses only work-experience programs and minors in home-based private educational programs and charter schools. [^wi-minors-hours-rule-current-text] The overtime exemptions, which are interpreted consistently with the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, include persons whose primary duty consists of administrative, executive or professional work. [^wi-overtime-exemptions-white-collar]
Federal overtime law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The hours-of-labor statute directs the Department of Workforce Development (DWD) to classify, by rule, periods to be paid at regular rates and periods to be paid at least one and one-half times the regular rates. [^wi-hours-classification-authority] The DWD overtime rule defines overtime as hours in excess of 40 hours of work per week. [^wi-overtime-definition-weekly]

Employees employed in domestic service in a household by a household are not covered by the DWD overtime chapter. [^wi-overtime-chapter-coverage]

A separate statute permits compensatory time off in lieu of overtime compensation only for one class of employer that the hours-of-labor law defines by cross-reference, and only as the federal compensatory-time provision, as amended to April 15, 1986, permits. [^wi-public-compensatory-time]

For a tipped employee, the regular rate used for overtime includes the tip credit the employer takes and the cash wages, including commissions and certain bonuses, the employer pays. [^wi-tipped-regular-rate]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Wisconsin employers are not required to give employees 18 or older breaks or meal periods, though a Department of Workforce Development rule recommends a meal period of at least 30 minutes. [^wi-dwd-adult-breaks-not-required] [^wi-meal-period-recommended]
When a Wisconsin employer authorizes rest periods or breaks of less than 30 consecutive minutes per shift, they count as work time with no deduction from wages. [^wi-short-breaks-work-time] An on-duty meal period, one without at least 30 minutes free from work or in which the employee is not free to leave the premises, must be paid as work time. [^wi-on-duty-meal-paid]

The paid on-duty meal rule belongs to a chapter of Department of Workforce Development rules whose coverage is limited, as the [overtime](#overtime) answer explains, to employees of listed establishments, including restaurants and hotels, and of state and local government, and excludes household domestic service employees. [^wi-breaks-chapter-coverage]

The Department of Workforce Development recommends that the meal period fall reasonably close to 6:00 a.m., 12:00 noon, 6:00 p.m. or 12:00 midnight or near the middle of a shift, and states that shifts of more than 6 consecutive hours without a meal period should be avoided. [^wi-meal-period-recommended] That guidance is phrased as a recommendation rather than a requirement. [^wi-meal-period-recommended]

An employee who is required to perform any duties, active or inactive, while eating is not relieved from duty. [^wi-meal-duties-not-relieved]

Minors are treated differently: no minor under 18 may be employed or permitted to work more than 6 consecutive hours without a meal period. [^wi-minor-meal-period] The Department of Workforce Development explains that a worker under 18 must receive a 30-minute duty-free meal period for every six consecutive hours of work, while other breaks, including shorter breaks, are not required. [^wi-dwd-minor-meal-period]

An employer that owns or operates a factory or mercantile establishment in Wisconsin must allow each covered employee at least 24 consecutive hours of rest in every 7 consecutive days, except for persons the statute lists and in emergencies such as a machinery breakdown that requires immediate experienced labor not otherwise available. [^wi-day-of-rest-factories] The Department of Workforce Development applies the rule as one day off of 24 consecutive hours per calendar week, Sunday through Saturday, unless an exception applies. [^wi-dwd-day-of-rest-calendar-week] Under that reading the law does not require rest every seven days, so an employer may schedule work for 12 consecutive days within a two-week period if the days of rest fall on the first and last days of that period. [^wi-dwd-day-of-rest-twelve-days]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** A Wisconsin employer must pay an employee covered by the wage payment chapter who quits or is discharged in full by the employee's regular payday or the pay-frequency deadline, whichever is earlier, subject to two further exclusions and a faster deadline after certain business closings. [^wi-final-pay-next-payday] [^wi-final-pay-covered-employee]
The rule excludes a sales agent employed on a commission basis and an employee with a written contract for a definite period, and when a separation results from the employer merging, liquidating or otherwise disposing of the business, ceasing operations in whole or in part, or relocating, all unpaid wages are due within 24 hours. [^wi-final-pay-next-payday] [^wi-final-pay-business-closing] Late wages can draw court-ordered increased wages of up to 50 or 100 percent of the unpaid amount, depending on when the action is commenced. [^wi-final-pay-increased-wages-early] [^wi-final-pay-increased-wages-late]
The chapter's definition of employee excludes corporate officers and directors, limited liability company members and managers, partners, sole proprietors, independent contractors, and persons employed in a managerial, executive or commissioned sales capacity or with access to confidential matters involving the employer-employee relationship. [^wi-final-pay-covered-employee]

When an employee dies with wages due, the employer must pay the full amount on demand to the spouse, domestic partner, children or other dependent living with the employee at the time of death. [^wi-final-pay-employee-death]

In a wage claim action an employee commences before the Department of Workforce Development has completed its investigation and settlement attempts, a circuit court may order increased wages of not more than 50 percent of the wages due and unpaid. [^wi-final-pay-increased-wages-early] In an action commenced after the department completes them, the court may order increased wages of not more than 100 percent. [^wi-final-pay-increased-wages-late]

The department may compromise and settle a wage claim filed with it [^wi-final-pay-department-settlement], and when the employer does not agree to settle, the department may refer the claim to a district attorney or the Department of Justice for a circuit court action to collect the unpaid wages plus increased wages. [^wi-final-pay-department-referral]

An employer that, having the ability to pay, fails to pay wages due or falsely denies them with intent to secure a discount or to annoy, harass, oppress, hinder or defraud the employee may be fined not more than $500, imprisoned not more than 90 days, or both. [^wi-final-pay-criminal-penalty]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Unless an exception such as a valid collective bargaining agreement applies, Wisconsin employers must pay wages at least monthly, covering earnings to a day not more than 31 days before payment, and logging and farm labor wages at least quarterly. [^wi-pay-frequency-monthly] [^wi-pay-frequency-bargaining-exception] [^wi-pay-frequency-logging-farm]
An employee who is absent at the payment time, or is not paid then for any other reason, must be paid at any time upon 6 days' demand. [^wi-pay-absent-employee-demand]

The frequency requirement does not apply to five listed groups: employees covered by a valid collective bargaining agreement that sets a different payment frequency; school district, cooperative educational service agency and private school employees who voluntarily request payment over a 12-month period for services performed during the school year, unless a valid collective bargaining agreement covering the private school employees precludes that payment method; University of Wisconsin System employees other than university staff; employees who receive compensatory time off in lieu of overtime compensation; and certain part-time fire fighters and emergency medical services practitioners who are paid at regular intervals but no less often than annually. [^wi-pay-frequency-bargaining-exception]

Except as the frequency rule itself provides, no employer may secure an exemption from the wage payment timing statute by special contract with employees or by any other means. [^wi-pay-no-contract-exemption]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** A Wisconsin employer must state on each paycheck, pay envelope or accompanying paper the hours worked, the rate of pay, and the amount of and reason for each deduction other than employee-requested personal deductions. [^wi-pay-statement-contents]
A separate statute requires the employer to state the amount of and reason for each deduction, except miscellaneous deductions the individual employee requested for personal reasons. [^wi-statute-deduction-listing]

An employer may not deduct from an employee's wages for defective or faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property, or damage to property unless the employee authorizes it in writing after the incident happens and before any deduction is taken [^wi-dwd-deduction-after-incident], the employer and a representative the employee designates determine that the employee's negligence, carelessness, or willful and intentional conduct caused it, or a court finds the employee guilty or liable for that conduct. [^wi-faulty-workmanship-deduction-bar] An employer that makes such a deduction or takes such a credit outside those terms is liable for twice the amount in a civil action brought by the employee. [^wi-unauthorized-deduction-double-liability]

Meal deductions may be made only for bona fide meals consistent with the employee's work shift, and no deduction or credit is allowed for meals not eaten except where weekly room and board is provided and accepted. [^wi-meal-deduction-bona-fide] When the employer furnishes board or lodging under the Department of Workforce Development's meal and lodging rule and the employee accepts and receives it, the allowance for employees other than opportunity and minor employees may not exceed $58.00 per week or $8.30 per day for lodging and $87.00 per week or $4.15 per meal for meals, and opportunity employees have lower amounts. [^wi-board-lodging-allowance]

Each employer must keep records of the names and addresses of student learners and employees and the hours and wages of each, with an exception for hours records of certain overtime-exempt employees not paid on an hourly basis. [^wi-minimum-wage-records] Except as another provision of the department's rules provides, each employer must keep permanent records for at least 3 years, available for department inspection, showing each employee's name, address, hours and wages. [^wi-payroll-records-three-years]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** The Department of Workforce Development's Bureau of Labor Standards uses a six-part test, sometimes called the economic realities test, to decide whether a Wisconsin worker is economically dependent on the employer. [^wi-dwd-six-part-test]
Not all or even a majority of the six parts need be met, and the decision rests on all the circumstances of the relationship, whatever the parties call it. [^wi-dwd-test-all-circumstances] [^wi-dwd-six-part-test]
Wisconsin's minimum wage and wage payment statutes define employee and employer without themselves stating a multi-factor test, and the wage payment chapter excludes independent contractors from its definition of employee. [^wi-minimum-wage-employee-definition] [^wi-wage-payment-employee-definition] [^wi-minimum-wage-employer-definition] [^wi-wage-payment-employer-definition]
The multi-condition tests in Wisconsin statutes belong to worker's compensation and unemployment insurance, and each governs only its own law. [^wi-workers-comp-contractor-default] [^wi-unemployment-control-test]

The department treats the degree of control exercised by the employer as the most important part of the six-part test. [^wi-dwd-control-most-important]

For minimum wage purposes, an employee is every individual who receives or is entitled to compensation for labor performed for any employer [^wi-minimum-wage-employee-definition], and an employer includes every person having control or direction of any person employed at any labor or responsible directly or indirectly for another's wages. [^wi-minimum-wage-employer-definition]

The wage payment chapter defines an employee as any person employed by an employer but excludes independent contractors, corporate officers and directors, limited liability company members and managers, partners, sole proprietors, and persons employed in managerial, executive, commissioned sales or confidential capacities. [^wi-wage-payment-employee-definition] It defines an employer as any person engaged in any activity, enterprise or business employing one or more persons within the state, including the state, its political subdivisions and nonprofit organizations. [^wi-wage-payment-employer-definition]

Under Wisconsin's worker's compensation law, an independent contractor performing service in the course of an employer's trade, business, profession or occupation is that employer's employee for worker's compensation purposes, subject to the exceptions in the next paragraphs of the same subsection. [^wi-workers-comp-contractor-default] Under one of those exceptions, a contractor who meets all nine listed conditions is not an employee for worker's compensation purposes; the conditions include a separate business, contracts for specific amounts, the main expenses borne by the contractor, and the chance of profit or loss. [^wi-workers-comp-contractor-conditions]

Under Wisconsin's unemployment insurance law, an individual working for an employing unit other than a government unit or nonprofit organization, in a capacity other than logger or trucker, is outside the definition of employee only if the employing unit shows, by contract and in fact, both that the services are performed free from its control or direction and that the individual meets 6 or more of nine listed conditions. [^wi-unemployment-control-test] [^wi-unemployment-six-conditions] The listed conditions include holding out as being in business, operating under multiple contracts, and not being economically dependent on a particular employing unit. [^wi-unemployment-six-conditions]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** A Wisconsin employer may pay a tipped employee a cash wage of $2.33 per hour, or $2.13 for an opportunity employee, only if tips plus wages reach the minimum rate and payroll records prove it. [^wi-tipped-wage-payroll-condition]
To take the credit, the employer must have a tip declaration signed by the tipped employee each pay period and payroll records showing required social security or taxes withheld, and without them no tip credit is allowed. [^wi-tip-declaration-required]

Department of Workforce Development rules must let an employer require a tipped employee to acknowledge the counting of tips by an electronic signature or other electronic means that uniquely identifies the employee. [^wi-tip-electronic-signature]

Where waiters or waitresses give part of their tips to bus persons, the amounts each keeps count as that individual's tips. [^wi-tip-splitting-rule] A compulsory service charge, such as 15 percent of the bill, is not a tip unless the employer distributes it to employees. [^wi-service-charge-not-tip] Under an agreement requiring tips to be turned over or credited to the employer as part of its gross receipts, the employer must pay the full minimum hourly wage. [^wi-tips-turned-over-employer]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A Wisconsin employee may sue an employer in any court of competent jurisdiction for unpaid wages and increased wages without first filing a wage claim with the Department of Workforce Development. [^wi-private-right-of-action] [^wi-no-department-filing-first]
Actions to recover unpaid wages must generally be commenced within 2 years after the cause of action accrues. [^wi-wage-action-two-years]

The Department of Workforce Development investigates and attempts to adjust wage claims, and it may receive and investigate a claim filed no later than 2 years after the wages are due. [^wi-department-wage-claims] When the department finds a wage claim valid, it may instruct the employer to audit its payroll records for other wage claims of the same type, and for later valid claims of that type the department must require increased wages of not more than 50 percent of the unpaid wages unless the employer shows extreme hardship. [^wi-department-audit-increased-wages] An action to recover wages under the department's wage-claim collection statute must be commenced within 2 years after the claim is filed with the department. [^wi-department-claim-action-two-years] Any person may register a complaint with the department that an employee is paid less than the minimum wage, and the department must investigate and take all proceedings necessary to enforce payment. [^wi-minimum-wage-complaints]

A circuit court may award increased wages of not more than 50 percent of the unpaid wages in an action commenced before the department completes its investigation and settlement attempts [^wi-enforcement-increased-wages-early], or not more than 100 percent in an action commenced after it completes them. [^wi-enforcement-increased-wages-late] In such a proceeding the court may allow the prevailing party a reasonable sum for expenses in addition to all other costs. [^wi-wage-claim-costs-expenses]

The department, or an employee who sues under the wage payment law, has a lien on all of the employer's real and personal property in Wisconsin for the full amount of the wage claim or wage deficiency. [^wi-wage-claim-lien]

Wisconsin's fair employment law protection against discharge applies to discharge and other discriminatory acts arising in connection with a proceeding under the wage payment timing statute. [^wi-wage-payment-retaliation] An employer that discharges or discriminates against an employee for testifying, or for being expected to testify, in a minimum wage enforcement proceeding may be fined $25 for each offense. [^wi-witness-intimidation-fine]

Stat. § 102.07 (independent contractor conditions)** — "An independent contractor is not an employee of an employer for whom the independent contractor performs work or services if the independent contractor meets all of the following conditions: 1. Maintains a separate business with his or her own office, equipment, materials and other facilities. 2. Holds or has applied for a federal employer identification number with the federal internal revenue service or has filed business or self-employment income tax returns with the federal internal revenue service based on that work or service in the previous year. 3. Operates under contracts to perform specific services or work for specific amounts of money and under which the independent contractor controls the means of performing the services or work. 4. Incurs the main expenses related to the service or work that he or she performs under contract. 5. Is responsible for the satisfactory completion of work or services that he or she contracts to perform and is liable for a failure to complete the work or service. 6. Receives compensation for work or service performed under a contract on a commission or per job or competitive bid basis and not on any other basis. 7. May realize a profit or suffer a loss under contracts to perform work or service. 8. Has continuing or recurring business liabilities or obligations. 9. The success or failure of the independent contractor’s business depends on the relationship of business receipts to expenditures." *Wis. Stat. § 102.07(8)(b)* <https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/102/07>

[^wi-unemployment-six-conditions]: **Wis. Stat. § 108.02 (unemployment insurance conditions)** — "2. The individual meets 6 or more of the following conditions: a. The individual operates under multiple contracts with one or more employing units to perform specific services. d. [^wi-tipped-wage-payroll-condition]: **Wis. Stat. § 104.035 (tipped employees)** — "Except as provided in subs. (4) to (8), if an employer of a tipped employee establishes by the employer’s payroll records that, when adding the tips received by the tipped employee in a week to the wages paid to the tipped employee in that week, the tipped employee receives not less than the applicable minimum wage specified in sub. (1), (2), or (2m), the minimum wage for the tipped employee is as follows: 1. For wages earned by a tipped employee who is not an opportunity employee, $2.33 per hour. 2. For wages earned by a tipped employee who is an opportunity employee, $2.13 per hour." *Wis. Stat. § 104.035(3)(a)* <https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/104/035>

[^wi-tip-declaration-required]: **Wis. Admin. Code § DWD 272.03 (tip credit burden of proof)** — "When the employer elects to take tip credit the employer must have a tip declaration signed by the tipped employee each pay period and show on the payroll records that any required social security or taxes have been withheld each pay period to show that when adding the tips received to the wages paid by the employer, no less than the minimum rate was received by the employee. When the employer’s time and payroll records do not contain these requirements, no tip credit shall be allowed." *Wis. Admin. [^wi-department-audit-increased-wages]: **Wis. For any valid wage claim that is filed against an employer after the department has instructed the employer to audit his or her payroll records under this paragraph and that is of the same type as the wage claim that prompted the audit instruction and for any valid wage claim that is discovered as a result of the department’s audit under this paragraph and that is of the same type as the wage claim that prompted the audit instruction, the department shall require the employer to pay, in addition to the amount of wages due and unpaid, increased wages of not more than 50 percent of the amount of wages due and unpaid, unless the employer shows the department that payment of the increased wages would cause extreme hardship." *Wis.

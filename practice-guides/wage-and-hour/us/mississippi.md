# Wage and Hour Law in Mississippi[^about]

How Mississippi, which has no state minimum wage or overtime law, relies on the federal Fair Labor Standards Act for the $7.25 wage floor, weekly overtime, tip credit and wage claims, bars counties and cities from setting a local minimum wage, requires twice-monthly pay only for large manufacturers and public service corporations, has no final-pay statute and no general requirement to provide meal or rest breaks, and gives employees a state equal pay claim.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Mississippi has no state minimum wage law. Under federal law, a covered, nonexempt employee generally must be paid at least $7.25 an hour. [^ms-dol-no-state-minimum-wage] [^ms-federal-minimum-wage-rate] The federal rate applies to an employee who, in any workweek, is engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce, or who is employed in an enterprise engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce. [^ms-federal-minimum-wage-rate] Mississippi law also bars every county, board of supervisors, municipality and municipal governing authority from establishing a mandatory minimum living wage rate that would regulate how a private employer pays its employees, so no Mississippi county or city can require private employers to pay more than the federal rate. [^ms-local-minimum-wage-preemption]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The same 2013 statute bars counties and cities from setting a minimum number of vacation or sick days, whether paid or unpaid, for private employers. [^ms-local-minimum-wage-preemption] The statute does not impede or supersede a municipality's authority under Sections 17-21-1, 17-21-5 and 17-21-7. [^ms-local-preemption-municipal-savings]

An employee who is outside federal coverage has no Mississippi minimum wage to fall back on. [^ms-dol-no-state-minimum-wage] Federal law allows an employer to pay an employee under age 20 not less than $4.25 an hour during the first 90 consecutive calendar days of employment. [^ms-federal-youth-wage] [^ms-federal-youth-wage-age] An employer may not displace other employees, including by reducing their hours, wages or benefits, to hire workers at that wage. [^ms-federal-youth-wage-displacement]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** A covered, nonexempt Mississippi employee generally earns overtime of at least one and one-half times the regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek under federal law. [^ms-federal-overtime-forty-hours] The federal rule counts hours by the workweek, not by the day, and no Mississippi statute adds daily overtime or double time. [^ms-federal-overtime-forty-hours] One federal alternative uses a daily threshold: a hospital, or an institution primarily engaged in caring for sick, aged, or mentally ill or disabled people who reside on its premises, may, by agreement with the employee before the work is performed, use a 14-day work period if it pays time and a half for work over eight hours in a workday and over 80 hours in the period. [^ms-federal-hospital-fourteen-day]

A federal executive, administrative or professional exemption generally requires a salary of at least $684 a week. [^ms-federal-white-collar-salary-684] An employer may make deductions from an exempt employee's salary only in the circumstances the federal salary-basis rule allows, and an exempt employee generally must receive the full salary for any week in which the employee performs any work. [^ms-federal-salary-basis-full-week]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** No Mississippi statute requires meal or rest breaks for adult private-sector employees. Mississippi is also not among the jurisdictions the U.S. Department of Labor lists as having separate meal-period rules for minors. [^ms-dol-meal-period-minor-list] When an employer chooses to give breaks, federal regulations govern whether they are paid. Short rest periods of about 5 to 20 minutes are customarily paid and must be counted as hours worked. [^ms-federal-rest-periods-paid] A bona fide meal period is not worktime, but only if the employee is completely relieved from duty for the purpose of eating regular meals, and 30 minutes or more is ordinarily long enough. [^ms-federal-meal-not-worktime] [^ms-federal-meal-relieved]

An employee who must perform any duties while eating, such as an office worker required to eat at a desk, is working while eating. [^ms-federal-meal-duties-while-eating]

Federal law generally requires an employer to provide a covered employee reasonable break time, and a private place other than a bathroom, for an employee to express breast milk for one year after the child's birth. [^ms-federal-lactation-break] The break time need not be paid unless other law requires it, but it counts as hours worked if the employee is not completely relieved from duty during the break. [^ms-federal-lactation-unpaid] [^ms-federal-lactation-hours-worked] An employer with fewer than 50 employees is exempt if compliance would impose an undue hardship, and other federal exemptions are covered in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us). [^ms-federal-lactation-small-employer] Mississippi law also bars any employer from prohibiting an employee from expressing breast milk during a meal period or other break the employer provides. [^ms-state-lactation-during-breaks]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** No Mississippi statute sets a deadline for final pay that is triggered by an employee quitting or being fired, or a penalty aimed specifically at late final wages. For a manufacturer employing 50 or more employees and employing public labor, or a public service corporation, the regular payment schedule in Miss. Code Ann. § 71-1-35 still governs wages earned before separation by employees other than those employed in a bona fide executive, administrative or professional capacity, including its requirement to pay at least every two weeks or twice a month. [^ms-71-1-35-text-fp] The U.S. Department of Labor notes that federal law does not require an employer to give a former employee the final paycheck immediately. [^ms-dol-last-paycheck] The federal Fair Labor Standards Act still applies to the final paycheck. Overtime earned in a workweek generally must be paid on the regular payday for the period in which that workweek ends, and payment may not be delayed beyond the next payday after the amount can be computed. [^ms-federal-overtime-regular-payday] [^ms-federal-overtime-payment-delay] An employer that fails to pay federal minimum wages or overtime generally owes the unpaid amount plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, although a court may reduce or deny liquidated damages if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds for believing it was not violating the law. [^ms-federal-unpaid-wages-liquidated-damages] [^ms-federal-good-faith-fp]

The Department of Labor directs an employee whose last regular payday has passed without payment to contact its Wage and Hour Division or the state labor department. [^ms-dol-last-paycheck]

Federal law limits what an employer may take out of a final paycheck. Wages count as paid only if they are paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear, and the wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back any part of the wage to the employer. [^ms-federal-free-and-clear] [^ms-federal-kickback] Deductions for tools and similar items that are not board, lodging or other facilities are illegal to the extent they reduce the employee's wages in a non-overtime workweek below the federal minimum. [^ms-federal-tool-deductions] In an overtime workweek, bona fide deductions made under the parties' agreement that do not evade the overtime requirements may be taken on the same basis as in other weeks, but deductions for tools or other items that are not facilities are illegal to the extent they exceed what could have been deducted had the employee worked only the maximum straight-time hours, while deductions for board, lodging or other facilities are limited only to their reasonable cost. [^ms-federal-overtime-deductions-fp] No Mississippi statute generally regulates wage deductions, although Mississippi bars an employer from deducting its unemployment insurance contributions from employees' wages. [^ms-ui-contributions-not-deducted-final-pay]

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** No Mississippi statute imposes a generally applicable pay-frequency requirement on private employers. Mississippi's pay-frequency statute, Miss. Code Ann. § 71-1-35, applies to every entity engaged in manufacturing in the state that employs 50 or more employees and employs public labor, and to every public service corporation doing business in the state, and requires those employers to pay at least once every two weeks or twice during each calendar month. [^ms-dol-payday-manufacturing] [^ms-71-1-35-text] The statute does not cover employees in a bona fide executive, administrative or professional capacity, and each payment must include everything due for work performed up to no more than 10 days before payment, or 15 days for a public service corporation. [^ms-71-1-35-text] For an employee covered by federal law, overtime earned in a workweek generally must be paid on the regular payday for the period in which that workweek ends. [^ms-federal-overtime-regular-payday-frequency]

The federal Fair Labor Standards Act itself does not require overtime to be paid weekly. [^ms-federal-overtime-not-weekly]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** No Mississippi statute requires a private employer to give employees a pay statement or generally regulates wage deductions. The federal Fair Labor Standards Act contains the deduction limits that apply, and under it wages count as paid only if they are paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear. [^ms-federal-free-and-clear-statements] Under federal law, a deduction for tools or similar items that are not board, lodging or other facilities is illegal to the extent it cuts the employee's wages below the federal minimum in a non-overtime workweek. [^ms-federal-tool-deductions-statements] In an overtime workweek, bona fide deductions made under the parties' agreement that do not evade the overtime requirements may be taken on the same basis as in other weeks, but deductions for tools or other items that are not facilities are illegal to the extent they exceed what could have been deducted had the employee worked only the maximum straight-time hours, while deductions for board, lodging or other facilities are limited only to their reasonable cost. [^ms-federal-overtime-deductions] One narrow Mississippi rule applies: an employer's unemployment insurance contributions may not be deducted, in whole or in part, from its employees' wages. [^ms-ui-contributions-not-deducted] In a non-overtime workweek, a deduction for board, lodging or other facilities may reduce the cash wage below the minimum wage only if the price charged does not exceed the reasonable cost of the facilities. [^ms-federal-facilities-reasonable-cost]

A deduction for an employee's own benefit, such as a payment to a third party the employee has authorized, is treated differently. Under the federal regulation, an amount the employer pays to a third party for the employee's benefit under the employee's voluntary assignment or order may be deducted and counts as payment to the employee, provided neither the employer nor anyone acting for it derives any profit or benefit from the transaction. [^ms-federal-third-party-payments]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** For federal minimum wage and overtime purposes, a Mississippi worker's status as an employee or independent contractor turns on the economic-realities test. [^ms-federal-economic-realities] That test asks whether the worker is economically dependent on the potential employer for work or is in business for themself. [^ms-federal-economic-realities] The federal law defines employ to include to suffer or permit to work. [^ms-federal-suffer-or-permit] For state unemployment insurance, Mississippi uses a different test: services performed for wages are employment unless the worker is shown to be free from control and direction both under the contract and in fact, and the relationship is judged under common-law master-and-servant principles. [^ms-ui-control-test]

The Mississippi Supreme Court decides employee status under that test case by case, describing the common-law inquiry as a flexible, functional test. [^ms-ui-common-law-factors] Control is among the most prominent factors. [^ms-ui-control-most-prominent] In 2022, the court reinstated the Mississippi Department of Employment Security's decision that a trucking company had the right to control a contract driver's work, so that he was an employee for unemployment purposes, even though he had signed an agreement stating he was self-employed and was to receive an IRS Form 1099. [^ms-ui-dover-trucking-self-employment-agreement] [^ms-ui-dover-trucking-holding]

The Department of Employment Security states that it, not the employer or the worker, has the authority to decide whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor for unemployment purposes. [^ms-mdes-classification-authority]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** No Mississippi statute addresses tip credits, so a covered employer may take the federal tip credit, subject to its conditions. [^ms-federal-tip-credit-formula] The employer must first inform the employee of the federal tip-credit provisions, and the employee must keep all tips, except that tips may be pooled among employees who customarily and regularly receive tips. [^ms-federal-tip-credit-notice] Under the federal formula, a tipped employee's cash wage must be at least $2.13 an hour, and the tip credit makes up the difference between that cash wage and the $7.25 minimum. [^ms-federal-tip-credit-formula] [^ms-federal-minimum-wage-rate-tips] The tip credit may not exceed the tips the employee actually receives, and if tips fall short, the employer must pay the balance so the employee receives at least the minimum wage. [^ms-federal-tip-credit-cap] [^ms-federal-tip-shortfall]

A tipped employee is an employee engaged in an occupation in which the employee customarily and regularly receives more than $30 a month in tips. [^ms-federal-tipped-employee-definition] Whether or not it takes a tip credit, an employer may not keep its employees' tips, including by letting managers or supervisors keep any portion. [^ms-federal-employer-may-not-keep-tips] An employer that keeps tips is liable for the tip credit taken and the tips kept, plus an equal amount as liquidated damages. [^ms-federal-tip-retention-damages]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Unpaid minimum wages and overtime in Mississippi are enforced under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, because Mississippi has no state minimum wage or overtime law. [^ms-federal-unpaid-wages-liquidated-damages-enforcement] An employer that violates the federal minimum wage or overtime rules generally owes the unpaid amount plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, although a court may reduce or deny liquidated damages if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds for believing it was not violating the law. [^ms-federal-unpaid-wages-liquidated-damages-enforcement] [^ms-federal-good-faith-enf] One or more employees may sue in any federal or state court of competent jurisdiction, for themselves and other employees similarly situated, and the court must award a reasonable attorney's fee and costs to a successful plaintiff. [^ms-federal-employee-action] [^ms-federal-attorney-fees] A federal wage claim must be filed within two years after the cause of action accrued, or three years for a willful violation. [^ms-federal-limitations]

In a federal collective action, no employee becomes a party plaintiff without filing written consent in the court. [^ms-federal-written-consent] The U.S. Secretary of Labor may also sue to recover unpaid minimum wages or overtime and an equal amount as liquidated damages, and a person who repeatedly or willfully violates the minimum wage or overtime rules faces a civil penalty. [^ms-federal-secretary-action] [^ms-federal-civil-penalty]

Mississippi does provide a state claim for sex-based pay discrimination. The Mississippi Equal Pay for Equal Work Act, effective July 1, 2022, bars an employer from paying an employee less than an employee of the opposite sex in the same establishment for equal work requiring equal skill, education, effort and responsibility under similar working conditions, unless the difference rests on seniority, merit, quantity or quality of production, or any other factor other than sex. [^ms-equal-pay-rule] [^ms-equal-pay-effective-date] The Act covers employers with five or more employees, and employees employed to work 40 or more hours a week. [^ms-equal-pay-definitions] An employee may sue in the circuit court of the county where the cause of action occurred, and a prevailing employee is awarded reasonable attorney's fees, prejudgment interest, back pay and costs. [^ms-equal-pay-remedies] The action must be brought within two years from the day the employee knew or should have known of the violation. An employee who brings a claim under the federal Equal Pay Act of 1963 may not also maintain an action under the state Act, an employee seeking state relief must first waive any right to federal Equal Pay Act relief, and a state action is dismissed with prejudice if the employee later brings a federal Equal Pay Act claim. [^ms-equal-pay-limitations] The Act also bars an employer from retaliating against an employee for invoking or assisting in its enforcement. [^ms-equal-pay-retaliation]

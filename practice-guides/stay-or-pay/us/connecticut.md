# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Connecticut[^about]

Connecticut voids an employment promissory note, including a training-repayment note, that an employer requires as a condition of employment, and from October 1, 2026 that rule reaches employers of every size; outside the statute's exceptions, a repayment term also faces the liquidated-damages test, and section 31-71e limits taking a repayment out of wages.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Connecticut? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Connecticut voids an employment promissory note that an employer requires an employee or prospective employee to sign as a condition of employment, subject to section 31-51r's statutory exceptions [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-note-void][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-exceptions]. The exceptions cover agreements to repay sums advanced, to pay for property the employer sold or leased to the employee, educational personnel's sabbatical terms, and programs agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-exceptions]. An employment promissory note is an instrument or agreement requiring the employee to pay the employer a sum of money if the employee leaves before a stated period, including one that describes the payment as reimbursement for training previously provided [^ct-sop-31-51r-definition][^ct-sop-31-51r-training-included]. When the note is part of a larger employment agreement, the rest of the agreement survives [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-severable].
Public Act 26-12 rewrote section 31-51r effective October 1, 2026 [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-effective]. Before that date the section defined an employer as one with twenty-six or more employees [^ct-sop-31-51r-old-employer]; the act struck that limit, so the rule now applies to any employer engaged in business, including the state and its political subdivisions [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-employer]. The rewritten definition covers instruments executed on or after October 1, 2026 [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-executed-date]. In our review we found no decision on how the rewritten section applies to a note signed before that date.
A second 2026 act, Public Act 26-122, also rewrote section 31-51r, effective from passage, and excluded from the definition a note for full or partial repayment of a federal H-1B visa fee that the employer paid on the employee's behalf [^ct-sop-pa-26-122-effective][^ct-sop-pa-26-122-h1b]. When two acts of the same session amend the same section, each amendment takes effect except where they irreconcilably conflict, in which case the act passed last in the second house prevails [^ct-sop-2-30b-multiple-amendments]. Public Act 26-122 kept the twenty-six-employee threshold and the 1985 execution-date cutoff in the definition, both of which Public Act 26-12 changed [^ct-sop-pa-26-122-effective][^ct-sop-pa-26-122-1985][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-employer][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-executed-date]. When we checked, no compiled text reconciling the two acts had been published. As an inference under section 2-30b, the conservative reading treats both the H-1B exclusion and the removal of the employee threshold as in force, because neither change contradicts the other [^ct-sop-2-30b-multiple-amendments][^ct-sop-pa-26-122-h1b][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-employer].
The H-1B exclusion only takes such a note outside section 31-51r. Federal H-1B rules separately bar an employer from requiring an H-1B worker to pay a penalty for leaving before an agreed date, and from receiving any part of the additional petition filing fee [^ct-sop-fed-655-731-penalty][^ct-sop-fed-655-731-fee].
Section 31-51r does not prohibit or void an agreement requiring the employee to repay sums advanced, to pay for property the employer sold or leased to the employee, requiring educational personnel to comply with the terms of a sabbatical leave, or entered into as part of a program agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-exceptions]. In our review we found no reported Connecticut appellate decision interpreting section 31-51r.
A term outside section 31-51r that fixes the damages owed for early departure must satisfy Connecticut's liquidated-damages test rather than impose a penalty. In *Berger v. Shanahan*, the Connecticut Supreme Court held that a contract term imposing a penalty for breach is invalid, and that a term fixing damages is enforceable when the expected damage was uncertain or difficult to prove, the parties intended to liquidate damages in advance, and the amount was not greatly disproportionate to the loss the parties could foresee [^ct-sop-berger-penalty-invalid][^ct-sop-berger-three-conditions]. Applying that test, the court enforced a salesman's promise to refund the $100 weekly allowance paid during his first six months if he left within a year without just cause [^ct-sop-berger-allowance][^ct-sop-berger-refund-term][^ct-sop-berger-uncertain-damage][^ct-sop-berger-enforceable]. *Berger* was decided in 1955, before section 31-51r was enacted, so a similar term that an employer now requires as a condition of employment, and that does not fit an exception, would be void under the statute.

"The execution of an employment promissory note as a condition of employment is against public policy and any such note shall be void."[^ct-sop-pa-26-12-note-void]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Making a training or retention repayment note part of the offer package, so that the candidate must sign it to take the job, risks the note being void [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-note-void-caution].

## Can a Connecticut employer claw back pay already received? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** Connecticut bars an employer from demanding repayment of wages on the understanding that repayment is necessary to secure or continue employment [^ct-sop-31-73-refund-bar]. Its wage payment sections define wages as compensation for labor or services rendered, however calculated [^ct-sop-31-71a-wages]. In *Weems v. Citigroup Inc.*, former employees argued that the forfeiture provisions of plans that paid part of their discretionary bonuses in restricted stock violated Connecticut's wage statutes, and the Connecticut Supreme Court held that the bonuses were discretionary merit bonuses not subject to those statutes [^ct-sop-weems-forfeiture-claim][^ct-sop-weems-bonus-plan][^ct-sop-weems-discretionary-bonus]. As an inference from *Weems*, a forfeiture condition attached to a discretionary bonus award of the kind addressed there is on firmer ground than demanding repayment of pay already earned as wages [^ct-sop-weems-discretionary-bonus][^ct-sop-31-73-refund-bar].
Section 31-73 separately bars an employer from demanding, requesting or receiving any refund of wages or other sum of money on the representation or understanding that it is necessary to secure or continue in employment [^ct-sop-31-73-refund-bar]. The same section makes an employee's repayment of wages received prima facie evidence of a violation unless the repayment pays a debt evidenced by an instrument in writing [^ct-sop-31-73-prima-facie].
A written promise to repay a cash bonus if the employee leaves early can itself be an employment promissory note, because the definition reaches any agreement requiring a payment to the employer on leaving before a stated period [^ct-sop-31-51r-definition-clawback]. Such a note is void if the employer required it as a condition of employment, unless an exception applies, such as an agreement to repay sums advanced [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-void-clawback][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-advance-exception]. In our review we found no Connecticut decision on whether a sign-on or retention bonus counts as a sum advanced, so whether such a bonus can be recovered is unsettled.

"We conclude that the bonuses, including those paid to branch managers, are discretionary merit bonuses not subject to Connecticut’s wage statutes."[^ct-sop-weems-discretionary-bonus]

## Can repayment come out of a Connecticut final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Connecticut employer may not withhold or divert any portion of an employee's wages, including final wages, unless state or federal law requires or empowers it, the employee has given written authorization on a form approved by the Labor Commissioner, or another listed exception applies [^ct-sop-31-71e-withholding]. The other exceptions cover written authorization for medical care deductions without financial benefit to the employer, automatic-enrollment retirement contributions and another state's required income tax withholding [^ct-sop-31-71e-withholding]. A contractual training or bonus repayment does not by itself authorize withholding under section 31-71e [^ct-sop-31-71e-withholding].
In *Weems*, the Connecticut Supreme Court held that an employer's failure to obtain the department's approval of the authorization form did not invalidate payroll deductions to which the employees had knowingly and voluntarily consented, or give the employees a right of action [^ct-sop-weems-form-approval]. The safer reading for an employer is still to use an approved form, because section 31-71e lists that form as the condition.
Final wages are due by the next regular payday when the employee quits, and by the next business day when the employer discharges the employee [^ct-sop-31-71c-quit][^ct-sop-31-71c-discharge]. In a dispute over the amount, the employer must pay the wages it concedes are due without condition and on time [^ct-sop-31-71d-conceded].
A deduction that recovers a cost of the employer's own business, such as training the employer requires for its work, can also run into federal wage law. Under the federal rule, wage requirements are not met when the employee kicks back the wage, directly or indirectly, to the employer or for the employer's benefit, and an employer-required cost borne by the employee violates the Fair Labor Standards Act in any workweek in which it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^ct-sop-fed-531-35-kickback][^ct-sop-fed-531-35-tools].

"No employer may withhold or divert any portion of an employee's wages unless (1) the employer is required or empowered to do so by state or federal law, or (2) the employer has written authorization from the employee for deductions on a form approved by the commissioner, or (3) the deductions are authorized by the employee, in writing, for medical, surgical or hospital care or service, without financial benefit to the employer and recorded in the employer's wage record book, or (4) the deductions are for contributions attributable to automatic enrollment, as defined in section 31-71j, in a retirement plan described in Section 401(k), 403(b), 408, 408A or 457 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986, or any subsequent corresponding internal revenue code of the United States, as from time to time amended, established by the employer, or in the Connecticut Retirement Security Program established pursuant to section 31-418, or (5) the employer is required under the law of another state to withhold income tax of such other state with respect to (A) employees performing services of the employer in such other state, or (B) employees residing in such other state."[^ct-sop-31-71e-withholding]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a training or bonus repayment against the last paycheck without the employee's knowing and voluntary written authorization for the deduction risks a wage claim for twice the withheld amount, with costs and attorney's fees, unless the employer proves a good faith belief that it complied with the law [^ct-sop-31-71e-caution][^ct-sop-31-72-caution].

## Are Connecticut training or tuition repayment terms treated differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Connecticut voids a training-repayment note that an employer requires as a condition of employment, subject to the statutory exceptions [^ct-sop-31-51r-training-definition][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-void][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-exceptions]. The exceptions cover agreements to repay sums advanced, to pay for property the employer sold or leased to the employee, educational personnel's sabbatical terms, and programs agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-exceptions]. Section 31-51r's definition of an employment promissory note includes an instrument that describes the payment owed on early departure as reimbursement for training previously provided to the employee [^ct-sop-31-51r-training-definition], and since October 1, 2026 any employer that requires an employee or prospective employee to sign such a note as a condition of employment makes it void [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-effective][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-void][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-employer].
The statute's exceptions still apply to training terms. An agreement to repay sums advanced, an educational employee's sabbatical terms, and a program agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative are not prohibited or void under section 31-51r [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-exceptions]. In our review we found no Connecticut decision on whether tuition the employer pays on the employee's behalf is a sum advanced, or on when a voluntary education program is required as a condition of employment, so both questions are open.
The statute does not name relocation costs. As an inference from its definition, a relocation repayment that becomes due when the employee leaves before a stated period is also an employment promissory note, and void when required as a condition of employment, unless a statutory exception applies, such as repayment of sums advanced or an agreement entered into as part of a collectively bargained program [^ct-sop-31-51r-relocation-definition][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-void][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-training-exceptions].

## What happens to a Connecticut employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Connecticut makes an employment promissory note required as a condition of employment void and unenforceable, subject to section 31-51r's exceptions [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-enforce-void][^ct-sop-pa-26-12-enforce-exceptions]. The exceptions cover agreements to repay sums advanced, to pay for property the employer sold or leased to the employee, educational personnel's sabbatical terms, and programs agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-enforce-exceptions]. The rest of the employment agreement stays in force [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-enforce-severable].
When an employer withholds a repayment from wages in violation of the wage payment sections, the employee may sue for twice the withheld wages with costs and reasonable attorney's fees, or for the wages alone with costs and fees if the employer proves a good faith belief that it complied with the law [^ct-sop-31-72-recovery]. An agreement between the employee and the employer to be paid other than as those sections require is no defense [^ct-sop-31-72-no-defense]. The Labor Commissioner may collect unpaid wages with interest and may sue for twice the unpaid wages [^ct-sop-31-72-commissioner-interest][^ct-sop-31-72-commissioner-double].
An action for periodically payable remuneration must be brought within two years after the right of action accrues, and filing a wage complaint with the Labor Commissioner tolls that period [^ct-sop-52-596-limit]. A violation of the refund-of-wages bar in section 31-73 is also a crime, punishable by a fine of up to one hundred dollars or up to thirty days in prison for a first offense [^ct-sop-31-73-penalty].

## What repayment structure is safest under Connecticut law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** The safest Connecticut structure does not make signing a repayment obligation a condition of getting or keeping the job, because section 31-51r voids an employment promissory note executed as a condition of employment [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-structure-void]. Where repayment is needed, the term is safest when it fits one of the statute's exceptions, such as a true advance of money the employee agrees to repay, or a program agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative [^ct-sop-pa-26-12-structure-exceptions].
A term that survives section 31-51r and fixes damages for early departure, rather than recovering an actual debt such as an advance, should still set an amount the employer can show was a reasonable forecast of its loss, because *Berger* enforces a fixed amount only when the damage was uncertain, the parties intended to liquidate it, and the amount was not greatly disproportionate to the foreseeable loss [^ct-sop-berger-structure-conditions].
Collecting the balance by separate demand is safer than withholding pay, because section 31-71e conditions a deduction on the employee's written authorization on a form approved by the Labor Commissioner [^ct-sop-31-71e-structure]. *Weems* held that missing departmental approval did not by itself invalidate deductions the employees knowingly and voluntarily authorized [^ct-sop-weems-structure-form], but a deduction without valid written consent has no such footing. A deduction that recovers a cost of the employer's own business also should not cut into required minimum or overtime wages [^ct-sop-fed-531-35-structure].

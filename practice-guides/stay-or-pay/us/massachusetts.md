# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Massachusetts[^about]

Massachusetts has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while the Wage Act separately limits collection through earned wages to a valid set-off for a clear and established debt.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Massachusetts? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Massachusetts enforces an agreed damages amount if, when the contract was made, actual damages were difficult to ascertain and the amount was a reasonable forecast of the damages expected from a breach [^ma-cummings-liquidated-damages-test]. Apart from section 24L's rules for forfeiture for competition agreements, discussed below, no Massachusetts statute bans or limits stay-or-pay, training-repayment or sign-on bonus repayment terms in private employment, so the conservative inference is that a court would test a fixed repayment amount under that liquidated damages rule [^ma-cummings-liquidated-damages-test].
In *Kelly v. Marx*, the Supreme Judicial Court declined to take a second look at the damages actually suffered and held that a liquidated damages clause will be enforced where, when the agreement was made, potential damages were difficult to determine and the clause was a reasonable forecast of the expected damages [^ma-kelly-formation-time-test]. In *NPS, LLC v. Minihane*, the court placed on the party resisting the clause the burden of showing that the amount is unreasonably and grossly disproportionate to the real damages from a breach, or unconscionably excessive [^ma-nps-challenger-burden]. Where damages are easily ascertainable and the agreed amount is grossly disproportionate or unconscionably excessive, the court awards no more than actual damages [^ma-nps-actual-damages-only].
The Massachusetts Noncompetition Agreement Act, chapter 149, section 24L, separates two kinds of forfeiture terms. A forfeiture agreement, as the Act defines it, imposes adverse financial consequences on a former employee because employment ended, regardless of whether the employee goes on to compete, and the Act expressly excludes forfeiture agreements from the noncompetition agreements it regulates [^ma-24l-forfeiture-agreement-definition][^ma-24l-forfeiture-agreement-excluded]. A forfeiture for competition agreement imposes those consequences if the employee engages in competitive activities, and the Act counts it as a noncompetition agreement [^ma-24l-forfeiture-for-competition-definition][^ma-miele-forfeiture-subset]. A repayment that is owed only if the employee joins a competitor must therefore meet the Act's minimum requirements to be valid and enforceable [^ma-24l-minimum-requirements], including support by a garden leave clause or other mutually agreed consideration specified in the agreement [^ma-24l-consideration-requirement]. In *Miele v. Foundation Medicine, Inc.*, the court held that a forfeiture clause triggered by breach of a nonsolicitation agreement is not a forfeiture for competition agreement under the Act [^ma-miele-nonsolicit-forfeiture]. The Act applies only to agreements entered into on or after October 1, 2018 [^ma-nuvasive-act-effective-date].

"...a liquidated damages clause in a purchase and sale agreement will be enforced where, at the time the agreement was made, potential damages were difficult to determine and the clause was a reasonable forecast of damages expected to occur in the event of a breach."[^ma-kelly-formation-time-test]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A competition-triggered repayment clause that omits section 24L's required safeguards, such as specified consideration, risks being unenforceable [^ma-24l-forfeiture-competition-caution][^ma-24l-validity-caution][^ma-24l-requirements-caution].

## Can a Massachusetts employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** A Massachusetts employer may not recapture wages the employee has already earned. In *Awuah v. Coverall North America, Inc.*, the Supreme Judicial Court held that chargebacks used to recapture wages already earned and paid were not a valid set-off because they corresponded to no clear and established debt owed by the employee [^ma-awuah-chargebacks-recapture], and that the employer was not free to withhold, much less recapture, earned wages [^ma-awuah-no-recapture]. The Wage Act bars an employer from exempting itself from its payment duties by a special contract with an employee or by any other means [^ma-s148-special-contract-clawback], and it applies to commissions once their amount, less allowable or authorized deductions, has been definitely determined and has become due and payable [^ma-s148-commissions-clawback].
Pay that is discretionary, or contingent on staying employed, may fall outside the Wage Act. In *Weems v. Citigroup Inc.*, the court held that discretionary restricted-stock bonus awards were not wages under the Act, noting that the full benefit came only if the employee remained with the company for the defined period after the award [^ma-weems-contingent-bonus][^ma-weems-not-wages]. In *Nunez v. Syncsort Inc.*, the court held that retention bonus payments conditioned on the employee staying until fixed dates were not wages but additional, contingent compensation outside the Wage Act [^ma-nunez-retention-not-wages]. That agreement also required the employee to return the entire retention bonus if he resigned before a retention date or was terminated for cause [^ma-nunez-disgorgement-term], but the court decided only whether the bonus was a wage. In our review we found no Massachusetts appellate decision on recovering a cash sign-on or retention bonus already paid, so the conservative course is to treat repayment of a bonus advanced before it was earned as a contract claim and, if the agreement instead fixes an amount as damages for leaving early, to expect a court to test that amount under the liquidated damages rule [^ma-cummings-test-clawback], and to collect either separately rather than from wages, remembering that an employer may not separately bill an employee for amounts it could not lawfully deduct [^ma-454-cmr-indirect-clawback].

"Coverall is not free to withhold — much less recapture — the employee’s earned wages."[^ma-awuah-no-recapture]

## Can a Massachusetts employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Massachusetts employer may set a repayment off against final wages only if it is a clear and established debt owed by the employee [^ma-camara-special-contract-setoff][^ma-awuah-no-clear-debt-setoff]. In *Camara v. Attorney General*, the court held that a deduction based on the employer's unilateral, untested judgment of fault and damages was not such a debt, while recognizing that a set-off arrangement could be valid where the parties voluntarily agreed to appropriately independent procedures for determining both the existence and amount of the debt [^ma-camara-unilateral-deductions][^ma-camara-independent-procedures]; the inference is that a training or bonus repayment the employee disputes, and that no such procedure has established, is unlikely to qualify. An employee who leaves must be paid in full on the next regular payday, and an employee who is discharged must be paid in full on the day of discharge [^ma-s148-final-pay-timing]. In a Wage Act action, the only defenses to a failure to pay are attachment by trustee process, a valid assignment, a valid set-off, the employee's absence at the time of payment, or an actual tender of the wages [^ma-s150-limited-defenses].
The Appeals Court has stated that an employer may not reduce wages by, or condition full and timely payment of wages on, payments by the employee to or on behalf of the employer [^ma-fraelick-no-wage-reduction]. In our review we found no Massachusetts appellate decision holding that an employee's advance written consent turns a training or bonus repayment into a clear and established debt, and the Act bars exempting an employer from its payment duties by a special contract [^ma-s148-special-contract-deduction], so the conservative course is to pay final wages in full and collect any balance separately, within the minimum-wage limits described next.
Massachusetts minimum-wage rules add a floor. No deduction other than those required or expressly allowed by law, or the listed lodging and meal deductions, may be made from the basic minimum wage, and an employer may not separately bill an employee for amounts not allowed as deductions [^ma-454-cmr-27-05-no-deductions][^ma-454-cmr-27-05-indirect]. The state minimum wage is $15.00 per hour [^ma-c151-s1-minimum-wage].

"On the trial no defence for failure to pay as required, other than the attachment of such wages by trustee process or a valid assignment thereof or a valid set-off against the same, or the absence of the employee from his regular place of labor at the time of payment, or an actual tender to such employee at the time of payment of the wages so earned by him, shall be valid."[^ma-s150-limited-defenses]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a disputed training or bonus repayment against the last paycheck risks a Wage Act claim in which a prevailing employee must be awarded treble damages, litigation costs and reasonable attorneys' fees [^ma-camara-deduction-caution][^ma-s150-treble-caution].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Massachusetts? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Massachusetts statute specifically regulates training, tuition or relocation repayment agreements in private employment, apart from section 24L's rules for repayments triggered by competition [^ma-24l-competition-trigger-training]. The conservative inference is that a court would test a fixed training-repayment amount under the same liquidated damages rule that applies to other agreed amounts [^ma-cummings-test-training]. Payroll collection of a training balance faces the same Wage Act limits as any other deduction, including the bar on special contracts that exempt the employer from paying earned wages [^ma-s148-special-contract-training].
The Supreme Judicial Court has treated some charges an employee pays to obtain work as contrary to public policy. In *Awuah*, the court viewed initial franchise fees that workers paid to enter what was in substance an employment relationship as special contracts that operate to require employees to buy their jobs, and said they violate public policy [^ma-awuah-buy-their-jobs]. In our review we found no Massachusetts appellate decision applying that reasoning, or the liquidated damages rule, to a private employer's training-repayment agreement, so the conservative course is to limit repayment to the documented cost of training that has value to the employee beyond the job, and to collect it separately, remembering that an employer may not separately bill an employee for amounts it could not lawfully deduct [^ma-454-cmr-indirect-training].
Federal law adds a separate floor: under the Fair Labor Standards Act regulations, wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back the whole or part of the wage to the employer, and the regulation's example treats the cost of employer-required tools as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the required minimum or overtime wages [^ma-fed-531-35-kickback-training][^ma-fed-531-35-tools-training].

## How are Massachusetts repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee whose wages are withheld to collect a repayment may sue under the Wage Act 90 days after filing a complaint with the Attorney General, or sooner with the Attorney General's written assent [^ma-s150-private-action-enforcement]. The suit must be brought within three years after the violation, but that period is tolled from the filing of a complaint with the Attorney General until the Attorney General authorizes a private action or an Attorney General enforcement action becomes final [^ma-s150-private-action-enforcement]. A prevailing employee must be awarded treble damages, as liquidated damages, for lost wages and other benefits, plus litigation costs and reasonable attorneys' fees [^ma-s150-treble-enforcement]. An employer may not penalize an employee in any way for seeking rights under the wage and hour provisions of chapter 149 [^ma-s148a-retaliation-enforcement].
An employer seeking to recover a repayment brings an ordinary contract action, which generally must be commenced within six years after the cause of action accrues [^ma-c260-s2-contract-limitations]. An employee who claims that a repayment amount is a penalty bears the burden of showing that the amount is grossly disproportionate or unconscionably excessive [^ma-nps-burden-enforcement].

## What repayment structure is safest under Massachusetts law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** The safest Massachusetts repayment term sets an amount that, when the agreement is signed, is a reasonable forecast of the employer's hard-to-measure loss from an early departure, such as documented training or recruitment costs, because that is the test Massachusetts courts apply to an agreed amount [^ma-cummings-test-structure]. As a drafting matter, a schedule that reduces the balance as the employee serves the commitment keeps the amount closer to the employer's unrecovered cost.
Making the repayment owed on any early departure, rather than only on joining a competitor, keeps it within the Act's definition of a forfeiture agreement, which the Act excludes from regulated noncompetition agreements [^ma-24l-forfeiture-definition-structure][^ma-24l-forfeiture-excluded-structure]. Collecting the balance by separate demand or action is safer than deducting it from pay, because the Wage Act recognizes a set-off only for a clear and established debt [^ma-awuah-setoff-structure], and any deduction may not reduce the basic minimum wage [^ma-454-cmr-27-05-structure]. Separate collection does not escape that limit, because an employer may not separately charge or bill an employee for amounts not allowed as deductions [^ma-454-cmr-indirect-structure].

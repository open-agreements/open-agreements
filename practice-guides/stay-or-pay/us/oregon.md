# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Oregon[^about]

Oregon has no statute that generally governs training or bonus repayment agreements, so they turn on general contract law, and a stipulated amount framed as damages for breach is tested for reasonableness under Illingworth v. Bushong; ORS 652.610(3) limits deductions from wages, and the Oregon Supreme Court held in Taylor v. Werner Enterprises that an employee's potential liability to the employer does not support a lawful deduction, while the final-pay loan exception excludes loans connected with the employment.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Oregon? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Oregon has no statute that generally permits, prohibits or caps an agreement requiring an employee to repay training costs or a signing, retention or relocation bonus on leaving early. Whether such a term binds the employee therefore turns on general contract law. The Oregon Supreme Court distinguishes an amount due because a contract term has been satisfied from liquidated damages due because of a breach [^or-sop-ditommaso-debt-vs-damages]. Where a repayment term fixes damages for breach, *Illingworth v. Bushong* allows the amount only if it is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual harm and the difficulty of proving loss, and treats an unreasonably large amount as a void penalty [^or-sop-illingworth-liquidated]. No Oregon decision located for this guide decides which category an employee repayment term falls into. Collecting the amount by deduction is limited: a deduction from wages is lawful only if ORS 652.610(3) permits it [^or-sop-deduction-conditions], and the Oregon Supreme Court held in *Taylor v. Werner Enterprises* that an employee's potential liability to the employer is not a reason that supports a lawful deduction [^or-sop-taylor-potential-liability].

"ORS 72.7180(1) provides: ‘Damages for breach by either party may be liquidated in the agreement but only at an amount which is reasonable in the light of the anticipated or actual harm caused by the breach, the difficulties of proof of loss, and the inconvenience or nonfeasibility of otherwise obtaining an adequate remedy. A term fixing unreasonably large liquidated damages is void as a penalty.’ Insofar as policy choice is concerned, enactment of the statute makes the choice, or choices, by the constitutional department of state government that is responsible to the people for that kind of choice. It is true that the legislature’s choice, by its terms, applies only to contracts for the sale of goods, but we are unable to perceive any good reason for not using that same rule as the initial point of departure for analyzing the validity of provisions for liquidated damages in contracts in general."[^or-sop-illingworth-liquidated]

ORS 653.295 limits noncompetition agreements, which it defines as written agreements not to compete with the employer for a period of time or within a geographic area after employment ends [^or-sop-noncompete-definition]. A repayment term triggered by any early departure does not by its terms restrict where the employee works next, and no Oregon decision located for this guide treats such a term as a noncompetition agreement. The conservative course is to make any repayment depend on the timing of the departure and not on whether the employee joins a competitor.

ORS 652.360 bars an employer from exempting itself, by contract or any other means, from a statute relating to the payment of wages without the written approval of the Commissioner of the Bureau of Labor and Industries [^or-sop-no-waiver], so a repayment agreement cannot authorize a deduction or a delay in final pay that the wage statutes do not allow.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment clause authorizing withholding solely because the employee owes the employer a debt risks an unlawful-deduction claim when used to collect from wages [^or-sop-deduction-conditions][^or-sop-taylor-potential-liability][^or-sop-deduction-remedy].

## Can an Oregon employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Oregon appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a signing, retention or relocation bonus already paid can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. ORS 653.295 treats one kind of bonus condition as lawful: a bonus restriction agreement, which Oregon courts may enforce [^or-sop-bonus-restriction-lawful-clawback], limits post-employment competition for a reasonable time, area and set of activities by an employee whose services include substantial management involvement, customer contact, knowledge of customer requirements or knowledge of trade secrets or other proprietary information, and its only penalty is forfeiture of profit sharing or other bonus compensation that has not yet been paid [^or-sop-bonus-restriction-definition-clawback]. That definition does not reach a demand to return a bonus already paid [^or-sop-bonus-restriction-definition-clawback]. Wages already earned must be paid when employment ends, by the deadlines in ORS 652.140, which run from a discharge or termination by mutual agreement [^or-sop-final-pay-discharge-clawback] or from a resignation with or without notice [^or-sop-final-pay-quit-notice-clawback][^or-sop-final-pay-quit-no-notice-clawback], and a willful failure to pay them triggers penalty wages [^or-sop-penalty-wages-clawback].

Whatever the bonus terms say, an employer that withholds wages to recover a bonus relies on the employee's liability to the employer, which *Taylor v. Werner Enterprises* held does not support a lawful deduction [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-clawback][^or-sop-taylor-potential-liability-clawback]. As a drafting recommendation whose effect no Oregon decision located for this guide has tested, an employer may state the service condition in the bonus terms and seek any repayment by demand or suit, not by withholding wages. Collecting separately does not escape every wage rule: the Bureau of Labor and Industries states that an employer may not require an employee to pay, including by direct charge, amounts that could not lawfully have been deducted under the minimum wage law if doing so would reduce the employee's minimum wage earnings [^or-sop-boli-direct-charge-clawback].

## Can an Oregon employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** An Oregon employer may deduct a training or bonus repayment from final wages only if one of the grounds in ORS 652.610(3) applies [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-final-pay]. ORS 652.610(3) lists the only grounds for a deduction from wages [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-final-pay]. A deduction relying on ORS 652.610(3)(b), which covers deductions the employee voluntarily authorizes in writing, is lawful only if it is for the employee's benefit and recorded in the employer's books, and in *Taylor v. Werner Enterprises* the Oregon Supreme Court held that the employee's potential liability to the employer does not support a lawful deduction [^or-sop-taylor-potential-liability-final-pay]. The separate ground for deducting a loan balance from final wages applies only to a loan paid in cash or another permitted medium, made solely for the employee's benefit and not used for any purpose required by the employer or connected with the employment, and caps the deduction at the amount that could be garnished [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-final-pay]. Applying subsection (3)(f)(C), training costs and a signing or relocation bonus are connected with the employment, so that ground does not fit them [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-final-pay].

"The wording of ORS 652.610(3)(b) is unambiguous. An item must fall within its strictures to be deducted under it — that is, the employee’s written authorization must be given, and the deduction must be recorded in the employer’s books and must be for the ultimate benefit of the employee. ORS 652.610(3)(b). Potential liability of an employee to the employer is not a reason that supports a lawful deduction."[^or-sop-taylor-potential-liability-final-pay]

In *Ayala v. Culbertsons LLC* the Oregon Court of Appeals upheld payroll deductions repaying wage advances the employee had asked for, reasoning that he received the money before the deductions and would not have received it without agreeing to repay [^or-sop-ayala-benefit-final-pay]. The court distinguished *Taylor* because the deduction there was an employer-imposed bond designed to protect the employer [^or-sop-ayala-distinguishes-taylor-final-pay], and none of the deductions in *Ayala* was taken from the final paycheck or upon termination [^or-sop-ayala-no-final-pay-deduction-final-pay]. No Oregon decision located for this guide applies *Ayala* to a deduction recovering training costs or a bonus at separation, which arises from the employee's debt to the employer.

Final wages are due by the end of the first business day after a discharge or a termination by mutual agreement [^or-sop-final-pay-discharge-final-pay], immediately if an employee without a contract for a definite period quits after at least 48 hours' notice, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and holidays [^or-sop-final-pay-quit-notice-final-pay], and otherwise, except for employees who regularly submit time records, within five days excluding Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, or at the next regularly scheduled payday, whichever comes first [^or-sop-final-pay-quit-no-notice-final-pay]. An employee who quits without notice and is regularly required to submit time records must be paid the employer's estimate of wages due within five days after quitting and the rest within five days after submitting the records [^or-sop-final-pay-time-records-final-pay].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A final-pay setoff clause authorizing a training or bonus deduction without a ground in ORS 652.610(3) exposes the employer that relies on it to a private action for actual damages or $200, whichever is greater, with a possible award of reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing party, and, if the withholding is willful, to penalty wages of eight hours' pay a day for up to 30 days [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-final-pay][^or-sop-deduction-remedy-final-pay][^or-sop-penalty-wages-final-pay][^or-sop-penalty-cap-final-pay].

## Are training or relocation repayments treated differently in Oregon? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Oregon has no statute that addresses training, tuition or relocation repayment agreements for private employees. Such a term is analyzed under general contract law, which distinguishes an amount due because a contract term has been satisfied from liquidated damages due because of a breach [^or-sop-ditommaso-debt-vs-damages-training]. If the fixed amount is damages for leaving early, the rule adopted in *Illingworth v. Bushong* allows it only if reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual harm and voids an unreasonably large amount as a penalty [^or-sop-illingworth-liquidated-training]. The loan-repayment ground for deductions from final wages excludes a loan used for any purpose required by the employer or connected with the employment [^or-sop-deduction-conditions-training], so a training or relocation advance cannot be recovered under that ground.

ORS 653.295 limits a noncompetition agreement to written agreements restricting post-employment competition [^or-sop-noncompete-definition-training]. A training repayment term that applies whenever the employee leaves early, and not only when the employee competes, falls outside that definition on its terms [^or-sop-noncompete-definition-training], although no Oregon decision located for this guide addresses the point.

## How are Oregon repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An Oregon employee whose wages were reduced by an unlawful deduction has a private cause of action for actual damages or $200, whichever is greater, and the court may award reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing party [^or-sop-deduction-remedy-enforcement]. An employer seeking repayment may bring a contract claim, and Oregon distinguishes two possible theories of recovery, an amount due because a contract term has been satisfied and liquidated damages due because of a breach [^or-sop-ditommaso-debt-vs-damages-enforcement]. A liquidated damages claim must satisfy the reasonableness rule of *Illingworth v. Bushong* [^or-sop-illingworth-liquidated-enforcement]. If the employer willfully fails to pay final wages, the wages continue as a penalty at the same hourly rate for eight hours a day until paid or until an action is commenced [^or-sop-penalty-wages-enforcement], but for no more than 30 days from the due date [^or-sop-penalty-cap-enforcement]. The penalty generally may not exceed 100 percent of the unpaid wages if the employee gives no written notice of nonpayment [^or-sop-penalty-no-notice-cap-enforcement], and the cap is lifted after written notice only if the employer fails to pay in full within 12 days [^or-sop-penalty-notice-cap-enforcement]; neither cap applies if the employer violated the final-pay statutes in the prior year or terminated other employees on the same date [^or-sop-penalty-cap-exceptions-enforcement]. In an action to collect wages unpaid for 48 hours, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, after they are due, the court must include reasonable attorney fees in a judgment for the employee, subject to the statutory exceptions [^or-sop-attorney-fees-enforcement].

An employer cannot avoid these remedies by contract, because ORS 652.360 bars an employer from exempting itself from any statute relating to the payment of wages without the commissioner's written approval [^or-sop-no-waiver-enforcement].

## What repayment structure is safest under Oregon law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** Where an Oregon repayment term fixes damages for breach, the rule adopted in *Illingworth v. Bushong* allows the amount only if it is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual harm and the difficulty of proving loss [^or-sop-illingworth-liquidated-safest]. As a drafting recommendation, not an assured result, the safest structure is therefore a written repayment agreement, signed before the training or payment, with an amount tied to the employer's documented cost that declines over the service period. Oregon treats an amount due because a contract term has been satisfied as a separate theory [^or-sop-ditommaso-debt-vs-damages-safest], and no Oregon decision located for this guide places an employee repayment term in either category. The repayment should also depend on an early departure and not on competition, keeping it outside the definition of a noncompetition agreement in ORS 653.295 [^or-sop-noncompete-definition-safest]. Where the employer wants a competition condition on a bonus, a forfeiture of bonus compensation not yet paid can qualify as a statutory bonus restriction agreement, but only if the restraint is reasonable in time, area and activities and the employee's services include substantial management involvement, customer contact, knowledge of customer requirements or knowledge of trade secrets or other proprietary information [^or-sop-bonus-restriction-definition-safest]. Final wages should be paid in full by the ORS 652.140 deadline that applies to a discharge or mutual termination [^or-sop-final-pay-discharge-safest] or to a resignation [^or-sop-final-pay-quit-notice-safest][^or-sop-final-pay-quit-no-notice-safest][^or-sop-final-pay-time-records-safest], with any balance pursued separately, because a deduction justified only by the employee's liability to the employer is not lawful [^or-sop-taylor-potential-liability-safest]. Separate collection remains subject to the Bureau of Labor and Industries position that an employer may not require an employee to pay, by direct charge or otherwise, amounts that could not lawfully have been deducted under the minimum wage law if that would reduce the employee's minimum wage earnings [^or-sop-boli-direct-charge-safest].

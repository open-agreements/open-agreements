# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Maryland[^about]

Maryland has no statute on stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is a contract question: a loan is read by its terms, and an early-departure charge is likely to be measured, by analogy, against the liquidated damages rule, while Labor and Employment § 3-503 allows a wage deduction only with a court order, the employee's express written authorization, the Commissioner's approval or another law's permission. A 2026 bill to void training repayment agreements did not pass.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Maryland? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Maryland's highest court has interpreted an employer tuition-loan note, repayable on a schedule that declined with service after the degree, to require repayment whether the employee was fired or quit [^md-sop-credible-schedule-enf][^md-sop-credible-holding-enf]. No Maryland appellate decision located for this guide applies the liquidated damages test to an early-departure charge that is not repayment of an actual loan; for such a charge, the closest Maryland rule is the liquidated damages test, under which the clause must state a certain sum in clear and unambiguous terms and must reasonably compensate the damages anticipated from the breach [^md-sop-barrie-elements-enf]. No Maryland statute governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment terms in private employment. Maryland's non-compete statute voids a noncompete or conflict of interest provision that restricts a covered employee from working for a new employer or becoming self-employed in the same or a similar business [^md-sop-3-716-void-enf], but it does not mention repayment terms.

"There are three essential elements of a valid and enforceable liquidated damages clause. First, such a clause must provide in clear and unambiguous terms for a certain sum. Secondly, the liquidated damages must reasonably be compensation for the damages anticipated by the breach. Thirdly, liquidated damage clauses are by their nature mandatory binding agreements before the fact which may not be altered to correspond to actual damages determined after the fact."[^md-sop-barrie-elements-enf]

In *Credible Behavioral Health, Inc. v. Johnson*, the note required repayment of a declining share of the loan on termination [^md-sop-credible-schedule-enf], and the Court of Appeals held, as a matter of reading the note, that repayment was due on that schedule whether the employee was fired or quit [^md-sop-credible-holding-enf]. In *Barrie School v. Patch*, the Court of Appeals, now the Supreme Court of Maryland, restated those three elements [^md-sop-barrie-elements-enf] and found the agreed sum in the contract before it a reasonable forecast of just compensation for the harm a breach could cause [^md-sop-barrie-forecast-enf]. *Barrie School* did not involve an employee repayment term, and no Maryland appellate decision located for this guide applies the liquidated damages rule to a training-repayment or stay-or-pay charge. The conservative course is to set any fixed repayment amount by reference to a documented estimate of the employer's loss from an early departure, such as the cost of the training or the hiring incentive, rather than as a deterrent.

A repayment term that applies only if the employee goes to work for a competitor raises a second question. In *Holloway v. Faw, Casson & Co.*, the Court of Appeals noted that many jurisdictions analyze comparable damage clauses in employment contracts or partnership agreements as restrictive covenants [^md-sop-holloway-covenant-enf], and it held that the intermediate appellate court did not err by allowing no more than three years [^md-sop-holloway-three-years-enf]. Under § 3-716, the ban on noncompete and conflict of interest provisions covers employees earning no more than 150 percent of the State minimum wage, licensed health care workers who provide direct patient care and earn no more than $350,000 a year (for agreements executed on or after July 1, 2025 [^md-sop-ch378-applicability-enf]), licensed veterinary practitioners and technicians, and licensed architects whose employer had more than 30 mostly Maryland-based employees when the employment began but, when it seeks enforcement, no longer has most of its employees or its principal place of business in Maryland [^md-sop-3-716-coverage-enf]. No Maryland decision located for this guide decides whether a repayment obligation counts as a noncompete or conflict of interest provision under § 3-716. The conservative course for a covered employee is not to tie any repayment to the employee's later work.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment schedule that fixes an amount unrelated to any estimate of the employer's loss from an early departure risks failing the requirement that liquidated damages reasonably compensate the anticipated damages [^md-sop-barrie-elements-enf].

## Can a Maryland employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Maryland appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a sign-on, retention or other bonus that has already been paid can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. A bonus promised as part of the compensation for service is a wage the employee may enforce [^md-sop-whiting-promised-claw], and the Court of Appeals has held that contract language cannot eliminate an employee's right to be compensated for work already performed [^md-sop-medex-contract-claw].

For the Wage Payment and Collection Law, a wage includes a bonus, a commission, a fringe benefit and any other remuneration promised for service [^md-sop-3-501-wage-claw]. In *Whiting-Turner Contracting Co. v. Fitzpatrick*, the Court of Appeals held that a payment that is not part of the promised compensation is a gratuity, revocable at any time before delivery [^md-sop-whiting-gratuity-claw]. In *Medex v. McCabe*, the Court held that an employee's right to incentive fees earned by his work vested without satisfying a plan condition requiring continued employment [^md-sop-medex-vesting-claw], while noting that payments dependent on conditions other than the employee's efforts lie outside the definition of wage [^md-sop-medex-conditions-claw]. Neither case involved recovering a payment the employee had already received, and no Maryland decision located for this guide decides whether labeling a bonus an advance repayable on early departure keeps it from being a wage already earned. Under *Medex*, a continued-employment condition cannot prevent earned incentive wages from vesting, although payments dependent on conditions other than the employee's efforts fall outside the wage definition [^md-sop-medex-vesting-claw][^md-sop-medex-conditions-claw]. By inference, an advance label may not control if the payment was in substance earned by work already performed.

Recovering the money from wages is a separate question. An employer may deduct from wages only with a court order, the employee's express written authorization, the Commissioner's approval or another law's permission [^md-sop-3-503-deductions-claw], so an enforceable repayment debt does not by itself permit withholding it from pay.

## Can a Maryland employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Maryland employer may deduct a repayment from final wages only if a court orders it, the employee expressly authorizes it in writing, the Commissioner allows it because the employee received full consideration for it, or another law or governmental regulation permits it [^md-sop-3-503-deductions-final]. All wages for work performed before termination are due on or before the day the employee would have been paid had employment continued [^md-sop-3-505-final-final].

"An employer may not make a deduction from the wage of an employee unless the deduction is: (1) ordered by a court of competent jurisdiction; (2) authorized expressly in writing by the employee; (3) allowed by the Commissioner because the employee has received full consideration for the deduction; or (4) otherwise made in accordance with any law or any rule or regulation issued by a governmental unit."[^md-sop-3-503-deductions-final]

An unauthorized deduction is itself a failure to pay wages: in *Marshall v. Safeway, Inc.*, the Court of Appeals held that a deduction that violates § 3-503 also violates § 3-502 or § 3-505 [^md-sop-marshall-deduction-final]. No Maryland decision located for this guide decides whether a training or bonus repayment term signed at hiring is an express written authorization for a later deduction from the final paycheck, or whether training an employee is the full consideration on which the Commissioner may allow a deduction. The conservative course is to obtain a separate written authorization that names the repayment and the amount when the deduction is taken, or to pay the final wages in full and collect the repayment by demand.

The minimum wage may also limit a deduction, although no Maryland appellate decision located for this guide decides whether an authorized deduction to repay an advance or loan may take pay below it. The State minimum wage is $15.00 an hour [^md-sop-3-413-minimum-final], an agreement to work for less than the wage required under the Wage Payment and Collection Law is void [^md-sop-3-502-void-final], and under the Wage and Hour Law an employee's agreement to work for less than the wage due is no defense to the employee's action for the shortfall [^md-sop-3-427-defense-final]. The conservative course is to leave pay at or above the minimum wage after any repayment deduction.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-paycheck deduction for a training or bonus repayment without a court order, the employee's express written authorization, the Commissioner's approval or another law's permission is an unpaid-wage violation that can expose the employer to an award of up to three times the amount withheld [^md-sop-marshall-deduction-final][^md-sop-3-507-2-treble-final].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Maryland? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Maryland statute gives training, tuition or relocation repayment terms a rule of their own. The Court of Appeals has interpreted a tuition-loan note repayable on a declining schedule to require repayment whether the employee was fired or quit [^md-sop-credible-schedule-train][^md-sop-credible-holding-train]. A charge fixed as damages for leaving early is likely to be tested, like a bonus repayment term, under the liquidated damages rule [^md-sop-barrie-elements-train], and any recovery from wages is subject to the same wage-deduction rule [^md-sop-3-503-deductions-train]. A 2026 bill, House Bill 203, would have made a training repayment agreement null and void [^md-sop-hb203-void-train], but as of November 20, 2026, the General Assembly's legislation record shows its status as a House hearing on February 5, 2026, with no later action listed [^md-sop-hb203-status-train], so it is not law.

"IN THIS SECTION, ‘TRAINING REPAYMENT AGREEMENT’ MEANS AN AGREEMENT THAT REQUIRES AN EMPLOYEE TO PAY AN EMPLOYER OR A THIRD PARTY, SUCH AS A TRAINING PROVIDER, A SUM OF MONEY IF THE EMPLOYEE VOLUNTARILY OR INVOLUNTARILY LEAVES EMPLOYMENT WITH THE EMPLOYER."[^md-sop-hb203-definition-train]

House Bill 203 defined a training repayment agreement as one requiring an employee to pay the employer or a third party, such as a training provider, if the employee leaves employment voluntarily or involuntarily [^md-sop-hb203-definition-train]. It would have barred an employer from requiring one as a condition of employment [^md-sop-hb203-void-train].

Maryland's non-compete rules for health care and veterinary workers do not address repayment. The § 3-716 ban now covers licensed health care workers in direct patient care who earn no more than $350,000 a year, and licensed veterinary practitioners and technicians [^md-sop-3-716-coverage-train]. Chapter 378 of 2024 added those health care provisions, and they apply only to agreements executed on or after July 1, 2025 [^md-sop-ch378-applicability-train]. For a licensed health care worker in direct patient care earning more than $350,000 a year, a noncompete or conflict of interest provision may last no more than one year after employment ends [^md-sop-3-716-health-limit-train]. Neither rule mentions recovering relocation, training or similar expenses, and no Maryland decision located for this guide decides whether § 3-716 reaches a repayment obligation.

## How are Maryland repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee whose wages an employer failed to pay as § 3-502 or § 3-505 requires, including by an unlawful repayment deduction, can sue to recover them once two weeks have passed since the wages were due [^md-sop-3-507-2-action-enforce][^md-sop-marshall-deduction-enforce]. If the court finds the wages were withheld in violation of the Wage Payment and Collection Law and not as a result of a bona fide dispute, it may award up to three times the wage plus reasonable counsel fees and costs [^md-sop-3-507-2-treble-enforce]. The employer's own repayment claim is an ordinary contract claim, enforceable as liquidated damages only if the amount reasonably compensates the damages anticipated from the breach [^md-sop-barrie-elements-enforce].

An unauthorized deduction supports that wage action: in *Marshall v. Safeway, Inc.*, the Court of Appeals held that a deduction that violates § 3-503 also violates § 3-502 or § 3-505 [^md-sop-marshall-deduction-enforce]. An employee paid less than the wage the Wage and Hour Law requires may recover the shortfall, an equal additional amount as liquidated damages, and counsel fees and costs [^md-sop-3-427-liquidated-enforce]; no Maryland appellate decision located for this guide decides when an authorized repayment deduction that takes pay below the minimum wage is such an underpayment. The conservative course is to leave pay at or above the minimum wage after any repayment deduction. A civil action at law must be filed within three years after it accrues unless another provision of the Code sets a different period [^md-sop-cjp-5-101-enforce].

## What repayment structure is safest under Maryland law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A Maryland employee repayment provision presents less penalty risk when it states a certain sum that reasonably compensates the employer's anticipated loss from an early departure [^md-sop-barrie-elements-safe]. Any deduction from wages should rest on the employee's express written authorization or another ground § 3-503 allows [^md-sop-3-503-deductions-safe], and should leave pay at or above the $15.00 State minimum wage [^md-sop-3-413-minimum-safe]; otherwise the repayment should be collected by demand rather than from wages.

A bonus promised as part of the compensation for service is an enforceable wage [^md-sop-whiting-promised-safe], so the safer course is not to recover a bonus from wages, and to set any repayment by reference to what the employee has not yet served rather than to pay already earned. Many jurisdictions analyze damage clauses triggered by later competitive work as restrictive covenants [^md-sop-holloway-covenant-safe], and Maryland sustains a covenant not to compete only if it is no wider in area and duration than reasonably necessary to protect the employer's business [^md-sop-holloway-maryland-rule-safe]. By analogy, the conservative course for a repayment triggered only by competitive work is to keep its scope and duration within that limit; a repayment triggered by any new employment, competitive or not, is further from that line of cases. Whether § 3-716, which voids covered noncompete and conflict of interest provisions [^md-sop-3-716-void-safe], reaches such a repayment is not decided by any Maryland decision located for this guide. The conservative course is not to impose one on a covered employee.

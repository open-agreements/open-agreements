# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Florida[^about]

Florida has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract, a sum fixed as damages for leaving early must pass Florida's two-part liquidated-damages test, and federal wage law, which Florida's minimum-wage law follows, limits taking the money out of pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Florida? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Florida has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment. In *City of South Miami v. Dembinsky*, a police recruit signed an agreement to reimburse the city's academy expenses if he voluntarily left within two years [^fl-sop-dem-agree-enf], and the Third District Court of Appeal held the promise enforceable because the city continued to employ him after he signed [^fl-sop-dem-hold-enf]. No Florida appellate decision located for this guide addresses a private employer's training or bonus repayment term. Where a repayment term fixes a sum in advance as damages for leaving early, it is tested as liquidated damages: Florida lets contracting parties stipulate in advance to an amount to be paid as liquidated damages for a breach [^fl-sop-lef-stipulate-enf], provided the damages from a breach are not readily ascertainable when the contract is made [^fl-sop-lef-first-enf][^fl-sop-lef-formation-enf] and the sum is not so grossly disproportionate to the expected damages as to show that the parties intended only to induce full performance [^fl-sop-lef-second-enf].

"We do so on the ground that the city's continued employment of Dembinsky after the agreement, despite its admitted right then and for more than six months thereafter to fire him if he refused to execute it or for any other reason, rendered his promise of reimbursement an enforceable one."[^fl-sop-dem-hold-enf]

The trial court in *Dembinsky* had held the agreement unenforceable for lack of consideration [^fl-sop-dem-trial-enf]. The appellate court stressed that the promise was conditional: the officer would owe nothing if the city fired him [^fl-sop-dem-cond-enf]. *Dembinsky* held that the city's continued employment supplied consideration for the reimbursement promise [^fl-sop-dem-hold-enf].

In *Lefemine v. Baron*, the Florida Supreme Court held that a clause giving the non-breaching party the option either to take the fixed sum or to sue for actual damages shows an intent to penalize [^fl-sop-lef-option-enf], and that such a clause is a penalty as a matter of law [^fl-sop-lef-penalty-enf]. When a liquidated damages clause fails, the claimant is left to prove its actual damages [^fl-sop-lef-actual-enf].

Florida's restrictive-covenant statute permits enforcement of contracts that restrict or prohibit competition if they are reasonable in time, area and line of business [^fl-sop-s542-scope-enf], and it lists extraordinary or specialized training among the legitimate business interests that can justify such a covenant [^fl-sop-s542-interests-enf]. No Florida appellate decision located for this guide applies that statute to a stand-alone obligation to repay training costs or a bonus, which does not by its terms restrict where the employee may work.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment clause that lets the employer recover either the stated amount or its actual losses, whichever it prefers, risks being struck as a penalty under *Lefemine* [^fl-sop-lef-option-enf].

## Can a Florida employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Florida appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a sign-on, retention or other bonus paid in cash can be recovered under an express stay-or-pay term. The closest decision points against recovering compensation already earned [^fl-sop-diaz-not-entitled-claw]. In *Diaz v. Dr. Bertram P. Shapiro, P.A.*, a physician resigned during his second contract year without the advance notice of termination his employment contract required [^fl-sop-diaz-breach-claw], and after a bench trial the employer obtained a judgment [^fl-sop-diaz-judgment-claw] equal to the year-end bonus already paid for his first year's work [^fl-sop-diaz-sued-claw]. The Third District Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the employer was not entitled to the return of a bonus earned for the first year's work [^fl-sop-diaz-not-entitled-claw].

"We agree with the employee that the employer was not entitled to a return of the bonus earned for the first year’s work."[^fl-sop-diaz-not-entitled-claw]

The court reasoned that the bonus was apportioned under the employment agreement to the first contract year [^fl-sop-diaz-apportioned-claw], so the employee was entitled to keep it for the services rendered in that year [^fl-sop-diaz-keep-claw]. The employer sought the bonus as damages for the missing termination notice [^fl-sop-diaz-theory-claw]. No Florida appellate decision located for this guide decides how an agreement stating that a bonus is advanced against a future service period is treated, and a repayment sum fixed as damages for leaving must still satisfy Florida's liquidated-damages test, including that it not be so grossly disproportionate to the expected damages as to show an intent only to induce performance [^fl-sop-lef-second-claw].

## Can repayment come out of a Florida final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Florida has no general wage-deduction or final-pay-deadline statute for private employment. A narrow exception covers labor pools employing day laborers, whose deductions not authorized by federal or state law may not bring pay below minimum wage [^fl-sop-labor-pool-final]. For other employers, the limit comes from the minimum wage. Florida's minimum wage reached $15.00 per hour on September 30, 2026 [^fl-sop-const-rate-final], and the Florida Constitution directs that federal Fair Labor Standards Act case law and administrative interpretations guide its construction [^fl-sop-const-flsa-final]. Under federal law, wages are not paid unless paid free and clear [^fl-sop-cfr-free-final], and the wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back any part of the wage to the employer [^fl-sop-cfr-kickback-final]. The Department of Labor treats a genuine loan or wage advance differently: its principal may be deducted even if the deduction cuts into the minimum wage or overtime pay [^fl-sop-dol-loan-final].

"The wage requirements of the Act will not be met where the employee ‘kicks-back’ directly or indirectly to the employer or to another person for the employer's benefit the whole or part of the wage delivered to the employee."[^fl-sop-cfr-kickback-final]

A deduction that recovers the employer's own costs violates federal law in any workweek in which it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^fl-sop-cfr-tools-final]. Tuition the employer advanced on condition of continued employment may be recouped without violating the FLSA, provided the employee clearly understood the terms before accepting the payment [^fl-sop-dol-tuition-final]. Whether a particular training or bonus repayment is an advance or an employer cost depends on how it was structured. Because the Florida constitutional minimum wage is higher than the federal one and is construed by reference to federal standards [^fl-sop-const-flsa-final], the conservative course is to leave every final paycheck at or above $15.00 for each hour worked in the final workweek and to collect any balance separately.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a training or bonus repayment against the last paycheck exposes the employer to the unpaid wages plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, with attorney's fees and costs, if a deduction for the employer's own costs pushes pay below the Florida minimum wage [^fl-sop-fmwa-remedy-final].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Florida? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Florida statute located for this guide treats private-sector training, tuition or relocation repayment differently from other repayment terms. Training repayment is the setting of *Dembinsky*, where the Third District Court of Appeal enforced a police officer's promise to reimburse academy expenses if he voluntarily left within two years [^fl-sop-dem-agree-train][^fl-sop-dem-hold-train]. Training also appears in Florida's restrictive-covenant statute, which lists extraordinary or specialized training among the legitimate business interests that can support a covenant restricting competition [^fl-sop-s542-interests-train], but that statute governs contracts that restrict or prohibit competition [^fl-sop-s542-scope-train]. No Florida appellate decision located for this guide applies that statute to a stand-alone obligation to repay training costs or a bonus.

"It is our opinion that an employer may recoup tuition monies advanced to an employee in the circumstances you describe without violating FLSA, provided that the employee clearly understands the terms of the employer's tuition payment policy prior to accepting the payment."[^fl-sop-dol-tuition-train]

Where a training-repayment amount is stipulated damages for leaving early, it has to satisfy Florida's liquidated-damages test: the damages from a breach must not be readily ascertainable when the agreement is made [^fl-sop-lef-first-train][^fl-sop-lef-formation-train], and the sum must not be grossly disproportionate to the damages reasonably expected [^fl-sop-lef-second-train]. A relocation payment the employer requires the employee to repay out of wages raises the federal question discussed for deductions from pay: a cost the employer requires the employee to bear for the employer's work may not cut into the minimum or overtime wages [^fl-sop-cfr-tools-train].

## What happens to a Florida employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee may sue over an unlawful deduction recovering the employer's own costs that reduces pay below Florida's minimum wage, and recover unpaid wages, liquidated damages, fees and costs [^fl-sop-const-flsa-enf2][^fl-sop-cfr-tools-enf2][^fl-sop-fmwa-remedy-enf2], subject to a good-faith exception. The court may reduce or deny the liquidated damages if the employer proves good faith and reasonable grounds [^fl-sop-fmwa-goodfaith-enf2]. Federal FLSA interpretations guide the construction of Florida's minimum wage [^fl-sop-const-flsa-enf2]; under them, a cost deduction is unlawful in any workweek in which it cuts into the minimum wage [^fl-sop-cfr-tools-enf2], while the principal of a genuine loan or wage advance may be deducted [^fl-sop-dol-loan-enf2]. Before suing under the Florida Minimum Wage Act, the employee must give the employer written notice of intent to sue [^fl-sop-fmwa-notice-enf2], and the employer then has 15 calendar days to pay or otherwise resolve the claim [^fl-sop-fmwa-cure-enf2]. Under federal law, an employer that violates the minimum wage or overtime provisions is liable for the unpaid amounts plus an equal amount as liquidated damages [^fl-sop-u216-enf2], which a court may reduce or deny if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds [^fl-sop-u260-enf2].

"Actions to enforce this amendment shall be subject to a statute of limitations of four years or, in the case of willful violations, five years."[^fl-sop-const-limit-enf2]

In any action for unpaid wages, a Florida court may award the prevailing party its costs and a reasonable attorney's fee [^fl-sop-fee-448-08-enf2]. An employer may collect a repayment by suit or, as the city did in *Dembinsky*, by counterclaim in the employee's own wage suit [^fl-sop-dem-counter-enf2]. An action on a contract founded on a written instrument has five years [^fl-sop-lim-written-enf2]. If the clause fixes the amount as liquidated damages, the employer must be ready to defend it against both parts of the Florida test [^fl-sop-lef-first-enf2][^fl-sop-lef-formation-enf2][^fl-sop-lef-second-enf2]; if the clause fails, the employer is left to prove its actual damages [^fl-sop-lef-actual-enf2].

## What repayment structure is safest under Florida law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** Under Florida law, a fixed early-departure damages clause requires damages that are not readily ascertainable when the agreement is made [^fl-sop-lef-first-safe][^fl-sop-lef-formation-safe] and a sum not so grossly disproportionate to expected losses as to show an intent merely to compel continued service [^fl-sop-lef-second-safe]. A stipulated-damages clause should make the fixed sum the employer's only remedy for early departure, because an option to choose between liquidated damages and actual damages shows an intent to penalize [^fl-sop-lef-option-safe] and makes the clause a penalty as a matter of law [^fl-sop-lef-penalty-safe]. As a drafting matter, the agreement should be signed before the employer pays, so that the employee understands the terms before accepting the payment [^fl-sop-dol-tuition-safe], and the amount should decline as the employee serves the commitment. In *Diaz*, the employee could keep a bonus earned for completed services [^fl-sop-diaz-keep-safe], and no Florida appellate decision located for this guide decides whether an express bonus advance against future service is recoverable.
Collecting the balance by demand or suit on the written agreement avoids netting it against pay. Separate collection does not by itself avoid the federal rule: wages kicked back to the employer directly or indirectly are treated as unpaid [^fl-sop-cfr-kickback-safe], and federal FLSA interpretations guide the construction of Florida's minimum wage [^fl-sop-const-flsa-safe], so recovering the employer's own costs must still leave the Florida minimum wage intact [^fl-sop-const-rate-safe], while the principal of a genuine loan or advance may be recovered [^fl-sop-dol-loan-safe]. If the repayment term is paired with a covenant restricting competition, the covenant must be in a writing signed by the employee [^fl-sop-s542-writing-safe].

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in California[^about]

A question-by-question summary of California stay-or-pay law for contracts signed before and after January 1, 2027, including Labor Code Sections 2802, 2804, 221 and 224 and the training-repayment cases that govern contracts signed before 2027, the Business and Professions Code Section 16608 ban that AB 692 and AB 1697 apply to contracts entered on or after January 1, 2027, its discretionary-bonus and tuition exceptions, the Labor Code Section 926 void rule and worker civil action, the bar on deducting repayment from final pay, and how Sections 925 and 16600.5 reach California workers regardless of out-of-state drafting.

## Are repayment terms enforceable? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** For a California employment contract entered into before January 1, 2027, Business and Professions Code Section 16608 does not apply, because AB 1697 made the 2026 version of Business and Professions Code Section 16608 inoperative and limited its ban to later contracts [^ca-16608-2026-version-inoperative][^california-labor-code-sec-926]. Repayment terms in those contracts are tested under general law: in *USS-POSCO Industries v. Case*, the Court of Appeal enforced repayment of the fronted cost of a voluntary, portable training program against Section 16600 and Labor Code Section 2802 challenges [^ca-posco-not-a-restraint][^ca-posco-voluntary-training-labor-code], and in *In re Acknowledgment Cases* it held that Section 2802 bars an employer from recovering the cost of training it mandates for its own needs [^ca-acknowledgment-mandated-training]. For contracts entered into on or after January 1, 2027, the ban described below applies. Most employee repayment terms in a California employment contract entered into on or after January 1, 2027 are void unless a statutory exception applies [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166][^california-labor-code-sec-926]. Business and Professions Code Section 16608, added by Assembly Bill 692 (AB 692) and amended by Assembly Bill 1697 (AB 1697), makes a term requiring a worker to pay the employer back when employment ends unlawful, and Labor Code Section 926 makes a contract or term that violates Section 16608 void as a matter of public policy [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166][^california-labor-code-sec-926]. AB 1697, an urgency statute approved on September 30, 2026, moved the start date from January 1, 2026 to January 1, 2027 and made the 2026 version of both sections inoperative, so these provisions do not impose liability for a term in a contract entered into during 2026 [^ca-ab-1697-approval-and-urgency][^ca-ab-1697-approval-date][^ca-16608-2026-version-inoperative][^ca-926-2026-version-inoperative][^ca-ab-1697-no-2026-liability].

AB 692 reframes repayment not as an ordinary debt but as a mobility restraint, the same family of terms California has long disfavored. The statute reaches a term that requires the worker to pay the employer, and the void rule is keyed to a hard date.

"A contract or contract term that violates Section 16608 of the Business and Professions Code is void as contrary to public policy only if entered into on or after January 1, 2027."[^california-labor-code-sec-926]

The date matters: a term in an agreement signed before January 1, 2027 is not voided by Section 926 [^california-labor-code-sec-926], but a term in an agreement entered on or after that date that requires the worker to pay falls within the ban unless one of the Section 16608(b)(2) exceptions applies [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166]. AB 692 originally applied the ban to contracts entered into on or after January 1, 2026, and firm commentary at the time described it as a broad prohibition on most employment-related repayment and exit-fee provisions [^mayer-brown-a-deeper-dive-into-california-s-new][^greenberg-traurig-california-claws-back-new-limi]. AB 1697 then declared that its amendments prevent liability from accruing for acts between January 1, 2026 and its effective date, and that any pending claim based on a violation alleged in that period is moot [^ca-ab-1697-no-2026-liability].

"Any pending claim based on a violation alleged to have occurred during that period shall be moot."[^ca-ab-1697-no-2026-liability]

AB 1697 also added exceptions for bonuses under specified recruitment and retention programs, for repayment of advanced paid time off on voluntary separation, and for certain affiliation contracts of securities broker-dealers, insurance producers and investment advisers [^ca-ab-1697-new-exceptions-digest].

## Can the employer claw back pay already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** Whenever the agreement was signed, California Labor Code Section 221 makes it unlawful for an employer to collect or receive back from an employee any part of wages the employer has already paid [^ca-lab-221-clawback]. For an agreement entered into on or after January 1, 2027, an employer generally has no enforceable right to claw back pay unless a statutory exception applies [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-clawback][^california-labor-code-sec-926-clawback]. Section 16608 makes a term requiring the worker to pay the employer when employment ends unlawful except as its exceptions provide, and Section 926 makes a term that violates Section 16608 void [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-clawback][^california-labor-code-sec-926-clawback]. The exception written for a bonus, Section 16608(b)(2)(D), covers a discretionary or unearned payment, including a bonus, that is not tied to specific job performance, and only if all five of its conditions are met [^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout-clawback].

Unless the term fits an exception, the repayment promise itself is void, so there is nothing to claw back. As enacted by AB 692, the bonus exception reached only a payment made at the outset of employment; AB 1697 removed that limit, so a sign-on or retention bonus qualifies under exception (D) only if the repayment terms meet every (D) condition, from the separate agreement and counsel window to proration within two years and a separation that was the worker's choice or for misconduct [^ca-ab-692-bonus-exception-outset][^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout-clawback]. Worse for the employer, Section 926(b) turns the violation into an affirmative claim that a worker or worker representative may bring, and under Section 926(c) a person found liable owes the worker actual damages or a fixed statutory sum per worker, whichever is greater, plus injunctive relief and attorney's fees [^ca-926-damages-gate].

"Any person found liable for a violation of this section shall be liable for actual damages sustained by the worker or workers on whose behalf the case is brought, or five thousand dollars ($5,000) per worker, whichever is greater, in addition to injunctive relief, and reasonable attorney’s fees and costs."[^ca-926-damages-gate]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A bonus-repayment agreement for a contract entered into on or after January 1, 2027 that gives the worker no option to defer receipt of the bonus to the end of the retention period fails the Section 16608(b)(2)(D) exception, so the repayment term is void under Section 926 unless another exception applies, and trying to enforce it exposes the employer to actual damages or five thousand dollars per worker, whichever is greater, plus fees [^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout-clawback][^ca-ab-1697-new-exceptions-digest-clawback][^california-labor-code-sec-926-clawback][^ca-926-damages-gate].

## Can repayment come out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A California employer cannot set off a claimed repayment against the final paycheck, whenever the repayment agreement was signed [^ca-barnhill-no-setoff]. Labor Code Section 224 permits withholding only when the law requires or empowers it or for listed deductions the employee authorizes in writing [^ca-lab-224]. For an agreement entered into on or after January 1, 2027, a repayment term that violates Section 16608 is also void under Section 926, and if the underlying repayment term is void, the employer has no lawful basis to take the amount out of the worker's wages, including the final paycheck [^california-labor-code-sec-926-deduction][^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-deduction].

California already restricts deductions from wages to narrow, authorized categories, and a void repayment obligation is not one of them. Because AB 692 voids the repayment term itself, an employer cannot convert it into a final-pay deduction; firm commentary treats the prohibition as sweeping in most repayment and exit-fee structures [^mayer-brown-a-deeper-dive-into-california-s-new-deduction][^greenberg-traurig-california-claws-back-new-limi-deduction].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Section 16608 does not apply to a California contract entered into before January 1, 2027 [^ca-16608-2026-version-inoperative-training]. For those contracts, Labor Code Section 2802.1 makes a general acute care hospital bear the cost of training it provides or requires for direct-patient-care employees and applicants [^ca-lab-2802-1-hospital-training][^ca-lab-2802-1-hospital-scope], except for a license, registration or certification needed to practice and for training the worker undertakes solely at their own discretion [^ca-lab-2802-1-exclusions]. In *USS-POSCO Industries v. Case*, the Court of Appeal enforced repayment of the fronted costs of a voluntary, portable training program [^ca-posco-voluntary-training-labor-code-training][^ca-posco-not-a-restraint-training], but Labor Code Section 2802 makes the employer bear the cost of training it mandates for its own needs, and in *In re Acknowledgment Cases* an agreement to reimburse that cost was held void under Section 2804 [^ca-acknowledgment-mandated-training-training][^ca-acknowledgment-void-2804-training][^ca-lab-2804-training]. Training, tuition, and relocation repayment terms in California contracts entered into on or after January 1, 2027 have no general exemption from the stay-or-pay ban [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-training][^california-labor-code-sec-926-training]. Section 16608 makes a term requiring the worker to pay an employer or training provider for a debt when employment ends unlawful except as its exceptions provide, and Section 926 makes a term that violates Section 16608 void [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-training][^california-labor-code-sec-926-training]. The exceptions written for education and training costs are narrow: a contract under a government loan repayment assistance or loan forgiveness program, a contract for the tuition cost of a transferable credential that meets five conditions, and a contract related to enrollment in an apprenticeship program approved by the Division of Apprenticeship Standards [^ca-16608-government-loan-program][^ca-16608-tuition-exception][^ca-16608-apprenticeship].

A training-repayment-agreement provision, often shortened to TRAP, asks a worker to repay the cost of training if they leave early. Because Section 16608 targets the repayment obligation itself rather than the label, a TRAP in an agreement entered into on or after January 1, 2027 is void unless it fits one of the Section 16608(b)(2) exceptions [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-training][^california-labor-code-sec-926-training].

The tuition exception in Section 16608(b)(2)(B) covers a contract for the cost of tuition for a transferable credential only if the contract is offered separately from any employment contract, does not make obtaining the credential a condition of employment, states the repayment amount in advance and caps it at the employer's cost, prorates repayment over any required employment period without accelerating it on separation, and requires no repayment if the employer terminates the worker other than for misconduct [^ca-16608-tuition-exception].

The credential itself must be a third-party degree that is portable, not training the current job requires [^ca-16608-transferable-credential].

"‘Transferable credential’ means a degree that is offered by a third-party institution that is accredited and authorized to operate in the state, is not required for a worker’s current employment, and is transferable and useful for employment beyond the worker’s current employer."[^ca-16608-transferable-credential]

Section 16608(b)(2)(C) separately excludes a contract related to enrollment in an apprenticeship program approved by the Division of Apprenticeship Standards [^ca-16608-apprenticeship]. Section 16608(b)(2)(A)(i) excludes a contract entered into under a loan repayment assistance or loan forgiveness program provided by a federal, state, or local governmental agency [^ca-16608-government-loan-program]. The discretionary-payment exception in Section 16608(b)(2)(D) is written for the worker's receipt of a discretionary or unearned monetary payment, including a bonus, and it does not mention training costs [^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout-training].

So a tuition program tied to a portable third-party degree can fall outside the ban if it meets every (B) condition, and an apprenticeship contract can if the program is approved by the Division of Apprenticeship Standards; an in-house training cost packaged as a repayment obligation generally cannot [^ca-16608-tuition-exception][^ca-16608-apprenticeship].

## Does California law reach an out-of-state agreement? {#out-of-state-reach}

**Short answer.** California law usually reaches a California worker's repayment term even when the agreement selects another state's law [^california-labor-code-sec-925]. Labor Code Section 925 limits an employer's ability to force a California-based employee to litigate elsewhere or under another state's law, and Business and Professions Code Section 16600.5 extends California's restraint rules regardless of where and when the contract was signed, so a choice-of-law clause does not reliably move a California worker's repayment term outside AB 692 [^california-labor-code-sec-925][^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-2].

Multistate employers often select a friendlier state's law. California pushes back on two fronts. Section 925 restricts forcing a worker who primarily resides and works in California into an out-of-state forum or law as a condition of employment.

"(a) An employer shall not require an employee who primarily resides and works in California, as a condition of employment, to agree to a provision that would do either of the following: (1) Require the employee to adjudicate outside of California a claim arising in California. (2) Deprive the employee of the substantive protection of California law with respect to a controversy arising in California."[^california-labor-code-sec-925]

Section 16600.5 reinforces this by applying California's restraint rules without regard to where or when the agreement was made [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-2].

## What repayment structure is safest under California law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** For a California contract entered into before January 1, 2027, a repayment term is most defensible when it recovers the fronted cost of voluntary, portable training rather than training the employer requires for its own needs [^ca-posco-not-a-restraint-safest][^ca-acknowledgment-mandated-training-safest]. The safest structure for a California worker in a contract entered into on or after January 1, 2027 carries no repayment term [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-safest]. Section 16608 makes it unlawful to include in an employment contract a term requiring the worker to pay the employer for a debt if the worker's employment with that employer ends, subject to narrow exceptions [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-safest]. One exception covers a discretionary or unearned payment, such as a bonus, that is not tied to specific job performance: its repayment obligation is lawful only if it is set out in a separate agreement, the worker is told of the right to consult an attorney and given at least five business days to do so before signing, the obligation carries no interest and is prorated over a retention period of no more than two years from receipt, the worker may instead defer receipt to the end of that period, and the worker left by their own choice or was terminated for misconduct [^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout][^ca-16608-counsel-window][^ca-16608-carveout-safest].

Because Section 16608 voids most repayment obligations, the safest design is simply not to use one. Where a bonus payment must be protected, the exception is exacting. It requires that the worker be told of the right to consult an attorney and given a real window to do so before signing.

"right to consult an attorney regarding the agreement and provided with a reasonable time period of not less than five business days to obtain advice of counsel prior to executing the agreement."[^ca-16608-counsel-window]

It also caps and de-risks the repayment itself — no interest, proration over a retention period that cannot exceed two years, and an option for the worker to defer receipt instead of taking on any repayment obligation.

"Any repayment obligation for early separation from employment is not subject to interest accrual and is prorated based on the remaining term of any retention period, which shall not exceed two years from the receipt of payment. (iv) The worker has an option to defer receipt of the payment to the end of a fully served retention period without any repayment obligation."[^ca-16608-carveout-safest]

Since AB 1697, the exception no longer requires the payment to be made at the outset of employment, so a retention bonus paid during employment can carry a repayment term if it meets every condition [^ca-ab-692-bonus-exception-outset-safest][^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> An imported bonus-repayment clause in a contract entered into on or after January 1, 2027 that omits the separate agreement or the five-business-day counsel window fails the Section 16608(b)(2)(D) exception, and the repayment obligation is then void under Section 926 unless another Section 16608(b)(2) exception applies [^california-business-and-professions-code-sec-166-safest][^california-labor-code-sec-926-safest][^ca-16608-sign-on-carveout].

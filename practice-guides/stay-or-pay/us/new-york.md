# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in New York[^about]

A question-by-question summary of New York stay-or-pay law, which has no stay-or-pay statute in force until Labor Law Article 37 (the Trapped at Work Act) takes effect on December 19, 2026, and until then limits repayment through the earned-versus-forfeitable wage doctrine and Labor Law Section 193, including Matter of William Mattar, P.C. v. Riley on when a bonus becomes vested wages, the bar on clawing back earned compensation, the deduction limits on final pay, the treatment of training repayment, and the safest forfeitable-bonus structure.

## Are repayment terms enforceable? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** A New York continued-service condition can govern a bonus that is still discretionary and forfeitable, but compensation that is vested and mandatory counts as wages, which Labor Law Section 193 bars an employer from deducting outside narrow categories [^truelove-v-northeast-capital-continued-employment-bonus][^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli][^ny-labor-law-193-deduction-bar-enforceability]. New York has no stay-or-pay statute, so a repayment or continued-service condition can be valid, but it cannot reach compensation the worker has already earned. New York courts draw the line between a bonus that is vested and mandatory and one that is discretionary and forfeitable, and only the latter can be conditioned on staying [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli]. From December 19, 2026, when Labor Law Article 37, the Trapped at Work Act, is scheduled to take effect, an employment promissory note required as a condition of employment will be void unless it fits one of the exceptions listed in Labor Law Section 1052(2) [^ny-labor-law-1052-condition-of-employment-bar][^ny-labor-law-1052-promissory-note-bar][^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions][^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-effective-date].

The controlling idea is that some compensation hardens into wages the worker owns, while other compensation stays contingent until a condition is met. In *Matter of William Mattar, P.C. v. Riley*, the court framed the distinction that decides which repayment terms hold.

"vested and mandatory as opposed to discretionary and forfeitable"[^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli]

Once a bonus is vested and mandatory, it is the worker's wages and a stay-or-pay condition cannot take it back; while it remains discretionary and forfeitable, a continued-service condition can apply. Commentary frames the same line in operational terms: a bonus tied to objective, formula-based criteria and calculable with reasonable certainty reads as mandatory rather than discretionary [^cohen-buckmann-bonus-season-strategies-getting-r].

New York enacted the Trapped at Work Act on December 19, 2025 and amended it on February 13, 2026 [^mayer-brown-trapped-at-work-signing-dates]. The amendment limited the Act to employees and prospective employees and moved its effective date by one year, to December 19, 2026 [^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-employee-scope][^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-effective-date]. The amendment's own sections on definitions, prohibition and enforcement take effect on the same date as the 2025 Act [^ny-s8822-effective-date-section]. An employment promissory note is a term that requires an employee to pay the employer if the employment relationship ends before a stated period of time [^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-amendment-summary]. From the effective date, Labor Law Section 1052(1) will bar an employer from requiring an employee or prospective employee to sign such a note as a condition of employment and will make a note signed on that condition void [^ny-labor-law-1052-condition-of-employment-bar][^ny-labor-law-1052-promissory-note-bar].

"The execution of an employment promissory note as a condition of employment is unconscionable, against public policy, and unenforceable, and any such note shall be null and void."[^ny-labor-law-1052-promissory-note-bar]

From December 19, 2026, Section 1052(2) will keep five kinds of agreement outside that prohibition. A tuition, fees and educational-materials repayment for a transferable credential will qualify only if it is in a written contract offered separately from the employment contract, the credential is not a condition of employment, the amount is fixed in advance and capped at the employer's cost, repayment is prorated without acceleration, and no repayment is owed if the employee is terminated other than for misconduct [^ny-labor-law-1052-tuition-written-contract][^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions]. Instruction in the employer's proprietary processes or systems, or on proprietary equipment unique to the employer, and safety or compliance training required by law, will not count as a transferable credential [^ny-labor-law-1050-transferable-credential]. A repayment term for a financial bonus, relocation assistance or other non-educational payment not tied to specific job performance will remain outside the prohibition only if repayment is not owed when the employee was terminated for any reason other than misconduct or when the duties or requirements of the job were misrepresented [^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions]. A voluntary sale or lease of employer property, the terms of a sabbatical leave for educational personnel, and a program agreed with the employees' collective bargaining representative will also stay outside it [^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions][^ny-labor-law-1052-sabbatical-and-bargained-programs].

Article 37 will also carry its own enforcement. An employee whom an employer sues to enforce a void note will be able to recover attorney's fees after a successful defense [^ny-labor-law-1053-attorney-fees]. An aggrieved employee or prospective employee will be able to file a complaint with the labor commissioner, who will fine an employer found in violation between one thousand and five thousand dollars for each violation [^ny-labor-law-1053-complaint-and-fines][^ny-labor-law-1053-commissioner-fines]. Each employee or prospective employee whom an employer required to sign a note, or against whom it seeks to enforce one, will count as a separate violation [^ny-labor-law-1053-separate-violations].

Commentary notes that the amended clause making the Act effective one year after it became law could be read to run from the February 13, 2026 amendment rather than the December 19, 2025 enactment, so the conservative course is to treat December 19, 2026 as the effective date [^holland-knight-trapped-at-work-effective-date-ambiguity].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A New York repayment clause required as a condition of employment from December 19, 2026, and drafted without fitting an applicable Labor Law Section 1052(2) exception, risks being void under Article 37 and exposes the employer to the employee's attorney's fees if the employee successfully defends a suit to enforce it [^ny-labor-law-1052-promissory-note-bar][^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions][^ny-labor-law-1053-attorney-fees].

## Can the employer claw back pay already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** A New York employer cannot withhold earned bonus wages from a departed employee on the strength of a continued-employment condition: in *Matter of William Mattar, P.C. v. Riley*, the Fourth Department treated the nonpayment as a deduction barred by Labor Law Section 193(1) and held the current-employee restriction void [^mattar-withheld-bonus-deduction-clawback]. The dividing line is whether the bonus is vested and mandatory rather than discretionary and forfeitable; vested, mandatory compensation belongs to the worker even if a contract clause purports to require repayment on early departure [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-clawback][^cohen-buckmann-bonus-season-strategies-getting-r-clawback].

The practical risk for employers is timing. Commentary applying *Mattar* says a bonus tied to objective, formula-based criteria and calculable with reasonable certainty is mandatory rather than discretionary compensation, which is the kind of compensation *Mattar* protects as wages [^cohen-buckmann-bonus-season-strategies-getting-r-clawback].

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> The defective provision is a current-employee condition attached to a formula bonus the worker has already earned: in *Mattar*, that condition was void to the extent it restricted payment to current employees, and the employer owed the earned amount [^mattar-withheld-bonus-deduction-clawback].

## Can repayment come out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A New York employer generally cannot net a claimed stay-or-pay repayment out of the final paycheck, because Labor Law Section 193 bars any deduction from wages outside narrow categories and a stay-or-pay repayment is not one of the authorized deductions [^ny-labor-law-193-deduction-bar][^ny-labor-law-193].

Section 193 is the wage-deduction gatekeeper. A deduction is permitted only when it falls within a defined category — chiefly deductions made under a law or government regulation, and a closed list of voluntary deductions the employee expressly authorizes in writing for the employee's own benefit.

"1. No employer shall make any deduction from the wages of an employee, except deductions which: a. are made in accordance with the provisions of any law or any rule or regulation issued by any governmental agency including regulations promulgated under paragraph c and paragraph d of this subdivision; or b. are expressly authorized in writing by the employee and are for the benefit of the employee, provided that such authorization is voluntary and only given following receipt by the employee of written notice of all terms and conditions of the payment and/or its benefits and the details of the manner in which deductions will be made."[^ny-labor-law-193]

A stay-or-pay clawback does not fit that list: it runs for the employer's benefit, not the employee's, so it cannot be taken from wages under the voluntary-deduction path, and recovering it requires a separate contract claim rather than self-help against the paycheck [^ny-labor-law-193].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently from bonus repayments in New York? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** New York Labor Law Section 193 restricts wage deductions for training, tuition, relocation and bonus repayments alike to its authorized statutory categories [^ny-labor-law-193-deduction-bar-training]. An employer may not deduct the amount from wages unless the deduction is made under a law or regulation, is expressly authorized in writing by the employee for the employee's own benefit, or falls in another narrow statutory category [^ny-labor-law-193-deduction-bar-training][^ny-labor-law-193-training]. The same section bars an employer from making any charge against wages, or requiring the employee to make a payment by separate transaction, unless the charge or payment is permitted as a deduction or under a current collective bargaining agreement [^ny-labor-law-193-separate-payment-training].

A training-repayment-agreement provision, often shortened to TRAP, asks a worker to repay the cost of training if they leave early. New York law in force today does not give TRAPs a separate statutory track. From December 19, 2026, Labor Law Article 37 will require a transferable-credential tuition repayment agreement to be a written contract offered separately from any employment contract, and will bar it from making the credential a condition of employment; its other conditions are set out in the answer on [whether repayment terms are enforceable](#repayment-enforceability) [^ny-labor-law-1052-tuition-written-contract-training][^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-effective-date-training]. The question Section 193 answers is how the employer may collect: a paycheck deduction must fit one of the section's categories, and the written-authorization category is limited to deductions for the employee's benefit [^ny-labor-law-193-deduction-bar-training][^ny-labor-law-193-training]. The limit is not confined to the paycheck: an employer may not make a charge against wages, or require a payment by separate transaction, that would not be permitted as a deduction, unless a current collective bargaining agreement permits or requires it [^ny-labor-law-193-separate-payment-training].

"No employer shall make any charge against wages, or require an employee to make any payment by separate transaction unless such charge or payment is permitted as a deduction from wages under the provisions of subdivision one of this section or is permitted or required under any provision of a current collective bargaining agreement."[^ny-labor-law-193-separate-payment-training]

The Section 193 excerpts quoted here restrict deductions from wages, charges against wages and payments required by separate transaction; they do not establish whether a particular training debt is enforceable as a contract.

*Matter of William Mattar, P.C. v. Riley* is a bonus case, and it decides a narrower question: whether compensation counts as wages, which turns on whether the compensation is vested and mandatory rather than discretionary and forfeitable [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-training]. It bears on a training repayment only when the employer tries to collect by withholding or taking back compensation, because the test then decides whether that compensation is protected wages. That is the line between recovering wages, which Section 193 restricts directly, and enforcing an independent training debt, which raises the separate contract question above. Neither Section 193 nor *Mattar* addresses whether a training repayment must be prorated or capped.

## What retention-bonus structure is safest under New York stay-or-pay law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** Under New York law, the more durable retention structure is a genuinely discretionary bonus with an express continued-service condition, because the Court of Appeals in *Truelove v. Northeast Capital & Advisory* held that a worker who resigned was not entitled to installments the plan predicated on continued employment [^truelove-discretionary-bonus-not-wages-safest][^truelove-continued-employment-bonus-safest]. A bonus that is vested and mandatory is wages instead, and a continued-service label does not change that [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-safest][^cohen-buckmann-bonus-season-strategies-getting-r-safest].

The earned-versus-forfeitable line points to a clear design choice. For a genuinely discretionary, forfeitable award, failing to satisfy a valid continued-service condition can leave the employee without any entitlement to the unpaid award, which is a forfeiture rather than a debt to repay [^truelove-continued-employment-bonus-safest][^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-safest]. The opposite posture, where the worker is paid first and then asked to repay, cannot use the paycheck as the route to recovery: once compensation is vested and mandatory it is wages, and Section 193 does not authorize a deduction for the employer's benefit [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-safest][^ny-labor-law-193-safest].

From December 19, 2026, Labor Law Section 1052(2)(c) will add a further limit on the pay-first-then-repay posture: a repayment term for a bonus or relocation benefit not tied to specific job performance will qualify for that exception only if no repayment will be owed after a termination for any reason other than misconduct or after a misrepresentation of the job's duties or requirements [^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions-safest][^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-effective-date-safest]. An award forfeited before it is earned, with no obligation to repay money, will not need that exception, because Article 37 reaches terms that require an employee to pay the employer when employment ends early [^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-amendment-summary-safest].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> The exposed combination is a retention bonus paid up front with a clause letting the employer deduct the repayment from wages if the worker leaves: Section 193 does not authorize a deduction for the employer's benefit, so the payroll route to recovery fails [^ny-labor-law-193-safest].

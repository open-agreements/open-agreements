# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Louisiana[^about]

Louisiana has no stay-or-pay statute, but its courts of appeal have held training-repayment terms imposed on at-will employees void as against public policy, and have treated a repayment clause that recovers employer-required training from a fixed-term employee's final pay as a forfeiture of wages that La. R.S. 23:634 forbids. A training-repayment agreement is not a non-compete under La. R.S. 23:921 when recoupment of the cost is its primary obligation, the Third Circuit has enforced an at-will employee's prorated signing-bonus repayment, and wrongful withholding of final pay can expose the employer to penalty wages and attorney fees under La. R.S. 23:632.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Louisiana? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** No Louisiana statute addresses stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment agreements in private employment.
Louisiana's courts of appeal have refused to enforce training-repayment terms against at-will employees. In *Newsom v. Global Data Systems, Inc.* the Third Circuit held void the employer's terms requiring at-will employees to reimburse training and education expenses [^la-sop-newsom-void][^la-sop-newsom-training-different-enf], reasoning that withholding their final pay would impose liability on them for leaving their jobs [^la-sop-newsom-at-will-liability]. In *Leprettre v. RCS, LLC* the same court rejected an employer's argument that a training loan for a short, defined period, forgiven over that period, was different [^la-sop-leprettre-distinction] and held that the employee's liability on leaving was itself the defect [^la-sop-leprettre-liability].

"It is the fact of liability at discharge which violates public policy, thus rendering the Loan Agreement at issue null and void."[^la-sop-leprettre-liability]

Fixed-term employment does not remove these limits. Louisiana law lets parties stipulate the damages recoverable for nonperformance, as a secondary obligation that enforces the principal one [^la-sop-cc-2005], and a court may modify stipulated damages only if they are so manifestly unreasonable as to be contrary to public policy [^la-sop-cc-2012]. Applying that reasonableness requirement, the Third Circuit refused to enforce a clause requiring an employee to pay a full year's salary for leaving at any point before the end of a five-year contract [^la-sop-scully-unreasonable], and the Fourth Circuit held that a fixed-term employee's obligation to repay employer-required training was a de facto forfeiture of wages that La. R.S. 23:634 forbids [^la-sop-gallo-forfeiture][^la-sop-gallo-fixed-term-enf][^la-sop-gallo-benefit-enf]. Section 23:634 bars an employer from requiring employees to sign contracts under which they forfeit their wages if they are discharged or resign before the contract is completed [^la-sop-forfeiture-bar].

No Louisiana Supreme Court decision located for this guide decides whether a training, signing-bonus or relocation repayment term is enforceable, and these are intermediate appellate decisions from the Third and Fourth Circuits. The conservative course for an employer is to assume that a training-repayment obligation triggered by an at-will employee's departure will not be enforced.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A loan label and forgiveness schedule in an at-will training-repayment clause can leave the repayment obligation unenforceable when departure itself triggers liability [^la-sop-leprettre-distinction][^la-sop-leprettre-liability].

## Can a Louisiana employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** A Louisiana employer can recover a prorated share of a signing bonus from an at-will employee who quits within the agreed period under a written repayment agreement, on the Third Circuit's decision in *Martco Partnership v. Frazier* [^la-sop-martco-clause][^la-sop-martco-enforceable]. The agreement there stated that the employee was at will and would return a proportionate share of the bonus if he left within three years [^la-sop-martco-at-will], and he quit before then [^la-sop-martco-quit].
*Martco* was decided in 2001, before *Newsom* [^la-sop-newsom-at-will-clawback] and *Leprettre* [^la-sop-leprettre-liability-clawback], and no later decision located for this guide applies their at-will reasoning on training repayment to a signing bonus. In *Kearney v. Lee Medical International, Inc.* the Fifth Circuit read a retention-bonus repayment condition triggered by leaving the employer as applying only if the employee left voluntarily [^la-sop-kearney-voluntary]. The employee there had been terminated [^la-sop-kearney-terminated], and the court held that the bonus was wages subject to the wage statutes [^la-sop-kearney-wages] and affirmed the award of the withheld $5,000 to him [^la-sop-kearney-award]. Wages already earned are protected: an employer may not require employees to sign contracts under which they forfeit their wages on discharge or resignation before the contract is completed, and employees remain entitled to the wages actually earned up to separation [^la-sop-forfeiture-bar-clawback].

For final-pay purposes, a commission, incentive pay or bonus counts as an amount then due only if, at separation, it has been earned and has not been modified under a written policy addressing it [^la-sop-bonus-earned-clawback]. A bonus that the employee has not yet earned under a written retention condition is therefore treated differently from one already paid: in *Avila v. Sanofi-Aventis U.S.* the Fifth Circuit held that an employer did not violate the wage payment law by refusing deferred bonus installments that were conditioned on continued employment when the employee was terminated before they were paid [^la-sop-avila-deferred][^la-sop-avila-installments][^la-sop-avila-conditions]. In *Clark v. CHRISTUS Health Northern Louisiana* the Second Circuit read an offer letter's two-year commitment as attached to the sign-on bonus rather than to the employment itself [^la-sop-clark-bonus-commitment] and found that no term was attached to the offer of employment [^la-sop-clark-no-term].

In *Leprettre* the Third Circuit later held a training-loan agreement void because it made an at-will employee liable on leaving [^la-sop-leprettre-liability-clawback]. How that reasoning bears on a signing-bonus repayment like the one enforced in *Martco* is unresolved in the decisions located for this guide. The conservative course is to put any signing-bonus repayment in a written agreement providing for a prorated share, as in *Martco* [^la-sop-martco-clause], or to pay a retention bonus in deferred installments conditioned on continued employment, the structure upheld in *Avila* [^la-sop-avila-deferred-cl2][^la-sop-avila-installments][^la-sop-avila-conditions], rather than paying it in advance and seeking it back.

## Can a Louisiana employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Louisiana courts of appeal have rejected an employer's withholding of final pay to recover training costs, both from at-will employees [^la-sop-newsom-at-will-final] and from a fixed-term employee whose training the employer required [^la-sop-gallo-forfeiture-final][^la-sop-gallo-fixed-term-final][^la-sop-gallo-benefit][^la-sop-gallo-offset-final]. No appellate decision located for this guide approves a deduction for training costs. An employer must pay a discharged employee the amount then due by the next regular payday or within fifteen days, whichever is first [^la-sop-final-pay-discharge], and a resigning employee by the next regular payday for the pay cycle in which the employee was working or within fifteen days, whichever is first [^la-sop-final-pay-resignation].

Louisiana courts have allowed an employer to set off against final pay certain definite debts, such as damage to the employer's property, salary overpayments, loans and outstanding account balances [^la-sop-newsom-setoff]. For a signing bonus, the Third Circuit in *Martco* upheld an employer's offset of the prorated bonus balance against the departing employee's accrued wages and vacation pay [^la-sop-martco-owed][^la-sop-martco-offset-facts][^la-sop-martco-offset], and held that the employer owed neither wages nor penalties [^la-sop-martco-no-penalty]. *Newsom* distinguished those debts from training costs that benefited the employer [^la-sop-newsom-training-different], and in *Gallo* the court found no evidence that the employer lost profits because the employee attended required training, which instead benefited the employer [^la-sop-gallo-benefit].

The fines statute bars an employer from assessing fines against employees or deducting fines from their wages [^la-sop-no-fines], except for wilful or negligent damage to goods, works or the employer's property, or theft of employer funds after conviction or a guilty plea, and then not beyond the actual damage [^la-sop-fines-exception]. The one statutory repayment deduction is narrow: an employer may require an employee who is paid at least one dollar above the federal minimum wage and is not a part-time or seasonal employee to agree that the cost of a preemployment medical examination or drug test may be withheld if the employee resigns within ninety working days from the first day of work, unless the resignation is attributable to a substantial change the employer made to the employment [^la-sop-medical-exam]. No statute extends that permission to training, bonuses or relocation.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-pay offset clause based on a void training-repayment agreement can expose the employer to penalty wages and attorney fees despite its reliance on the signed agreement [^la-sop-penalty-not-good-faith-final][^la-sop-leprettre-bad-faith].

## Are training repayments treated differently in Louisiana? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** The Third Circuit has held that a Louisiana training-repayment agreement whose primary obligation was recouping the training cost did not restrain the employee from a lawful profession under the non-compete statute [^la-sop-era-not-restraint][^la-sop-era-primary-obligation]. The same court has distinguished reimbursement of training that significantly benefited the employer from collection of an ordinary debt [^la-sop-newsom-training-different-tr].
The non-compete statute makes void every contract by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business, except as the statute provides [^la-sop-921-void], and its current text contains no provision on training costs. In *ERA Helicopters, LLC v. Amegin* the Third Circuit held that an agreement to repay $80,000 of pilot training on leaving within two years did not restrain the pilot from working elsewhere [^la-sop-era-terms][^la-sop-era-not-restraint], and treated recoupment of the cost as the agreement's primary obligation [^la-sop-era-primary-obligation].

"The Pilot Training Agreement does not restrain Mr. Amegin in any way from seeking other employment as a helicopter pilot or in any other capacity in the helicopter industry or in any other profession, business, or trade."[^la-sop-era-not-restraint]

*ERA Helicopters* reversed the dismissal of the employer's suit on an exception of no cause of action and remanded for further proceedings [^la-sop-era-disposition]. The only challenge it decided was the employee's argument that § 23:921 barred the repayment provision [^la-sop-era-premise]. The agreement in that case declined with service, so that the $80,000 would have fallen to $40,000 after one year [^la-sop-era-proration]. In *Leprettre* the same court found it inequitable that an employer could end the employment just before the six-month period ran out and still demand repayment [^la-sop-leprettre-termination].

In *Garage Solutions, LLC v. Person* the Third Circuit said an employee who had already received training was free to agree to reimburse its cost [^la-sop-garage-free-to-agree], but it refused to enforce the agreements because the employer would not release the employee's pay for the training days until he signed [^la-sop-garage-duress], holding that his consent was vitiated by duress [^la-sop-garage-holding]. In *Gallo* the Fourth Circuit noted that the employee there was on a two-year fixed term rather than at will [^la-sop-gallo-fixed-term] and still held the training-reimbursement clause a de facto forfeiture of wages [^la-sop-gallo-forfeiture-tr]. No Louisiana appellate decision located for this guide addresses a relocation repayment term.

## How are Louisiana repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A Louisiana employee whose final pay was withheld to cover a repayment can sue the employer for the unpaid wages [^la-sop-venue] and for penalty wages [^la-sop-penalty-wages]. An employer that fails or refuses to pay final wages as La. R.S. 23:631 requires is liable for the lesser of ninety days' wages at the employee's daily rate or full wages from the employee's demand until payment or tender [^la-sop-penalty-wages], and penalty wages apply when the court finds the failure to pay was not in good faith [^la-sop-penalty-not-good-faith]. In *Newsom* the Third Circuit awarded the employees their earned wages plus ninety days' wages [^la-sop-newsom-award].

The court must allow reasonable attorney fees, taxed against the employer, when the employee files a well-founded suit for unpaid wages more than three days after first demanding payment following discharge or resignation [^la-sop-attorney-fees]. Violations of La. R.S. 23:634 or 23:635 carry a fine of twenty-five to one hundred dollars or imprisonment for thirty days to three months [^la-sop-criminal-penalty].

An employee may sue for wages in the district court of the parish where the work was performed, in addition to any other appropriate court [^la-sop-venue], and an action for wages is subject to a three-year liberative prescription [^la-sop-prescription]. An employer seeking to collect a repayment it believes is valid may sue on the agreement, as the employer in *ERA Helicopters* did, and the court of appeal reversed the dismissal of that suit and remanded [^la-sop-era-disposition-enf]; the employee may still raise the at-will and wage-forfeiture defenses accepted in *Newsom* [^la-sop-newsom-at-will-enf] and *Gallo* [^la-sop-gallo-forfeiture-enf].

## What repayment structure is safest under Louisiana law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** For an at-will employee, the safest course is not to rely on a training-repayment term triggered by leaving, of the kind the Third Circuit held void, and not to withhold final pay to collect one. The Third Circuit has held that liability on leaving is what makes such a term void for an at-will employee [^la-sop-leprettre-liability-safest], and wages actually earned must be paid whatever the contract says [^la-sop-forfeiture-bar-safest].

Where an employer still uses a repayment term, the decisions point to several features that reduce risk:

1. Training that is not required by the employer and does not chiefly benefit the employer. *Gallo* turned on required training that benefited the employer [^la-sop-gallo-benefit-safest].
2. An agreement signed without holding back any pay while the employee decides. The agreements in *Garage Solutions* failed because pay was withheld until the employee signed [^la-sop-garage-duress-safest], which the court held vitiated his consent by duress [^la-sop-garage-holding-safest].
3. A primary obligation of recouping the cost. The Third Circuit treated recoupment as the primary obligation of the agreement in *ERA Helicopters* and held that agreement did not restrain the employee from other work [^la-sop-era-primary-safest][^la-sop-era-not-restraint-safest]. That agreement also declined with service [^la-sop-era-proration-safest], but a declining balance does not cure the at-will defect the Third Circuit identified in *Leprettre*, where liability on leaving was itself what made the agreement void [^la-sop-leprettre-liability-safest].
4. Collection by demand or suit rather than by deduction from final pay. That avoids withholding earned wages, but it does not make an otherwise void training-repayment obligation enforceable. The forfeiture bar protects wages actually earned [^la-sop-forfeiture-bar-safest], the fines statute bars deducting fines from wages [^la-sop-no-fines-safest], and the Third Circuit held that withholding final pay to recover training costs imposed liability on at-will employees for leaving [^la-sop-newsom-at-will-safest].

For a signing bonus, a written agreement to repay a prorated share if employment ends within a stated period is the structure the Third Circuit enforced in *Martco* [^la-sop-martco-clause-safest][^la-sop-martco-enforceable-safest]. For a retention bonus, paying it in deferred installments conditioned on continued employment, rather than paying it in advance and seeking it back, follows the structure the Fifth Circuit upheld in *Avila* [^la-sop-avila-deferred-safest][^la-sop-avila-installments-safest][^la-sop-avila-conditions-safest]. A bonus counts as an amount due at separation only if it has been earned and not modified under a written policy addressing it [^la-sop-bonus-earned-safest]. Whether the later training-repayment decisions affect *Martco*'s signing-bonus holding is unresolved in the decisions located for this guide.

## Documents

* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Alabama](alabama.md) - Alabama has no statute aimed at private-employer stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment clause is tested as a contract under the penalty rule and, if it is triggered by competing, under the restrictive-covenant statute, while Alabama has no general wage-deduction statute and the federal minimum wage limits taking the money out of pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Alaska](alaska.md) - Alaska has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while a Department of Labor regulation separately limits collecting the debt from wages.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Arizona](arizona.md) - Arizona has no generally applicable statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms outside a narrow peace-officer rule, so a repayment clause is tested as a contract, while the wage-withholding statute, treble damages and the state and local minimum-wage floors limit taking the money out of pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in California](california.md) - A question-by-question summary of California stay-or-pay law for contracts signed before and after January 1, 2027, including Labor Code Sections 2802, 2804, 221 and 224 and the training-repayment cases that govern contracts signed before 2027, the Business and Professions Code Section 16608 ban that AB 692 and AB 1697 apply to contracts entered on or after January 1, 2027, its discretionary-bonus and tuition exceptions, the Labor Code Section 926 void rule and worker civil action, the bar on deducting repayment from final pay, and how Sections 925 and 16600.5 reach California workers regardless of out-of-state drafting.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Colorado](colorado.md) - Colorado permits recovery of qualifying training costs on a two-year prorated schedule; other repayment terms raise noncompete questions, and the Wage Act separately limits payroll deductions.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Connecticut](connecticut.md) - Connecticut voids an employment promissory note, including a training-repayment note, that an employer requires as a condition of employment, and from October 1, 2026 that rule reaches employers of every size; outside the statute's exceptions, a repayment term also faces the liquidated-damages test, and section 31-71e limits taking a repayment out of wages.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Delaware](delaware.md) - Delaware has no generally applicable statute aimed at stay-or-pay terms in private employment, so repayment agreements are tested as contracts under the penalty rule, while the Wage Payment and Collection Act separately limits collecting the debt from pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in the District of Columbia](district-of-columbia.md) - The District of Columbia has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is a contract question tested against the common-law rule that a liquidated damages clause disproportionate to foreseeable damages is void as a penalty; the District's non-compete ban governs only provisions that prohibit working for another or running a business, and the Wage Payment and Collection Law requires final wages on a short deadline and bars private agreements that set its protections aside.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Florida](florida.md) - Florida has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract, a sum fixed as damages for leaving early must pass Florida's two-part liquidated-damages test, and federal wage law, which Florida's minimum-wage law follows, limits taking the money out of pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Georgia](georgia.md) - Georgia has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract, a sum fixed as damages for leaving early must pass the three-part liquidated-damages test, and federal wage law limits taking the money out of pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Idaho](idaho.md) - Idaho has no statute that limits private stay-or-pay terms; Idaho courts enforce a repayment term that works as stipulated damages for leaving early only if damages are hard to determine and the amount bears a reasonable relation to actual damages, Idaho Code § 45-609 allows a deduction from wages, including final wages, only under law or the employee's written authorization for a lawful purpose, and the only training-cost statute covers peace officers trained by the state council.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Illinois](illinois.md) - Illinois repayment terms raise separate questions about competition restrictions, earned bonuses, payroll consent, cash advances, training costs, and wage claims.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Indiana](indiana.md) - Indiana has no general stay-or-pay statute, so a repayment term turns on contract law, and the Court of Appeals has treated a fixed training-cost sum far above the employer's measurable costs as an unenforceable penalty. Ind. Code § 22-2-6-2 allows deductions from wages to repay education or skills training only under a written, personally signed and revocable wage assignment. Since July 1, 2025, a hospital or hospital system may not require a physician employed for at least three years to repay bonuses or training costs for leaving to practice with a new employer.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Iowa](iowa.md) - Iowa has no generally applicable statute aimed at stay-or-pay terms in private employment, so repayment agreements are tested as contracts under the penalty rule, while the Wage Payment Collection Law separately limits collecting the debt from pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Kansas](kansas.md) - Kansas has no statute that limits stay-or-pay terms, and Kansas courts enforce a stipulated amount owed for leaving early only if it is reasonable and the damages are hard to measure; the Kansas Wage Payment Act separately bars withholding earned wages as liquidated damages, allows a deduction to repay an employment-related loan or advance under a signed written agreement or, from final wages, after written notice and explanation, and keeps every deduction above the minimum wage.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Kentucky](kentucky.md) - Kentucky has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract and a fixed amount owed for leaving is tested under the liquidated-damages penalty rule, while KRS 337.060 separately limits taking the money out of wages.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Louisiana](louisiana.md) - Louisiana has no stay-or-pay statute, but its courts of appeal have held training-repayment terms imposed on at-will employees void as against public policy, and have treated a repayment clause that recovers employer-required training from a fixed-term employee's final pay as a forfeiture of wages that La. R.S. 23:634 forbids. A training-repayment agreement is not a non-compete under La. R.S. 23:921 when recoupment of the cost is its primary obligation, the Third Circuit has enforced an at-will employee's prorated signing-bonus repayment, and wrongful withholding of final pay can expose the employer to penalty wages and attorney fees under La. R.S. 23:632.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Maryland](maryland.md) - Maryland has no statute on stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is a contract question: a loan is read by its terms, and an early-departure charge is likely to be measured, by analogy, against the liquidated damages rule, while Labor and Employment § 3-503 allows a wage deduction only with a court order, the employee's express written authorization, the Commissioner's approval or another law's permission. A 2026 bill to void training repayment agreements did not pass.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Massachusetts](massachusetts.md) - Massachusetts has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while the Wage Act separately limits collection through earned wages to a valid set-off for a clear and established debt.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Michigan](michigan.md) - Michigan's wage payment act bars an employer from demanding or receiving consideration from an employee as a condition of employment, and the Michigan Supreme Court held in Sands Appliance Services v. Wilson that a mandatory tuition-repayment contract was void under that rule; a 2023 amendment carves out optional education repayment agreements and law enforcement academy costs on a declining four-year schedule, and MCL 408.477 requires written consent for each wage payment from which an employer-benefit deduction is taken.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Minnesota](minnesota.md) - Minnesota has no general stay-or-pay statute, but Minn. Stat. § 181.645 bars requiring an employee to pay for training that law or the employer requires for the current job unless the training is required to obtain or maintain a license, registration or certification, and the Attorney General has applied it to training repayment agreements; other repayment terms are enforced as contracts subject to the penalty rule, while Minn. Stat. § 181.79 bars deducting a claimed debt from wages unless the employee authorizes it in writing after the debt arises or, for a loan, before the loan is made. A 2026 Senate bill that would ban stay-or-pay provisions has not been enacted.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Missouri](missouri.md) - Missouri has no statute that limits stay-or-pay terms, and the Missouri Court of Appeals in Smith v. Kriska enforced a prorated police training-repayment agreement that was a condition of employment; on discharge, earned wages are due that day without abatement or deduction, but older Missouri authority allows an employer to set off a valid employee debt, and the minimum wage and a court's penalty review still limit what the employer can take or recover.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Nevada](nevada.md) - Nevada has no statute specifically regulating stay-or-pay or training-repayment agreements in private employment, so a provision fixing the amount owed for leaving is tested under the liquidated-damages penalty rule, while NRS 608.100, NRS 608.110 and NAC 608.160 separately limit taking the money back out of pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in New Jersey](new-jersey.md) - New Jersey has no statute in force that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract and a sum stipulated as damages for breaching a promise to stay must be a reasonable forecast of the employer's loss, while the Wage Payment Law allows a deduction from wages only for a listed purpose, such as an employer loan repaid on the schedule in the original loan agreement. A pending 2026 bill would make training repayment agreements void.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in New York](new-york.md) - A question-by-question summary of New York stay-or-pay law, which has no stay-or-pay statute in force until Labor Law Article 37 (the Trapped at Work Act) takes effect on December 19, 2026, and until then limits repayment through the earned-versus-forfeitable wage doctrine and Labor Law Section 193, including Matter of William Mattar, P.C. v. Riley on when a bonus becomes vested wages, the bar on clawing back earned compensation, the deduction limits on final pay, the treatment of training repayment, and the safest forfeitable-bonus structure.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in North Carolina](north-carolina.md) - North Carolina has no statute aimed at private-employer stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment clause is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while the Wage and Hour Act's withholding rules and minimum-wage floor limit taking the money out of pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in North Dakota](north-dakota.md) - North Dakota has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay terms, but its Century Code voids contract penalties, allows agreed damages only when actual damage is impracticable or extremely difficult to fix, and voids restraints on a lawful profession, while chapter 34-14 limits collecting a repayment from wages.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Ohio](ohio.md) - Ohio has no statute in force that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so courts enforce repayment promises as contracts, test a fixed amount stipulated for leaving under the liquidated-damages penalty rule and limit cost reimbursement to proven costs, while the wage-assignment statutes and the minimum wage separately limit collecting the debt from pay.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Oregon](oregon.md) - Oregon has no statute that generally governs training or bonus repayment agreements, so they turn on general contract law, and a stipulated amount framed as damages for breach is tested for reasonableness under Illingworth v. Bushong; ORS 652.610(3) limits deductions from wages, and the Oregon Supreme Court held in Taylor v. Werner Enterprises that an employee's potential liability to the employer does not support a lawful deduction, while the final-pay loan exception excludes loans connected with the employment.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Pennsylvania](pennsylvania.md) - Pennsylvania has no general statute on stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while the Wage Payment and Collection Law allows deductions from wages only as the law or a Department of Labor and Industry regulation authorizes them. A 2024 statute on health care practitioners' non-competes preserves recovery of certain relocation and training expenses.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Texas](texas.md) - Texas employee repayment contracts, the limits of the training-cost holding in Sanders v. Future Com, ordinary contract defenses and the penalty doctrine, and the three statutory grounds for wage deductions under Labor Code Section 61.018.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Utah](utah.md) - Utah has no statute that limits stay-or-pay terms, and the Utah Supreme Court presumes a stipulated damages amount enforceable unless it is unconscionable; the Utah Payment of Wages chapter separately bars an employer from requiring an employee to return part of the wages to be paid and allows a deduction from wages, including final wages, only on a listed ground such as the employee's express written authorization.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Virginia](virginia.md) - Virginia has no general stay-or-pay or training-repayment statute, so a repayment term turns on contract law, including the general liquidated-damages test for a sum fixed as damages for leaving early, the non-compete statute expressly preserves prorated cost repayment by health care professionals who leave within five years, and Code § 40.1-29 requires the employee's written and signed authorization before an employer withholds any part of wages.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Washington](washington.md) - Washington repayment terms raise separate questions about payroll deductions, final pay, wage rebates, training costs and the noncompetition statute amended effective June 30, 2027.
* [Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Wisconsin](wisconsin.md) - Wisconsin has no stay-or-pay statute, so a repayment term turns on contract law: the Wisconsin Supreme Court tests a stipulated sum for reasonableness, and the Seventh Circuit has held that a training-repayment obligation not tied to competition falls outside the restrictive-covenant statute, Wis. Stat. § 103.465. Wis. Stat. § 103.455 bars wage deductions for faulty workmanship, loss, theft or damage unless the employee authorizes them in writing, the employer and an employee-designated representative find the employee at fault, or a court finds the employee liable, and makes an employer that violates it liable for twice the amount deducted.

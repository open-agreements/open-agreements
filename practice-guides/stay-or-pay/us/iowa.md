# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Iowa[^about]

Iowa has no generally applicable statute aimed at stay-or-pay terms in private employment, so repayment agreements are tested as contracts under the penalty rule, while the Wage Payment Collection Law separately limits collecting the debt from pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Iowa? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Iowa enforces an agreed damages amount only if it is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss and the difficulty of proving that loss, and refuses an unreasonably large amount as a penalty [^ia-rohlin-penalty-test]. Iowa has no generally applicable statute that bans or limits stay-or-pay, training-repayment or sign-on bonus repayment terms in private employment, so the conservative inference is that a court would test a fixed repayment amount under that penalty rule [^ia-rohlin-penalty-test]. One narrow statute applies only to health-care staffing agencies, Iowa Code section 135Q.2: a health-care employment agency may not, in any contract with an agency worker or health-care entity, require payment of liquidated damages, employment fees or other compensation if the agency worker is later hired as a permanent employee of the health-care entity [^ia-135q2-agency-fee-bar]. That bar does not apply to a contract placing an agency worker whom the agency helped obtain authorization to work in the United States, if the contract has an initial term of at least twenty-four months, a total term of no more than thirty-six months, and requires the worker to work for a single health-care entity throughout [^ia-135q2-work-authorization-exception].
The Iowa Supreme Court's test in *Rohlin Construction Co. v. City of Hinton* allows damages to be liquidated only at an amount that is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss caused by the breach and the difficulty of proving that loss, and it treats a term fixing unreasonably large liquidated damages as an unenforceable penalty [^ia-rohlin-penalty-test]. Applying that test, the court held that a $400-per-day clause was an unrealistic amount and therefore a penalty [^ia-rohlin-400-penalty].
In *Cedar Valley Medical Specialists, P.C. v. Wright*, the Iowa Court of Appeals placed on the employee challenging a liquidated damages payment in an employment contract the burden of proving that it was a penalty [^ia-cedar-valley-burden]. The court upheld the payment because, given the employer's recruitment and promotion expenses, a forgiven deficit and lost payments, the amount did not exceed the employer's anticipated losses from the breach [^ia-cedar-valley-reasonable]. That clause was triggered by competing after leaving [^ia-cedar-valley-reasonable], and an Iowa court separately tests a covenant restricting competition under a three-pronged reasonableness test [^ia-lamp-three-prong], with the employer seeking enforcement bearing the burden of proving reasonableness [^ia-iowa-glass-burden].

"A term fixing unreasonably large liquidated damages is unenforceable on grounds of public policy as a penalty."[^ia-rohlin-penalty-test]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A flat repayment amount that the employer cannot tie to any estimate of the loss the employee's early departure causes risks being treated as a penalty and refused [^ia-rohlin-penalty-caution][^ia-rohlin-400-caution].

## Can an Iowa employer claw back pay already received? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** An Iowa employer may not claw back pay by withholding it from wages unless state or federal law or a court order requires or permits the deduction, or the employee has given written authorization for a lawful purpose benefiting the employee [^ia-91a5-withholding-clawback]. Iowa treats a contractually promised bonus as wages: in *Dallenbach v. MAPCO Gas Products, Inc.*, the Iowa Supreme Court held that an annual bonus was wages because it was part of the compensation owed for the employee's labor or services [^ia-dallenbach-bonus-wages]. The Wage Payment Collection Law defines wages as compensation owed by an employer for labor or services, however calculated [^ia-91a2-wages-definition], and an employer may not withhold or divert any portion of wages except as the law or a court order permits or under the employee's written authorization for a lawful purpose benefiting the employee [^ia-91a5-withholding-clawback].
A forfeiture triggered by going to work for a competitor faces a separate limit. In *Van Hosen v. Bankers Trust Co.*, the Iowa Supreme Court held that forfeiting all of an employee's pension rights merely because he accepted employment with a competing institution imposed an unjust penalty, and that the forfeiture clause was so unreasonable as to violate public policy [^ia-van-hosen-forfeiture].
In our review we found no Iowa appellate decision on recovering a cash sign-on bonus, retention bonus or other compensation already paid, so the conservative course is to assume a court will test the repayment amount under the *Rohlin* penalty rule, which limits an agreed amount to one reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss [^ia-rohlin-test-clawback], and to collect the repayment separately rather than by withholding wages.

"We now hold Article XII of the Agreement, being so unreasonable as to be in violation of public policy, is accordingly unenforceable."[^ia-van-hosen-forfeiture]

## Can repayment come out of an Iowa final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** An Iowa employer may take a repayment out of wages, including final wages, only when state or federal law or a court order requires or permits the deduction, or the employee has given written authorization for a lawful purpose benefiting the employee [^ia-91a5-withholding-limits]. When employment ends, the employer must pay all wages earned, less lawful deductions specified in section 91A.5, by the next regular payday for the pay period in which they were earned [^ia-91a4-final-pay]. Section 91A.5 permits a deduction only when state or federal law or a court order requires or permits it, or when the employee has given written authorization for a lawful purpose accruing to the benefit of the employee [^ia-91a5-withholding-limits].
Section 91A.5 also lists amounts that may not be deducted at all. These include losses from breakage, damage to property, customer credit defaults or nonpayment, unless the loss is attributable to the employee's willful or intentional disregard of the employer's interests [^ia-91a5-damage-bar]. They also include relocation costs above twenty dollars, a bar that applies only to employers that employ 100 or more people in Iowa for hourly wages, as section 91E.1 defines the term with its exclusions [^ia-91a5-relocation-bar][^ia-91e1-employer-definition].
In *Mujkic v. Lynx, Inc.*, the Iowa Court of Appeals held that withholding escrow payments from a driver's wages without written authorization violated chapter 91A, and added that deductions to reimburse the employer for damage to its vehicle were not for the employee's benefit and were expressly prohibited [^ia-mujkic-no-authorization][^ia-mujkic-not-employee-benefit]. In our review we found no Iowa appellate decision on whether a deduction that repays an employer's training, tuition or bonus costs is a purpose accruing to the employee's benefit, so the conservative course is to pay final wages in full and collect any balance separately.
A repayment deduction for the employer's benefit also may not cut into required minimum wages [^ia-481-233-3-kickback]. Iowa's hourly minimum wage is $7.25 from January 1, 2008, or the federal minimum wage if it is greater [^ia-91d1-state-rate][^ia-91d1-federal-greater], and the Iowa rule implementing chapter 91D provides that wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back wages, directly or indirectly, to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^ia-481-233-3-kickback].

"b. The employer has written authorization from the employee to so deduct for any lawful purpose accruing to the benefit of the employee."[^ia-91a5-withholding-limits]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a training or bonus repayment against the last paycheck without a qualifying written authorization risks a wage claim in which an employer that intentionally failed to pay wages owes the unpaid wages, liquidated damages, court costs and attorney's fees [^ia-91a5-withholding-caution][^ia-91a8-damages-caution].

## Are Iowa training or tuition repayments treated differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No generally applicable Iowa statute specifically regulates training or tuition repayment agreements in private employment. The one narrow exception is for health-care staffing agencies: under Iowa Code section 135Q.2, a health-care employment agency may not, in any contract with an agency worker or health-care entity, require payment of liquidated damages, employment fees or other compensation if the agency worker is later hired as a permanent employee of the health-care entity [^ia-135q2-agency-fee-bar-training], unless the contract places an agency worker whom the agency helped obtain authorization to work in the United States, has an initial term of at least twenty-four months and a total term of no more than thirty-six months, and requires the worker to work for a single health-care entity throughout [^ia-135q2-work-authorization-exception-training]. Payroll collection of a training balance faces the same section 91A.5 limits as any other deduction [^ia-91a5-withholding-training], and the conservative inference is that a court would test a fixed training-repayment amount under the general *Rohlin* penalty rule [^ia-rohlin-test-training]. Relocation is the one category the deduction statute names: an employer that employs 100 or more people in Iowa for hourly wages, as section 91E.1 defines the term with its exclusions, may not deduct more than twenty dollars of an employee's relocation costs from wages, which limits payroll collection but does not address whether a separate relocation repayment promise is enforceable [^ia-91a5-relocation-training][^ia-91e1-employer-training].
The closest Iowa appellate decision is *City of Creston v. Barney*, an unpublished Court of Appeals decision involving a public employer. A police officer's agreement required him to reimburse the city for his training costs if he left within three years after completing the training, on a sliding scale from one hundred percent within six months to twenty-five percent in the third year [^ia-barney-training-agreement][^ia-barney-sliding-scale], and after he resigned the district court entered judgment against him for $14,360.85 [^ia-barney-judgment]. On appeal, the court affirmed that the city council had properly ratified the agreement [^ia-barney-ratified]. Iowa also reimburses a city or county for an officer's training expenses when the officer resigns within four years after completing the training [^ia-384-15-officer-reimbursement], a public funding program rather than a rule governing private repayment agreements.
In our review we found no Iowa appellate decision on a private employer's training-repayment agreement, so the conservative course is to treat a training repayment as an agreed amount that must be reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss from the employee's early departure and the difficulty of proving that loss [^ia-rohlin-test-training]. Federal law adds a separate floor: under the Fair Labor Standards Act regulations, wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back the whole or part of the wage to the employer, and the regulation's example treats the cost of employer-required tools as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the required minimum or overtime wages [^fed-531-35-kickback-rule-training][^fed-531-35-tools-training].

## What happens to an Iowa employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An Iowa employee whose employer unlawfully withholds wages may recover the unpaid wages under section 91A.8 [^ia-91a8-other-enforcement]. When the employer intentionally failed to pay, it owes the unpaid wages plus liquidated damages, court costs and usual and necessary attorney's fees; in other cases it owes the unpaid wages, court costs and usual and necessary attorney's fees [^ia-91a8-intentional-enforcement][^ia-91a8-other-enforcement]. Liquidated damages are five percent of the unpaid wages multiplied by the number of days they remain unpaid, excluding Sundays, legal holidays and the first seven days after the regular payday, capped at the amount of the unpaid wages, and they do not accumulate while the employer is subject to a petition filed in bankruptcy [^ia-91a2-liquidated-enforcement].
On the employee's written complaint, the director of the Department of Inspections, Appeals, and Licensing may determine whether unpaid wages may constitute an enforceable claim; if the director determines that there is an enforceable claim, the director must, with the employee's consent, take an assignment of the claim for the wages and liquidated damages [^ia-91a10-one-year]. The director may not accept a complaint for unpaid wages and liquidated damages more than one year after the wages became due and payable [^ia-91a10-one-year]. An employer that violates chapter 91A or its rules is also subject to a civil money penalty of up to $500 per pay period for each violation [^ia-91a12-civil-penalty].

## What repayment structure is safest under Iowa law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** The safest Iowa repayment term sets an amount the employer can show is reasonable in light of its anticipated or actual loss, such as documented training or recruitment costs, because Iowa refuses an unreasonably large agreed amount as a penalty [^ia-rohlin-test-structure]. *Cedar Valley* upheld an amount that did not exceed the employer's anticipated losses, including recruitment and promotion expenses [^ia-cedar-valley-structure]. The training-reimbursement policy in *Barney* is one example of a schedule that reduces the balance over the commitment period, using a sliding scale [^ia-barney-structure]; as a drafting matter, such a schedule keeps the amount closer to the employer's unrecovered cost.
Collecting the balance by separate demand or action is safer than withholding pay, because section 91A.5 permits a deduction only when law or a court order allows it or under the employee's written authorization for a lawful purpose accruing to the employee's benefit [^ia-91a5-authorization-structure], and an employer that employs 100 or more people in Iowa for hourly wages, as section 91E.1 defines the term, may not deduct relocation costs above twenty dollars [^ia-91a5-relocation-structure][^ia-91e1-employer-structure]. Any deduction for the employer's benefit that is taken should leave pay at or above the $7.25 hourly minimum wage, or the federal minimum wage if it is greater [^ia-91d1-rate-structure][^ia-91d1-floor-structure].

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Virginia[^about]

Virginia has no general stay-or-pay or training-repayment statute, so a repayment term turns on contract law, including the general liquidated-damages test for a sum fixed as damages for leaving early, the non-compete statute expressly preserves prorated cost repayment by health care professionals who leave within five years, and Code § 40.1-29 requires the employee's written and signed authorization before an employer withholds any part of wages.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Virginia? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** No Virginia statute generally governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment agreements in private employment. Where a repayment term fixes an amount as compensation for the breach of leaving early, the Supreme Court of Virginia enforces the amount as liquidated damages when the actual damages were uncertain and difficult to determine when the parties contracted and the amount is not out of all proportion to the probable loss [^va-sop-taylor-liquidated-test]. The one statute located for this guide that speaks to repayment preserves it: Code § 40.1-28.7:8 states that it does not limit agreements requiring a health care professional employed for fewer than five years to repay recruitment-related, education or training costs [^va-sop-hcp-repayment-preserved].

"When the actual damages contemplated at the time of the agreement are uncertain and difficult to determine with exactness and when the amount fixed is not out of all proportion to the probable loss, the amount is deemed to have been intended as enforceable liquidated damages."[^va-sop-taylor-liquidated-test]

The same opinion states the other side of the test: where the damage from a breach is susceptible of definite measurement, or the stipulated amount would be grossly in excess of actual damages, courts usually treat the stipulation as an unenforceable penalty [^va-sop-taylor-penalty]. In *Brooks v. Bankson* the court applied the test and enforced a stipulated amount of 10 percent of a real estate contract price, holding that the sellers' damages from the buyers' breach were not susceptible of definite measurement [^va-sop-brooks-applied]. No Virginia appellate decision located for this guide applies the test to a training-repayment or bonus-repayment clause, or decides whether a promise to return an advance or reimburse an actual expenditure is liquidated damages at all rather than a debt. A repayment amount equal to the employer's documented outlay is arguably a measurable sum rather than an uncertain loss, so the conservative course is to tie the amount to the actual cost and reduce it as the employee serves the commitment.

A repayment term can also be challenged as a restraint on competition. Code § 40.1-28.7:8 defines a covenant not to compete as an agreement that restrains, prohibits or otherwise restricts an individual's ability, after employment ends, to compete with the former employer [^va-sop-covenant-definition], and it bars an employer from entering into, enforcing or threatening to enforce a covenant not to compete with a low-wage employee or a health care professional [^va-sop-covenant-ban]. No Virginia decision located for this guide decides whether a payment owed only if the employee goes to work for a competitor is a covenant not to compete under that definition. The conservative course for an employee covered by the ban is to make any repayment obligation depend on the timing of the departure and not on where the employee works next.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A fixed repayment amount that bears no relation to the loss an early departure causes risks being construed as an unenforceable penalty [^va-sop-taylor-penalty][^va-sop-taylor-liquidated-test].

## Can a Virginia employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Virginia appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a bonus or other payment already made can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. Code § 40.1-29 defines wages to include bonuses and commissions [^va-sop-wages-definition], bars an employer from requiring an employee other than executive personnel to sign an agreement forfeiting wages for time worked as a condition of employment [^va-sop-forfeiture-ban], and bars withholding any part of wages without the employee's written and signed authorization, apart from taxes and deductions made in accordance with law [^va-sop-withholding-authorization-clawback]. The conservative course is to pay every earned amount in full and pursue any repayment separately rather than by keeping back wages.

The forfeiture bar applies to wages for time worked [^va-sop-forfeiture-ban]. No Virginia decision located for this guide decides whether recovering a signing or retention bonus paid in advance and conditioned on future service is a forfeiture of wages for time worked under the section. The statutory definition of wages includes bonuses an employer owes [^va-sop-wages-definition], so the conservative course is to state in the agreement the service condition on which the bonus is earned and never to cancel or withhold a bonus after it has become payable.

## Can a Virginia employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Virginia employer may deduct a repayment from final wages only with the employee's written and signed authorization, because Code § 40.1-29 bars withholding any part of wages without one, apart from taxes and deductions made in accordance with law [^va-sop-withholding-authorization]. Final wages for work performed before separation must be paid on or before the date they would have been paid had employment continued [^va-sop-final-pay-timing].

"No employer shall withhold any part of the wages of any employee except for payroll, wage or withholding taxes or in accordance with law, without the written and signed authorization of the employee."[^va-sop-withholding-authorization]

The statute does not prescribe the authorization's contents, and no Virginia regulation or decision located for this guide does so for repayment deductions. The conservative course is a separate authorization, signed before the deduction, that names the repayment obligation and the amount or method for calculating the balance to be deducted at separation. On each regular pay date, most employers must give a written statement showing the amount and purpose of every deduction [^va-sop-paystub-deductions].

A deduction is also measured against the minimum wage. Virginia's minimum wage is the greater of $12.77 an hour or the federal minimum wage from January 1, 2026, until January 1, 2027 [^va-sop-state-minimum-wage]. Under the federal rule, wages count as paid only when paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^va-sop-flsa-free-and-clear], and the wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^va-sop-flsa-kickback]. The federal rule illustrates the point with employer-required tools: there is a violation in any workweek in which their cost cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^va-sop-flsa-tools-workweek]. No Virginia rule located for this guide addresses whether an authorized repayment deduction may take pay below the state minimum wage, so the conservative course is to keep any deduction from reducing a workweek's pay below the minimum wage or cutting into overtime pay.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment clause that lets the employer deduct the balance from final wages, without the employee's written and signed authorization covering the deduction, exposes the employer to the unpaid wages, an equal amount as liquidated damages and 8 percent annual interest, or triple the wages due if the failure was knowing [^va-sop-withholding-authorization][^va-sop-liquidated-damages][^va-sop-triple-damages]. In an action commenced on or after July 1, 2026, the additional damages are barred if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds and cured the violation within 14 days of notice by paying the wages withheld [^va-sop-good-faith-cure-final-pay].

## Are training or relocation repayments treated differently in Virginia? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Virginia's requirement of the employee's written and signed authorization applies to any deduction from wages for training, tuition or relocation repayment, as it does to any other withholding apart from taxes and deductions made in accordance with law [^va-sop-withholding-authorization-training]. No Virginia statute located for this guide sets a separate rule for those terms outside health care, and a term that fixes an amount as compensation for leaving early is tested under the liquidated-damages rule [^va-sop-taylor-liquidated-test-training]. Employers of health care professionals have an express statutory provision: Code § 40.1-28.7:8 states that it does not limit agreements requiring a health care professional employed for fewer than five years to repay all or a prorated portion of relocation expenses, signing or retention bonuses and recruiting, education or training expenses, and declares those provisions valid and enforceable [^va-sop-hcp-repayment-preserved-training].

The health care provision covers people licensed, registered or certified by the Boards of Medicine, Nursing, Counseling, Optometry, Psychology or Social Work [^va-sop-hcp-definition]. It says the repayment provisions are valid and enforceable but does not say whether wages may be withheld to collect them, so the Code § 40.1-29 authorization requirement still governs any deduction [^va-sop-withholding-authorization-training].

A broader bill was considered in the 2026 session. H.B. 923, as introduced, would have barred an employer from entering into, enforcing or threatening to enforce a stay-or-pay contract with any employee [^va-sop-hb923-ban], subject to exceptions that included repayment of tuition for a transferable credential under a separate, prorated agreement capped at the employer's actual cost [^va-sop-hb923-tuition-exception]. No stay-or-pay section corresponding to the bill was located in the current Code of Virginia for this guide, so this guide does not treat the bill as current law.

## How are Virginia repayment and wage-withholding rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A repayment term that fixes an amount as compensation for leaving early is enforceable as liquidated damages only when the actual damages were uncertain and difficult to determine at contracting and the amount is not out of all proportion to the probable loss [^va-sop-taylor-liquidated-test-enforcement]. An employee whose wages were withheld may sue for the wages owed, an equal amount as liquidated damages, prejudgment interest and reasonable attorney fees and costs, and the court must award triple the wages due if it finds the employer acted knowingly [^va-sop-employee-action], subject to the good-faith-and-cure defense for actions commenced on or after July 1, 2026 [^va-sop-good-faith-cure].

The Commissioner of Labor and Industry may investigate on a complaint from an employee or an interested third party, or on the Commissioner's own initiative [^va-sop-commissioner-investigation], and a knowing failure to pay wages can draw a civil penalty of up to $1,000 for each violation [^va-sop-civil-penalty]. A wage-payment action must be commenced within three years after it accrued [^va-sop-three-year-limit]. In an action to recover unpaid wages commenced on or after July 1, 2026, an employer that shows good faith and reasonable grounds avoids additional damages and penalties, but only if it cures the violation within 14 days of notice by paying all wages unlawfully withheld [^va-sop-good-faith-cure]. An employee or health care professional may sue a former employer that attempts to enforce a covenant not to compete in violation of Code § 40.1-28.7:8, and the court may void the covenant and award liquidated damages, lost compensation, damages and reasonable attorney fees and costs [^va-sop-covenant-action].

## What repayment structure is safest under Virginia law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A stipulated repayment amount is enforceable as liquidated damages when the employer's damages from an early departure were uncertain and difficult to determine at contracting and the amount is not out of all proportion to the probable loss [^va-sop-taylor-liquidated-test-safest]. As a drafting recommendation, not an assured result, tying the amount to the employer's actual cost and reducing it as the employee serves the commitment answers the proportionality concern, although no Virginia decision located for this guide decides whether repayment of a measurable expenditure is liquidated damages at all. For a health care professional, a repayment of all or a prorated portion of recruitment, relocation, bonus or training costs by one employed fewer than five years is expressly valid and enforceable [^va-sop-hcp-repayment-preserved-safest]. Any deduction from wages, including final wages, needs the employee's written and signed authorization [^va-sop-withholding-authorization-safest], and where no authorization covers the balance, paying final wages in full on time and suing on the agreement avoids liability for the withheld wages plus an equal amount as liquidated damages and interest [^va-sop-liquidated-damages-safest].

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Wisconsin[^about]

Wisconsin has no stay-or-pay statute, so a repayment term turns on contract law: the Wisconsin Supreme Court tests a stipulated sum for reasonableness, and the Seventh Circuit has held that a training-repayment obligation not tied to competition falls outside the restrictive-covenant statute, Wis. Stat. § 103.465. Wis. Stat. § 103.455 bars wage deductions for faulty workmanship, loss, theft or damage unless the employee authorizes them in writing, the employer and an employee-designated representative find the employee at fault, or a court finds the employee liable, and makes an employer that violates it liable for twice the amount deducted.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Wisconsin? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** No Wisconsin statute addresses stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment agreements in private employment.
Where a repayment term stipulates damages for breaching a commitment to stay, the Wisconsin Supreme Court applies a reasonableness test that respects the parties' bargain but prevents abuse [^wi-sop-wass-test], and a stipulated amount that deviates significantly from readily ascertainable damages will appear unreasonable [^wi-sop-wass-ascertainable]. In *Heder v. City of Two Rivers* the Seventh Circuit, applying Wisconsin law, held that an obligation to repay paramedic training costs on leaving within three years was not a covenant not to compete, because it applied whether the employee went back to school, changed occupation or retired [^wi-sop-heder-not-covenant].

"But the agreement between Two Rivers and the firefighters’ union does not restrict Heder’s ability to compete against the City after leaving its employ. The obligation is unconditional: a firefighter departing before three years have expired must repay training costs even if he goes back to school, changes occupation, or retires. Competition has nothing to do with the matter."[^wi-sop-heder-not-covenant]

The restrictive-covenant statute makes a covenant not to compete enforceable only if its restrictions are reasonably necessary to protect the employer, and voids an unreasonable covenant entirely [^wi-sop-noncompete-statute]. *Heder* read Wisconsin decisions as applying that statute only where a consequence is linked to working for a competitor [^wi-sop-heder-competitor-link], so a repayment obligation triggered by joining a competitor, rather than by leaving early for any reason, risks being judged as a covenant not to compete. The party seeking to set aside a stipulated damages clause bears the burden of proving facts that justify not enforcing it [^wi-sop-wass-burden]. A term framed as a training loan forgiven over the commitment period may instead be treated as a debt rather than stipulated damages: *Heder* reasoned that an employer-paid training cost repayable on early departure is the economic equivalent of such a loan [^wi-sop-heder-loan]. No Wisconsin appellate decision located for this guide decides whether the stipulated-damages test applies to repayment of a training loan or advance.

Wisconsin's work recruitment rule defines a purchase or investment as any arrangement involving a direct or indirect payment from a recruit to a recruiter [^wi-sop-atcp-purchase]. A work advertisement must clearly disclose the nature and amount of every purchase or investment a recruit must make to obtain a work offer [^wi-sop-atcp-disclosure], and a required purchase or investment must be documented in writing, with a copy given to the recruit before the recruit agrees to it [^wi-sop-atcp-documented]. No Wisconsin appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a repayment obligation that a recruit must sign to obtain a work offer is a purchase or investment under the rule. The conservative course is to disclose any repayment obligation, and its amount, in the job advertisement and to give the recruit the written terms before the recruit commits.

*Heder* is a federal decision, and the Wisconsin Court of Appeals has said it is not bound by federal court decisions that construe Wisconsin law in civil cases [^wi-sop-farady-not-bound]. No Wisconsin appellate decision located for this guide applies the reasonableness test or § 103.465 to a training, signing-bonus or relocation repayment term. The conservative course is to tie the repayment amount to the employer's documented cost, reduce it as the employee serves the commitment, and make it payable on early departure for any reason rather than on going to work for a competitor.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment obligation triggered by going to work for a competitor risks being treated as a covenant not to compete, which is void in its entirety if any part of the restraint is unreasonable [^wi-sop-heder-competitor-link][^wi-sop-noncompete-statute].

## Can a Wisconsin employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Wisconsin appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a signing, retention or relocation bonus already paid can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. The wage payment statute requires an employee who quits or is discharged, other than a sales agent employed on commission or an employee with a written contract for a definite period, to be paid in full by the next regular payday or the date the monthly payment rule requires, whichever is earlier [^wi-sop-final-pay-clawback] [^wi-sop-monthly-clawback], and, except for its pay-frequency subsection, bars an employer from securing an exemption from that section by special contract or other means [^wi-sop-no-contract-exemption-clawback]. The conservative course is to pay every earned amount in full and pursue any repayment separately rather than by keeping back wages.

Wis. Stat. § 103.455 separately bars deductions from wages due or earned for faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property or damage to property unless the employee authorizes the deduction in writing, a joint determination finds the employee at fault, or a court finds the employee liable [^wi-sop-deduction-bar-clawback]. The Wisconsin Supreme Court has explained that the statute does not stop an employer from shifting costs to an employee but protects against an employer's arbitrary determination that pay is not due because of losses attributed to the employee [^wi-sop-erdman-purpose-clawback]. No Wisconsin decision located for this guide decides whether withholding a bonus repayment is a deduction for a loss under § 103.455. The conservative course is to state in the agreement the service condition on which a bonus is earned, and not to describe a repayment as compensation for a loss the employee caused.

## Can a Wisconsin employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** No Wisconsin statute expressly permits or forbids deducting a training or bonus repayment from final pay.
An employee who quits or is discharged, other than a sales agent employed on commission or an employee with a written contract for a definite period, must be paid in full by the next regular payday or the date the monthly payment rule requires, whichever is earlier [^wi-sop-final-pay-fp] [^wi-sop-monthly-fp]. In *Heder* the Seventh Circuit held that withholding final pay under a valid repayment term in a collective bargaining agreement did not violate that requirement, because the statute does not prevent agreements that reduce what payment in full means [^wi-sop-heder-final-pay-fp]. The employer in *Heder* conceded that the employee was entitled to retain at least the statutory minimum wage for his final two pay periods and time and a half for overtime hours where federal law requires that premium, leaving the employer to collect any residue as an ordinary creditor [^wi-sop-heder-min-wage-fp].

"This statute does not prevent employees from striking agreements that reduce what ‘in full’ means, and as we have held that the collective bargaining agreement’s repayment proviso is valid there is no problem under § 109.03(2)."[^wi-sop-heder-final-pay-fp]

*Heder* involved a repayment term bargained by a union, and Wisconsin courts are not bound by the federal court's reading of state law [^wi-sop-farady-not-bound-fp]. The wage payment statute also bars an employer from securing an exemption from it by special contract [^wi-sop-no-contract-exemption-fp]. No Wisconsin appellate decision located for this guide decides whether an individual employee's signed repayment agreement permits withholding from final pay.

A deduction for faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property or damage to property needs the employee's written authorization, a joint determination of fault, or a court finding [^wi-sop-deduction-bar-fp], and the Department of Workforce Development states that the written authorization must come after the incident and before any deduction [^wi-sop-dwd-after-incident-fp]. An employer that deducts in breach of § 103.455 is liable for twice the amount, and any contrary agreement is void [^wi-sop-deduction-double-fp]. A training or bonus repayment is not on the statute's list, but a repayment clause that describes the amount as damages or a loss the employee caused invites the argument that it falls within it.

Wisconsin's wage assignment statute recognizes an employee's authorization directing future deductions for any indebtedness to the employer, and exempts such an authorization from its spousal-signature and six-month limits [^wi-sop-assignment-indebtedness-fp]. Any deduction is also measured against the minimum wage, which every wage paid must meet [^wi-sop-min-wage-fp]. Under the federal rule, wages count as paid only when paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^wi-sop-flsa-free-clear-fp], and the wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer [^wi-sop-flsa-kickback-fp]. The conservative course is to take any deduction only under a separate written authorization the employee signs for a debt owed to the employer, to keep each deduction from reducing pay below the minimum wage or cutting into overtime pay, and otherwise to collect the balance by demand or suit.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment clause that purports to let the employer deduct for faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property or damage to property without the employee's written authorization, a joint determination of fault or a court finding is void as contrary to § 103.455, and deductions taken under it expose the employer to twice the amount deducted [^wi-sop-deduction-bar-fp][^wi-sop-deduction-double-fp].

## Are training or relocation repayments treated differently in Wisconsin? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Wisconsin statute addresses training, tuition or relocation repayment separately from bonus repayment.
The leading decision on training is *Heder*, in which the Seventh Circuit rejected the view that Wisconsin law requires a training-repayment obligation to decline as time passes and enforced full repayment of the employee's books and tuition [^wi-sop-heder-no-proration-tr]. The court treated the obligation as outside the restrictive-covenant statute because it did not depend on whether the employee competed [^wi-sop-heder-not-covenant-tr].

"We do not think that the Supreme Court of Wisconsin is apt to require employers and employees to amortize training costs with precision, to factor in the time value of money (the agreement does not require Heder to pay interest, though it might have done so), or to craft an individual schedule based on the number of years each employee is expected to remain able to work. The collective bargaining agreement is valid under state law, so Heder must repay the full cost of his books and tuition, which came to about $1,400."[^wi-sop-heder-no-proration-tr]

*Heder* declined to require employers and employees to amortize training costs with precision [^wi-sop-heder-no-proration-tr]. Wisconsin courts are not bound by the federal court's view [^wi-sop-farady-not-bound-tr], and a stipulated amount that deviates significantly from readily ascertainable damages will appear unreasonable [^wi-sop-wass-ascertainable-tr]. A training cost is usually documented, so, as drafting advice, the conservative course is to set the repayment at no more than that cost and reduce it as the commitment is served, even though *Heder* did not require proration.

## How are Wisconsin repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employer that makes a deduction not in accordance with Wis. Stat. § 103.455 is liable for twice the amount in a civil action brought by the employee [^wi-sop-deduction-double-enf]. An employee not paid in full may sue in any court of competent jurisdiction for the wages due and increased wages [^wi-sop-private-action-enf], and the court may order increased wages of not more than 50 percent of the unpaid wages in an action commenced before the Department of Workforce Development completes its investigation and settlement attempts [^wi-sop-increased-early-enf], or not more than 100 percent in an action commenced afterwards [^wi-sop-increased-late-enf].

In a wage claim proceeding the court may allow the prevailing party a reasonable sum for expenses in addition to all other costs [^wi-sop-expenses-enf]. An action to recover unpaid wages must generally be commenced within 2 years after the cause of action accrues [^wi-sop-two-years-enf]. On the employer's side, a repayment claim is a contract claim, and the employee who seeks to avoid a stipulated sum bears the burden of proving facts that justify not enforcing it [^wi-sop-wass-burden-enf].

## What repayment structure is safest under Wisconsin law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** As a drafting recommendation rather than an assured result, the repayment amount should match the employer's documented cost and decline as the employee serves the commitment, because a stipulated amount that deviates significantly from readily ascertainable damages will appear unreasonable [^wi-sop-wass-ascertainable-safest]. The obligation should apply on early departure for any reason, not on going to work for a competitor, so that it stays outside the restrictive-covenant statute as *Heder* read it [^wi-sop-heder-competitor-link-safest]. Final wages should be paid in full on time [^wi-sop-final-pay-safest], with any deduction taken only under a separate written authorization for a debt owed to the employer [^wi-sop-assignment-indebtedness-safest], with any deduction for faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property or damage to property made only on a basis § 103.455 permits [^wi-sop-deduction-bar-safest], and never reducing pay below the minimum wage [^wi-sop-min-wage-safest]. Where signing a repayment obligation is a condition of a work offer, disclosing its nature and amount in the job advertisement [^wi-sop-atcp-disclosure-safest] and giving the recruit the written terms before the recruit agrees [^wi-sop-atcp-documented-safest] addresses the work recruitment rule; any balance not covered is then a matter for demand or suit.

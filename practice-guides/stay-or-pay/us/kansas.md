# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Kansas[^about]

Kansas has no statute that limits stay-or-pay terms, and Kansas courts enforce a stipulated amount owed for leaving early only if it is reasonable and the damages are hard to measure; the Kansas Wage Payment Act separately bars withholding earned wages as liquidated damages, allows a deduction to repay an employment-related loan or advance under a signed written agreement or, from final wages, after written notice and explanation, and keeps every deduction above the minimum wage.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Kansas? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Kansas courts enforce a contract term that fixes what an employee owes for leaving early if it is a valid liquidated damages clause rather than a penalty [^ks-sop-dewerff-employment-clauses-enforceable][^ks-sop-dewerff-reasonable-and-difficult-test]. In *Unified School District No. 315 v. DeWerff* the Kansas Court of Appeals stated that valid liquidated damages provisions are generally enforceable in employment contracts when the employee wrongfully ends the employment [^ks-sop-dewerff-employment-clauses-enforceable], and that a stipulated amount is valid if it is reasonable and the damages are difficult to ascertain [^ks-sop-dewerff-reasonable-and-difficult-test]. No Kansas statute located for this guide limits stay-or-pay terms between an employer and an employee, and no Kansas appellate decision located for this guide rules on a training, signing-bonus or relocation repayment agreement. The Kansas Supreme Court has stated that employment contract terms that are not contrary to law or unreasonable should be enforced [^ks-sop-weinzirl-contract-terms-honored].

"Where actual damages are readily ascertainable, a provision purporting to be a liquidated damages clause will be construed as a penalty, with the injured party recovering only those damages established by proof."[^ks-sop-dewerff-ascertainable-damages-penalty]

The penalty test is the main risk for a repayment term. A penalty is meant to secure performance, while liquidated damages are a sum paid in place of performance [^ks-sop-dewerff-penalty-secures-performance], and a provision is generally a penalty when no attempt was made to calculate the damages a breach might cause [^ks-sop-dewerff-no-calculation-penalty]. The *DeWerff* contract required a larger payment as the school year began and progressed [^ks-sop-dewerff-escalating-sum], and the court treated that as an attempt to estimate the district's damages. *DeWerff* involved a public school district and a negotiated teacher contract, not a private employer's training or bonus repayment term.

The penalty test applies to a repayment term that works as stipulated damages for leaving early; no Kansas decision located for this guide decides whether a term framed as repayment of an actual advance is tested the same way. A training or relocation repayment also differs from the *DeWerff* clause in one respect that matters under the penalty test: the employer usually knows what it spent. Because a Kansas court limits recovery to proven damages where actual damages are readily ascertainable [^ks-sop-dewerff-ascertainable-damages-penalty], the conservative course is to state the amount as repayment of the employer's documented cost and reduce it as the employee serves the commitment, rather than to fix a larger sum meant to discourage leaving.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A flat repayment amount that exceeds what the employer spent and does not decline with service risks being treated as a penalty, limiting the employer to the damages it can prove [^ks-sop-dewerff-no-calculation-penalty][^ks-sop-dewerff-ascertainable-damages-penalty].

## Can a Kansas employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** A Kansas employer may not keep an employee's earned wages as liquidated damages for leaving. In *Weinzirl v. The Wells Group* the contract provided that commissions the employer received after the employee quit would be forfeited as liquidated damages [^ks-sop-weinzirl-forfeiture-clause-clawback], and the Kansas Supreme Court held that the provision violated Kansas law and could not be upheld [^ks-sop-weinzirl-liquidated-damages-void-clawback]. The Wage Payment Act defines wages as compensation for labor or services, however the amount is determined [^ks-sop-wages-definition-clawback], and its rights cannot be waived except as K.S.A. 44-324 provides [^ks-sop-wage-act-no-waiver-clawback]. No Kansas appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a signing, retention or relocation bonus already paid can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term.

"Thus, the employment contract provision which allowed respondent to withhold claimant's earned commission as liquidated damages was in contravention of the law and could not be upheld."[^ks-sop-weinzirl-liquidated-damages-void-clawback]

*Weinzirl* concerned wages the employer withheld, not money it had already paid out. A bonus or relocation payment made in advance on the employee's written promise to repay it if the employee leaves early is closer to the loan or advance that K.S.A. 44-319(b) lets an employer recover by deduction under a signed written agreement [^ks-sop-written-agreement-loan-advance-clawback]. No Kansas decision located for this guide applies that subsection to a signing, retention or relocation bonus. The conservative course is to state the repayment obligation in a written agreement signed before payment, with the amount and the service period, and not to make the payment depend on forfeiting wages the employee has already earned.

## Can a Kansas employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Kansas employer may deduct repayment of an employment-related loan or advance from wages, including final wages, under a signed written agreement [^ks-sop-written-agreement-loan-advance-final-pay], but no deduction may take pay below the applicable minimum wage [^ks-sop-deductions-minimum-wage-floor-final-pay]. Otherwise an employer may not withhold, deduct or divert any part of an employee's wages unless the law requires or empowers it, the deduction is for certain medical care without financial benefit to the employer, the employee signed an authorization for a lawful purpose accruing to the employee's benefit, or the deduction is for an automatic retirement-plan enrollment [^ks-sop-deduction-permitted-cases-final-pay]. Under a signed written agreement, an employer may also deduct wages to let the employee repay a loan or advance the employer made during the course of and within the scope of employment [^ks-sop-written-agreement-loan-advance-final-pay]. From final wages specifically, an employer may also deduct to let the employee repay such a loan or advance after giving written notice and an explanation [^ks-sop-final-wage-loan-advance-notice-final-pay]. No deduction may reduce wages below the applicable federal or Kansas minimum wage [^ks-sop-deductions-minimum-wage-floor-final-pay]. Whether the employee quits or is discharged, earned wages are due by the next regular payday [^ks-sop-final-pay-next-payday-final-pay].

"(b) Subject to the provisions of subsection (e), pursuant to a signed written agreement between the employer and employee, an employer may withhold, deduct or divert any portion of an employee's wages for the following purposes: (1) To allow the employee to repay a loan or advance which the employer made to the employee during the course of and within the scope of employment; (2) to allow for recovery of payroll overpayment; and (3) to compensate the employer for the replacement cost or unpaid balance of the cost of the employer's merchandise or uniforms purchased by the employee."[^ks-sop-written-agreement-loan-advance-final-pay]

No Kansas decision located for this guide decides whether training costs or a signing or relocation bonus count as a loan or advance under K.S.A. 44-319(b) or (c). A signed authorization does not by itself make a deduction lawful. In *Excel Corp. v. Kansas Department of Human Resources* the employee signed a card authorizing the employer to withhold the cost of replacement equipment from his pay [^ks-sop-excel-signed-purchase-card-final-pay], and the Court of Appeals affirmed the agency's ruling that the deduction was for the employer's burglary loss and was not authorized under K.S.A. 44-319 and K.A.R. 49-20-1 [^ks-sop-excel-burglary-loss-not-authorized-final-pay]. The same final-wage subsection lets an employer, after written notice and explanation, withhold final wages to recover employer property until it is returned [^ks-sop-final-wage-property-notice-final-pay]; it does not mention training costs or bonuses by name.

The minimum wage floor depends on coverage. The Kansas minimum wage is $7.25 an hour [^ks-sop-minimum-wage-hourly-rate-final-pay], but that section does not apply to employers and employees covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act [^ks-sop-minimum-wage-flsa-carveout-final-pay], whose wages count as paid only when paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^ks-sop-flsa-free-and-clear-final-pay]. Federal wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^ks-sop-flsa-kickback-final-pay], and the federal example treats a required employee expense for the employer's benefit as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages the Act requires [^ks-sop-flsa-tools-example-final-pay].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> Withholding a disputed repayment balance from final wages risks a penalty of up to 100% of the unpaid wages if the withholding is found willful and the deduction does not fit K.S.A. 44-319 [^ks-sop-willful-nonpayment-penalty-final-pay][^ks-sop-deduction-permitted-cases-final-pay].

## Are training or relocation repayments treated differently in Kansas? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Kansas statute located for this guide treats an employee's training, tuition or relocation repayment differently from bonus repayment. The one Kansas training-cost statute located for this guide runs between public employers rather than against the employee: K.S.A. 74-5609a(b) requires a city, county or state agency that hires a police or law enforcement officer within one year after the officer completes training at a state or local law enforcement training school to reimburse the agency that paid for the training, including tuition, salary, travel and incidental expenses [^ks-sop-police-training-agency-reimbursement-training]. Where an employee's repayment term works as stipulated damages for leaving early, the ordinary liquidated damages rules apply [^ks-sop-dewerff-reasonable-and-difficult-test-training], and any collection from wages must fit K.S.A. 44-319 [^ks-sop-written-agreement-loan-advance-training][^ks-sop-final-wage-loan-advance-notice-training].

"(b) Any city, county or state agency which commences employment of a police officer or law enforcement officer within one year of the time such police officer or law enforcement officer has completed a course of instruction at a state or local law enforcement training school shall reimburse the city, county or state agency which paid the tuition for training such officer. This reimbursement shall include the amount of the tuition paid, the officer's salary and travel expenses and any other expenses incurred which were incidental to training such officer."[^ks-sop-police-training-agency-reimbursement-training]

No Kansas appellate decision located for this guide addresses a private employer's training, tuition or relocation repayment agreement. Because the employer's training or relocation cost is usually documented, a repayment amount well above that cost risks the rule that a purported liquidated damages clause is a penalty where actual damages are readily ascertainable [^ks-sop-dewerff-ascertainable-damages-penalty-training].

## How are Kansas repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A Kansas employer can enforce a valid stipulated repayment amount by suing on the contract [^ks-sop-dewerff-judgment-affirmed-enforcement]. In *DeWerff* the school district sued when a teacher refused to make the payment his contract required [^ks-sop-dewerff-district-sued-enforcement], and the Court of Appeals affirmed the judgment that the provision was an enforceable liquidated damages clause [^ks-sop-dewerff-judgment-affirmed-enforcement]. An employee who says a repayment was wrongly taken from wages can bring a Wage Payment Act claim in any court of competent jurisdiction [^ks-sop-wage-act-suit-any-court-enforcement], and for a valid claim under $10,000 the secretary of labor must, on the employee's written request, take an assignment of the claim and act to enforce it [^ks-sop-secretary-wage-claim-assignment-enforcement]. An employer that willfully fails to pay final wages on time owes the wages plus a penalty of 1% of the unpaid wages for each day, except Sundays and legal holidays, after the eighth day, capped at 100% of the unpaid wages [^ks-sop-willful-nonpayment-penalty-enforcement].

In a dispute over the amount owed, the employer must pay the wages it concedes are due, without conditions, by the next regular payday [^ks-sop-conceded-wages-next-payday-enforcement], and unless payment is made under a binding settlement agreement, a release required as a condition of that payment is null and void [^ks-sop-conceded-payment-no-release-enforcement]. An action on a liability created by statute, other than a penalty, must be brought within three years [^ks-sop-three-year-statutory-liability-enforcement], and an action on a statutory penalty within one year [^ks-sop-one-year-statutory-penalty-enforcement].

## What repayment structure is safest under Kansas law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** Under Kansas law a repayment amount that works as stipulated damages for leaving early is enforceable only if it is reasonable and the damages are difficult to ascertain [^ks-sop-dewerff-reasonable-and-difficult-test-safest]. In *DeWerff* the court found an attempt to calculate damages in a payment schedule tied to how hard the teacher would be to replace as the school year progressed [^ks-sop-dewerff-escalating-sum-safest], and a Kansas court limits recovery to proven damages where actual damages are readily ascertainable [^ks-sop-dewerff-ascertainable-damages-penalty-safest]. As a drafting recommendation rather than a rule from a Kansas decision, the conservative structure is a written agreement signed before the employer pays for the training, bonus or relocation, stating the amount and the service period, with an amount that tracks the employer's documented cost and declines as the employee serves the commitment. The agreement should frame the payment as an advance to be repaid, not as a forfeiture of earned pay, because the Kansas Supreme Court refused to let an employer withhold earned wages as liquidated damages [^ks-sop-weinzirl-liquidated-damages-void-safest]. Any deduction should rest on a signed written agreement for repayment of a loan or advance [^ks-sop-written-agreement-loan-advance-safest], a final-wage deduction should follow written notice and an explanation [^ks-sop-final-wage-loan-advance-notice-safest], and should not take pay below the applicable minimum wage [^ks-sop-deductions-minimum-wage-floor-safest]. Where the balance is disputed, paying final wages in full by the next regular payday [^ks-sop-final-pay-next-payday-safest] and pursuing the balance separately avoids the willful-nonpayment penalty [^ks-sop-willful-nonpayment-penalty-safest].

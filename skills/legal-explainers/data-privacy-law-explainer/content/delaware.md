# Delaware Consumer Privacy Law (DPDPA)[^about]

The Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act gives Delaware residents rights over their personal data and imposes notice, contracting, and consent duties on controllers above unusually low thresholds — it reaches early-stage startups and most nonprofits, is enforced exclusively by the Delaware Department of Justice, and provides no private right of action.

## Does the Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act apply to your business? {#does-dpdpa-apply}

**Short answer.** The Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act (DPDPA) applies to a person doing business in Delaware or targeting its residents only if, in the prior calendar year, it met one of two consumer-count thresholds [^stat-103-apply]. Those thresholds are controlling or processing the personal data of at least 35,000 consumers, not counting data processed solely to complete a payment transaction, or of at least 10,000 consumers while deriving more than 20% of gross revenue from selling personal data [^stat-103-apply]. From January 1, 2027, amendments enacted in 2026 (85 Del. Laws c. 463) lower those thresholds to 10,000 consumers, still excluding payment-only data, or 5,000 consumers while deriving more than 20% of gross revenue from selling personal data, and bring within the law's scope third parties that acquire personal data from a controller [^dpdpa-2027-applicability-thresholds].

The thresholds here are among the lowest in the country, and there is no dollar revenue floor — so an early-stage, pre-revenue startup that simply touches 35,000 Delaware residents is covered. Because the amended test also looks back to the preceding calendar year, a business's 2026 figures decide whether the lower thresholds cover it on January 1, 2027; the payment-only exclusion applies to the 10,000-consumer threshold but not to the 5,000-consumer threshold tied to revenue from selling personal data [^dpdpa-2027-applicability-thresholds]. Delaware is also unusually broad on nonprofits: rather than exempting them as a class, it carves out only a nonprofit dedicated exclusively to preventing and addressing insurance crime, so most charitable and trade organizations fall within the law. A consumer is a Delaware resident acting in a personal or household context, not an employee or a business-to-business contact. State bodies and political subdivisions, other than institutions of higher education, are excluded, and until January 1, 2027 so are financial institutions and affiliates subject to Title V of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act [^dpdpa-financial-institution-exemption-current]. From that date, those state bodies stay excluded, again other than institutions of higher education, but the financial-sector entity exemptions instead name particular kinds of firm, including insurers and federal or state-chartered banks, credit unions, and savings associations, together with their affiliates principally engaged in financial activities [^dpdpa-2027-financial-entity-exemptions]. Certain federally regulated data also stays outside the law, including protected health information under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) [^dpdpa-excluded-health-research-data] and data subject to Title V of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act [^dpdpa-excluded-glba-data].

## What must your Delaware privacy policy contain? {#privacy-policy-contents}

**Short answer.** A controller must provide a reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful privacy notice that lists the categories of personal data processed, the purpose for processing, how consumers exercise and appeal their rights, the categories of personal data shared with third parties, the categories of those third parties, and a way to contact the controller [^stat-106-notice]. From January 1, 2027, the notice must also be reasonably particular to the product or service offered, identify the controller, and describe the consumer's personal data rights under § 12D-104(a) [^dpdpa-2027-privacy-notice-contents].

Section 12D-106(c) is the content checklist for a Delaware privacy policy, and a policy that will still be in use on January 1, 2027 needs the three added items by then. The DPDPA also requires data minimization — collection limited to what is adequate, relevant, and reasonably necessary — and, where a controller sells personal data or processes it for targeted advertising, a clear and conspicuous disclosure of that practice and how to opt out. As of January 1, 2026, the opt-out mechanism must also honor a universal opt-out preference signal such as Global Privacy Control. The notice the policy presents should match the data practices the controller actually carries out.

## What must your contracts with processors say? {#vendor-contracts}

**Short answer.** A binding contract must govern any processor's handling of personal data on the controller's behalf — so a data processing agreement is a statutory requirement, not a best practice — and that contract must set out the processing instructions, the nature and purpose of processing, the type of data, the duration, and the rights and obligations of both parties [^stat-107-contract].

Section 12D-107(b) then layers in the required processor commitments: a duty of confidentiality for everyone handling the data, deletion or return of data at the controller's direction, the information needed to demonstrate compliance, advance notice before engaging a subcontractor (which must be bound by written contract to the same obligations), and cooperation with reasonable assessments. From January 1, 2027, the contract must also identify each limited and specific purpose for which the processor processes personal data, described with specificity rather than by a general reference to the whole contract, and the processor's compliance-information term will extend to cooperating with the controller's due-diligence assessments [^dpdpa-2027-processor-contract-terms]. A compliant template DPA tracks each of these, and one in use from January 1, 2027 also needs the purpose clause and the due-diligence term.

The amendments also reach recipients that are not processors. From January 1, 2027, a controller must enter into binding contracts with third parties to which it discloses personal data, including in a sale or for targeted advertising, limiting the data to specified purposes and obligating the third party to comply with the chapter [^dpdpa-2027-third-party-contracts]. It must also conduct reasonable due diligence on those third parties, at a minimum through questionnaires and review of their relevant documents [^dpdpa-2027-third-party-due-diligence]. A third party that receives personal data without a required contract may not further process it, and a third party must comply with any required contract and supply the information the controller needs for its assessments [^dpdpa-2027-third-party-duties].

## Do you need consent to process sensitive data? {#sensitive-data}

**Short answer.** A controller may not process a consumer's sensitive data without first obtaining consent, and for a known child it must instead obtain a parent's or guardian's consent and otherwise comply with Delaware's children's-data provisions [^stat-106-consent]. Sensitive data covers data revealing race or ethnicity, religious beliefs, a mental or physical health condition or diagnosis, sex life, sexual orientation, transgender or nonbinary status, or citizenship or immigration status; genetic or biometric data; the personal data of a known child; and precise geolocation [^stat-102-sensitive]. From January 1, 2027, the definition will also reach national origin, health treatment or status, neural data, financial account numbers or log-in credentials that would allow access to an account, government-issued identification numbers that the law does not require to be publicly displayed, and inferences used to reveal or identify any listed category [^dpdpa-2027-sensitive-data-definition].

This is the opt-in model that the Virginia-family privacy laws share — consent must be a freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous affirmative act, and it cannot be manufactured through buried terms of use or dark patterns. Delaware also goes further than some peers for teenagers: a controller cannot run targeted advertising on, or sell the data of, a consumer it knows to be at least 13 but under 18 without that consumer's consent.

Amendments enacted in 2026 tighten these rules from January 1, 2027. Processing sensitive data will then require both the consumer's consent and processing that is reasonably necessary and proportionate to the disclosed purposes, and a controller that knows or wilfully disregards that a consumer is a child will need a parent's or guardian's consent before processing any of that child's personal data, not only sensitive data [^dpdpa-2027-sensitive-data-processing]. A controller will also be barred from disclosing sensitive data in a sale of personal data unless the disclosure is strictly necessary for a product or service the consumer affirmatively requested, the controller gives clear and conspicuous prior notice of the sensitive-data categories, purpose, and recipients, the consumer consents, and the controller keeps a record of that consent for 5 years and provides it with any data protection assessment [^dpdpa-2027-sensitive-data-sale-limits].

## Can a consumer sue your business under the DPDPA? {#consumer-lawsuit}

**Short answer.** No. The DPDPA gives the Delaware Department of Justice exclusive enforcement authority and expressly forecloses any private right of action [^stat-111-enforce]. The law's right-to-cure was temporary: through the end of 2025 the Department had to issue a notice and allow 60 days to fix a curable violation, but that mandatory cure period applied only to the period ending December 31, 2025 [^stat-111-cure].

Unlike Virginia's permanent cure period, Delaware's sunset on January 1, 2026. Since then, whether to offer a controller a chance to cure is left to the Department's discretion, weighed against factors such as the number of violations and the likelihood of injury to the public — so a covered business can no longer count on a guaranteed off-ramp. The practical posture is to build the notice, consent, and contracting controls up front rather than rely on a cure window that may not be offered.

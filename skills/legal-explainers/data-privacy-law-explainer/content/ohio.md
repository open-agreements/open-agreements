# Ohio Consumer Privacy Law[^about]

Ohio has no comprehensive consumer-privacy law. The main Ohio rules are the 45-day breach-notice law (Ohio Rev. Code § 1349.19), which only the Attorney General enforces, the Consumer Sales Practices Act, and the chapter 1354 cybersecurity safe harbor. Federal law adds the FTC Act ban on deceptive practices and sector rules under GLBA, HIPAA, and COPPA.

## Which privacy laws apply to your business in Ohio? {#which-privacy-laws-apply}

**Short answer.** Our review found no comprehensive Ohio consumer-privacy law; instead, Ohio law requires notice of qualifying data breaches, bans deceptive acts in consumer transactions, and gives a lawsuit defense to businesses with a qualifying cybersecurity program [^q1-breach-duty] [^q1-cspa-deception] [^q1-dpa-defense].
The breach-notice law requires a person or business that owns or licenses computerized personal information to notify Ohio residents of a qualifying data breach [^q1-breach-duty]; some health-care and financial organizations are exempt [^q1-exempt-hipaa] [^q1-exempt-fi]. The Ohio Data Protection Act gives a business with a qualifying written cybersecurity program a defense to tort claims that blame a data breach on weak security [^q1-dpa-defense]. Federal law adds the FTC Act, GLBA for financial institutions, HIPAA for covered health entities, and COPPA for websites and online services directed to children [^q1-ftc5] [^q1-glba-notice] [^q1-hipaa-notice] [^q1-coppa-notice].
A multistate business must also comply with each other state's privacy law that applies to it; the [50-state consumer privacy survey](/surveys/privacy/us) compares them.

Our review found no Ohio statute that gives residents a general right to see, delete, correct, or move their personal data, or to opt out of data sales or targeted ads. It found no universal opt-out-signal rule and no general duty to give notice at collection, get consent, or run data-protection assessments.

Two narrower Ohio rules also apply:

- **Insurance companies.** Each insurance licensee must develop, implement, and maintain a written information security program based on its own risk assessment [^q1-insurance-program].
- **Payment records.** A narrow rule limits recording a credit card number when a customer pays by check, and a phone number or Social Security number when a customer pays by card or check [^q1-payment-recording].

## What must your Ohio privacy policy contain? {#privacy-policy-contents}

**Short answer.** No Ohio statute requires a general consumer privacy policy or sets its contents, but a policy that misstates the business's data practices can be a deceptive act under FTC Act § 5 and, in a consumer transaction, under Ohio's Consumer Sales Practices Act [^q2-ftc5] [^q2-cspa-deception] [^q2-cspa-benefits].
Some federal sector laws require specific privacy notices [^q2-hipaa-notice] [^q2-glba-notice].

- **Health care.** A HIPAA covered entity must tell people how it may use and disclose their health information, and what their rights and the entity's duties are [^q2-hipaa-notice].
- **Financial institutions.** A financial institution may not share nonpublic personal information with an unaffiliated third party unless it first gives the consumer a notice that meets GLBA's rules [^q2-glba-notice].
- **Children's services.** An operator of a website or online service directed to children under 13 that collects their personal information must give notice of its information practices under COPPA's rules [^q2-coppa-notice].

Ohio courts must give great weight to FTC orders and rules and to federal court decisions under the FTC Act when they construe § 1345.02(A), the Act's general ban on unfair or deceptive acts [^q2-cspa-ftc-construction].

## What must your contracts with vendors say? {#vendor-contracts}

**Short answer.** No Ohio statute sets general data terms for vendor contracts, but Ohio law requires a vendor that stores computerized personal information for another business to notify that business of a breach in an expeditious manner [^q3-custodian-notice].
Ohio insurance law also puts a vendor duty on insurance licensees, and some federal sector laws require contract terms [^q3-insurance-vendor] [^q3-glba-safeguards] [^q3-hipaa-baa].

- **Ohio insurance law.** An insurance licensee must require its third-party service providers to protect the systems and nonpublic information they hold or can access [^q3-insurance-vendor].
- **GLBA Safeguards Rule.** A financial institution must oversee its service providers, including by requiring them by contract to keep appropriate safeguards [^q3-glba-safeguards].
- **HIPAA.** A covered entity needs a written business-associate contract that limits how the vendor uses and discloses health information, requires safeguards and breach reporting, and passes the same limits down to subcontractors [^q3-hipaa-baa].

A vendor that is slow to share facts after the business discovers or is notified of a breach uses up the business's 45 days to notify residents [^q3-timing].

## When must you notify people of a data breach in Ohio? {#breach-notification}

**Short answer.** A person or business that owns or licenses computerized personal information must notify affected Ohio residents of a qualifying data breach in the most expedient time possible and no later than 45 days after it discovers or is notified of the breach, unless one of two exemptions applies [^q4-breach-duty] [^q4-timing]. The duty does not apply to a HIPAA covered entity, or to a financial institution, trust company, or credit union (or its affiliate) that federal law requires to notify customers of breaches and that its regulator examines for compliance with that law [^q4-exempt-hipaa] [^q4-exempt-fi].
A breach qualifies when someone without permission accesses and acquires computerized personal information, and this causes, or is reasonably believed to cause, a material risk of identity theft or other fraud to an Ohio resident [^q4-trigger]. The deadline is subject to law-enforcement needs and to the time needed to find the breach's scope and restore the system [^q4-timing].

**What counts as personal information.** Personal information is a person's first name or first initial and last name, linked to at least one of these:

- a Social Security number;
- a driver's license or state ID number;
- a financial account or card number, with the code or password needed to access it.

The data must also be unencrypted and unredacted [^q4-personal-info]. So a breach of properly encrypted data generally needs no Ohio notice.

**How to give notice.** Notice may be written, by phone, or electronic if that is the business's main way of reaching the resident [^q4-methods]. Substitute notice is allowed if the business lacks enough contact information, notice would cost more than $250,000, or more than 500,000 residents are affected. A business with 10 or fewer employees has a separate substitute if notice would cost more than $10,000. Each substitute has its own steps, set out in the statute [^q4-substitute-notice].

**What else the law requires.** Section 1349.19 sets who must be notified, when, and by what method [^q4-breach-duty] [^q4-timing] [^q4-methods]. Our review found nothing in § 1349.19 that sets the notice's contents or requires notice to the Attorney General. If more than 1,000 Ohio residents must be told about one breach, the business must also tell the nationwide consumer reporting agencies without unreasonable delay [^q4-cra]. Any waiver of the statute is void [^q4-waiver].

**Insurance companies have a faster deadline.** An insurance licensee must report a qualifying cybersecurity event to the superintendent of insurance as promptly as possible and no later than three business days after it determines the event occurred [^q4-insurance-event]. It must also follow the consumer breach-notice law where it applies and send the superintendent a copy of its consumer notice [^q4-insurance-consumer-notice].

## Can a written cybersecurity program give you an affirmative defense in Ohio data-breach lawsuits? {#cybersecurity-safe-harbor}

**Short answer.** Under the Ohio Data Protection Act (Ohio Revised Code chapter 1354), a business has an affirmative defense to tort claims, brought under Ohio law or in Ohio courts, that blame a data breach on weak security, but only if it creates, maintains, and complies with a written cybersecurity program that meets three conditions [^q5-dpa-defense] [^q5-dpa-optin]. The program must (1) have administrative, technical, and physical safeguards for personal information, or for both personal and restricted information, and reasonably conform to a recognized cybersecurity framework; (2) be designed to protect the information's security and confidentiality, and to guard against anticipated threats or hazards to its security or integrity and against unauthorized access and acquisition likely to create a material risk of identity theft or other fraud; and (3) have a scale and scope based on the business's size and complexity, the nature and scope of its activities, how sensitive the information is, the cost and availability of security tools, and its resources [^q5-dpa-optin] [^q5-dpa-design] [^q5-dpa-scale].
The defense covers restricted-information breach claims only if the program protects both kinds of information [^q5-dpa-optin] [^q5-dpa-defense].

**The two kinds of information.** Personal information has the same narrow meaning as in the breach-notice law above. Restricted information is other information that can identify or trace a person, or is linked or linkable to them, if it is not encrypted, redacted, or otherwise made unreadable and its breach would likely create a material risk of identity theft or other fraud [^q5-dpa-restricted-info].

**Who can use it.** Any business that handles personal or restricted information can use it, with systems in or outside Ohio and no size threshold [^q5-dpa-covered-entity].

**Which frameworks count.** The program must reasonably conform to one or more of these frameworks [^q5-dpa-frameworks]:

- the NIST Cybersecurity Framework;
- NIST Special Publication 800-171;
- NIST Special Publications 800-53 and 800-53a;
- the FedRAMP security assessment framework;
- the CIS Critical Security Controls;
- the ISO/IEC 27000 family.

A business regulated under HIPAA, GLBA, FISMA, or HITECH may instead conform to the full security requirements of that law [^q5-dpa-regulated-frameworks]. PCI DSS counts only if the program also conforms to one of the listed frameworks [^q5-dpa-pci]. When a framework or standard changes, the program generally has one year to conform to the new version [^q5-dpa-revision].

**What the defense does not do.**

- **It is voluntary.** The chapter applies only to a business *seeking* the defense [^q5-dpa-optin]. Other laws or contracts may still require security controls.
- **It creates no right to sue.** The chapter provides no private right of action, including a class action [^q5-dpa-no-pra].
- **It covers only those tort claims.** Contract claims, other non-tort claims, and suits outside Ohio under another state's law are outside its text [^q5-dpa-defense].

We found no reported Ohio appellate decision on what *reasonably conforms* requires, so records matter: the framework mapping, dated reviews, and proof the business followed the program.

## Can a consumer sue your business in Ohio over privacy? {#consumer-lawsuit}

**Short answer.** A consumer cannot sue under Ohio's breach-notice law, because the Attorney General has the exclusive authority to bring a civil action for failing to comply with it [^q6-ag-exclusive]. A consumer may still bring a tort claim, such as negligence, that blames a breach on weak security [^q6-dpa-defense].
Under the Consumer Sales Practices Act, a consumer may sue individually to cancel the transaction or recover actual economic damages plus up to $5,000 in noneconomic damages [^q6-cspa-individual]. A consumer may also seek a declaratory judgment or an injunction against a violation [^q6-cspa-injunction]. Triple damages and class actions are available only if an Attorney General rule adopted before the transaction, or an Ohio court decision made public before the conduct, had already found that practice deceptive or unconscionable [^q6-cspa-gate].

**Attorney General enforcement of the breach law.** The Attorney General may seek an injunction and civil penalties [^q6-ag-exclusive]. It may investigate on complaints or its own inquiries [^q6-ag-investigation], and in an investigation may take testimony under oath, subpoena witnesses, and demand documents [^q6-ag-powers].

For intentional or reckless failures, civil penalties add up by the day:

- up to $1,000 per day for the first 60 days [^q6-penalty-base];
- up to $5,000 per day for days 61 through 90;
- up to $10,000 per day from day 91 on [^q6-penalty-escalator].

A violator must also pay the Attorney General's costs of investigating and suing [^q6-ag-costs].

**Attorney General enforcement of the Consumer Sales Practices Act.** The Attorney General may seek a declaratory judgment or an injunction when it has reasonable cause to believe a supplier is violating the Act and action is in the public interest [^q6-ag-cspa]. Its class actions are limited to certain listed practices, violations of earlier Attorney General rules, and practices that earlier published Ohio court decisions found unlawful [^q6-ag-cspa-class].

**Privacy claims under the Act.** We found no earlier Ohio rule or published decision that declares a specific data-privacy or data-security practice deceptive, as the triple-damages and class-action limit requires.

The Act also covers only a consumer transaction: a sale or other transfer of goods, a service, a franchise, or an intangible to an individual for mainly personal, family, or household purposes, or a solicitation to supply any of these [^q6-consumer-transaction]. We found no Ohio appellate decision that settles whether a free, ad-supported online service fits that definition.

# Wage and Hour Law in Indiana[^about]

Indiana wage and hour law on the minimum wage and its link to the federal floor, weekly overtime, breaks, final pay, pay frequency and pay statements, worker classification, the tip credit and enforcement.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Indiana's Minimum Wage Law requires an employer with at least two employees in a work week, and not subject to the federal minimum wage provisions, to pay at least the federal minimum wage [^ind-minimum-wage-federal-rate] [^ind-minimum-wage-employer-definition]. The Indiana rate is set by reference to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, whose minimum wage is $7.25 an hour [^ind-minimum-wage-federal-rate] [^fed-minimum-wage-seven-twenty-five]. Employers that are subject to the federal minimum wage fall outside the Indiana law's definition of employer, and the federal act itself sets the $7.25 rate for the employees it covers [^ind-minimum-wage-employer-definition] [^fed-minimum-wage-seven-twenty-five].

The Minimum Wage Law's definition of employee leaves out a list of workers. That list includes persons under 16 [^ind-employee-exclusion-under-sixteen] and persons employed on a commission basis [^ind-employee-exclusion-commission-basis]. It also includes persons in executive, administrative or professional occupations who have authority to employ or discharge and who earn $150 or more a week, and outside salesmen [^ind-employee-exclusion-executive-salesmen]. Any person not employed for more than four weeks in any four consecutive three-month periods is excluded too [^ind-employee-exclusion-short-term].

An employer that consistently discharges people within four weeks of their employment and replaces them without a work stoppage commits a Class A infraction [^ind-four-week-discharge-infraction].

Unless federal or state law provides otherwise, a unit (the statute's term) may not establish, mandate or otherwise require a minimum wage above the one the Minimum Wage Law requires or the federal minimum hourly wage [^ind-local-minimum-wage-preemption].

The Minimum Wage Law does not authorize paying any employee less than another applicable law prescribes [^ind-no-lower-rate-other-law]. The federal act likewise does not excuse an employer from complying with a state or municipal minimum wage that is higher than the federal one [^fed-higher-state-minimum-wage]. The [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us) covers the federal rules.

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** For nonexempt employment governed by the standard forty-hour work week, Indiana's Minimum Wage Law requires a covered employer (at least two employees, not subject to the federal minimum wage) to pay at least one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over forty [^ind-weekly-overtime-forty-hours] [^ind-overtime-employer-definition]. Subsection (f) measures overtime by the work week, and the cited sections set no general daily overtime threshold [^ind-weekly-overtime-forty-hours].

The Minimum Wage Law's definition of employer excludes any employer subject to the federal minimum wage provisions, so the Indiana overtime rule reaches only employers outside those provisions [^ind-overtime-employer-definition].

The regular rate includes all pay for employment made to or on behalf of the employee. The statute lists payments the regular rate does not include. Among them are gifts not measured by hours, production or efficiency, pay for periods with no work such as vacation, holiday or illness, and reimbursable expenses [^ind-regular-rate-listed-exclusions]. The section defines compensatory time off as hours not worked that are not counted as hours worked for overtime purposes and that are paid at the employee's regular rate [^ind-compensatory-time-definition].

Subsection (f) applies except as the section otherwise provides [^ind-weekly-overtime-forty-hours]. The section's exceptions include these:

- Section 4 as a whole does not apply to an employee who provides companionship services to the aged and infirm and is employed by an employer or agency other than the family or household using those services, so neither its minimum wage nor its overtime rule reaches that employee [^ind-companionship-services-exception].
- Some collective bargaining agreements made by representatives certified as bona fide by the National Labor Relations Board are exempt from subsection (f). One kind caps work at 1,040 hours in any 26 consecutive weeks. The other sets a 52-week period of not more than 2,240 hours with a guaranteed minimum, and pays one and one-half times the regular rate for certain hours beyond the guaranty [^ind-collective-bargaining-hour-caps].
- An employee whose duties require irregular hours may work beyond the maximum work week under a bona fide individual contract or collective bargaining agreement that specifies a regular rate of at least the applicable minimum hourly rate and pay of at least one and one-half times that rate for all hours over the maximum work week [^ind-irregular-hours-contract-exception] [^ind-irregular-hours-overtime-multiplier]. The agreement must also provide a weekly pay guaranty for not more than 60 hours at the specified rates [^ind-irregular-hours-sixty-hour-guaranty].
- An agreement reached before the work is performed may compute overtime at not less than one and one-half times bona fide piece rates or other bona fide rates, or at one and one-half times a basic rate substantially equal to the employee's average hourly earnings, subject to the conditions the subsection lists [^ind-piece-rate-overtime-agreements].
- Extra compensation paid as the section describes counts toward the overtime compensation owed [^ind-extra-compensation-creditable-overtime].
- Subsection (f) does not reach an employee of a retail or service establishment whose regular rate is more than one and one-half times the applicable minimum hourly rate and more than half of whose pay over a representative period of at least one month is commissions on goods or services [^ind-retail-commission-overtime-exception].
- A hospital, or an institution primarily engaged in caring for the sick, the aged, or individuals with a mental illness or defect who live on the premises, may use a fourteen-day work period in place of the seven-day work week. This requires an agreement with the employee before the work is performed. The employer then pays one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over eight in any workday and over eighty in the fourteen-day period [^ind-hospital-fourteen-day-period].
- Subsection (f) does not apply to an employee of a motion picture theater [^ind-motion-picture-theater-exception].
- Subsection (f) does not apply to an employee of a seasonal amusement or recreational establishment, an organized camp, or a religious or nonprofit educational conference center that is exempt under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act [^ind-seasonal-recreation-camp-exception].

The section also makes the forty-hour rule apply to domestic service in one or more households [^ind-domestic-service-overtime-rule].

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** No Indiana statute requires a private employer to give an adult employee a meal period or a general rest break [^ind-minor-rest-break-recommendations]. The only general rest-break provision cited here is in the chapter on employing minors under 18, and it lets the department set recommendations for rest breaks, not requirements [^ind-minor-rest-break-recommendations] [^ind-minor-employment-chapter-scope].

For 14- and 15-year-olds, the minors chapter limits working hours. Section 16(a) of the chapter confines that limit, with some exceptions, to minors under 16 [^ind-young-teen-section-scope]. Such a minor may not work before 7 a.m. or after 7 p.m., except until 9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day. Nor may the minor work more than three hours on a school day, eighteen hours in a school week, eight hours on a nonschool day or forty hours in a nonschool week [^ind-young-teen-hour-limits].

A separate statute covers employers with 25 or more employees [^ind-lactation-employer-twenty-five]. To the extent reasonably possible, such an employer must provide a private location, other than a toilet stall, where an employee can express breast milk during any period away from assigned duties [^ind-lactation-private-location]. Also to the extent reasonably possible, that employer must provide a refrigerator or other cold storage for expressed milk, or allow the employee to bring a portable cold storage device [^ind-lactation-cold-storage].

Indiana does require breaks for one group of adult employees. The state and its political subdivisions must provide reasonable paid break time each day to an employee who needs to express breast milk for the employee's infant child [^ind-public-employee-paid-lactation-breaks]. They need not provide that break time if doing so would unduly disrupt their operations [^ind-public-lactation-undue-disruption-exception].

Federal law separately requires an employer to provide reasonable break time for an employee to express breast milk for the employee's nursing child for one year after the child's birth, each time the employee needs to express milk. The employer must also provide a place, other than a bathroom, that is shielded from view and free from intrusion from coworkers and the public [^fed-lactation-break-time-and-place]. That break time counts as hours worked if the employee is not completely relieved from duty during the entire break [^fed-lactation-break-complete-relief-hours]. Otherwise, the federal statute does not require the employer to pay for that break time unless other federal or state law or a municipal ordinance requires it [^fed-lactation-break-unpaid-default]. An employer with fewer than 50 employees is not subject to these federal requirements if they would impose an undue hardship by causing significant difficulty or expense in relation to the employer's size, financial resources, nature or structure [^fed-lactation-small-employer-hardship].

Under the federal hours-worked regulations, rest periods of 5 to about 20 minutes are customarily paid as working time and must be counted as hours worked [^fed-short-rest-periods-counted].

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** Indiana requires an employer that separates an employee from the payroll to pay the unpaid wages by the regular payday for the pay period in which the separation occurred, a rule that does not apply to railroads [^ind-separation-final-pay-regular-payday]. When an employee voluntarily leaves, the employer need not pay the amount due until the next usual and regular payday the employer has set [^ind-voluntary-quit-next-payday]. If an employer fails to pay wages as the wage payment statute requires and a court finds it did not act in good faith, the court must award liquidated damages of two times the wages due. The statute also requires the court to order a reasonable fee for the plaintiff's attorney and court costs [^ind-final-pay-fees-double-damages] [^ind-final-pay-court-costs-double-damages].

When work stops because of an industrial dispute, the wages earned and unpaid when work stops become due at the next regular payday, without abatement or reduction [^ind-industrial-dispute-next-payday].

An employee may quit without the employer knowing the employee's whereabouts or address. In that case the employer is not subject to the wage payment statute's damages remedy until ten business days after the employee demands the wages, or until the employee gives an address where the wages can be sent [^ind-quit-unknown-address-rule].

In a dispute over wages, the employer must tell the employee how much it concedes is due. It must pay that amount without condition within the time the wage claims statute sets. The employee's acceptance of that payment does not release any balance of the claim [^ind-disputed-wages-conceded-amount]. For the wage claims statute, wages means all amounts paid for labor or service, whether fixed or calculated on a time, task, piece or commission basis or by another method [^ind-wage-claims-wages-definition].

The commissioner of labor may refer wage claims under the wage claims statute to the attorney general. The wage payment statute's damages provision applies to civil actions the attorney general or a designee brings on that referral [^ind-final-pay-attorney-general-referral].

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Except for salaried employees eligible for federal overtime and for farmers and those in agriculture and horticulture, Indiana's wage payment statute requires payment at least semimonthly or biweekly when the employee requests it [^ind-pay-frequency-semimonthly-biweekly] [^ind-naugle-employee-request-provision] [^ind-overtime-eligible-salaried-exempt] [^ind-farm-employers-payment-exempt]. Salaried employees who are eligible for overtime under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act are exempt from that pay-frequency section [^ind-overtime-eligible-salaried-exempt]. Farmers and those in agriculture and horticulture are exempt from the entire wage payment chapter [^ind-farm-employers-payment-exempt]. In its *Naugle* decision, the Indiana Supreme Court explained that the statute's subsections operate in concert, so the parties may freely agree to various pay periods [^ind-naugle-parties-agree-pay-periods], but each payment must still cover all wages earned up to a date not more than ten business days before payment [^ind-pay-lag-ten-business-days]. Employers subject to Indiana's Minimum Wage Law must give each employee, every pay period, a statement showing at least the hours worked, the wages paid and a listing of deductions [^ind-pay-statement-required-contents].

Wages must be paid in lawful money of the United States, by negotiable check, draft or money order, or by electronic transfer to the financial institution the employee designates. Any contract that violates the pay-frequency subsection is void [^ind-pay-frequency-semimonthly-biweekly]. Each payment must cover all wages earned up to a date not more than ten business days before payment [^ind-pay-lag-ten-business-days]. A business day is any day other than a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday [^ind-business-day-definition-weekdays].

On demand, an employer subject to the Minimum Wage Law must give the commissioner of labor a sworn statement of the information it gave employees. The related records are open to inspection by the commissioner, a deputy or an authorized agent of the department at any reasonable time [^ind-pay-statement-sworn-commissioner]. Those employers must also post a one-page notice in a conspicuous place showing the current Indiana minimum wage, employees' basic rights under the Minimum Wage Law and how to contact the Indiana department of labor [^ind-minimum-wage-poster-required]. The cited sections do not state a pay-statement requirement for employers outside the Minimum Wage Law.

When an employee directs the employer to deduct from future wages, that direction is an assignment of wages [^ind-deduction-direction-is-assignment]. A wage assignment is valid only if it meets every listed condition. It must be in writing, signed by the employee personally, revocable by the employee at any time on written notice, and agreed to in writing by the employer. A signed copy must reach the employer within ten days, and the assignment must serve a permitted purpose [^ind-wage-assignment-validity-conditions]. The permitted purposes include uniforms and other job-related clothing, and equipment or tools needed for the job, each at no more than the employer's direct cost from an outside vendor. They also include reimbursement for education or skills training, except training paid for in whole or in part through an economic development incentive, and advances of payroll or vacation pay [^ind-wage-assignment-listed-purposes]. The total wages assigned for uniforms and other job-related clothing and for equipment or tools may not exceed the lesser of $2,500 a year or 5 percent of the employee's weekly disposable earnings [^ind-wage-assignment-annual-weekly-cap]. Except as provided in the federal occupational safety standards the statute names, an employee may not be charged or subject to such an assignment for protective equipment, including personal protective equipment identified under those standards [^ind-protective-equipment-assignment-bar]. Voluntary contributions described in subsection (b)(19) of that section may be assigned without meeting those conditions [^ind-voluntary-contribution-assignment-exception].

An employer that has overpaid an employee may deduct the overpayment after two weeks' notice, and the deduction is neither a fine nor a wage assignment. The employer may not deduct an amount that is in dispute [^ind-overpayment-deduction-notice-dispute]. The weekly deduction may not exceed the lesser of 25 percent of disposable earnings or the amount by which disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum hourly wage [^ind-overpayment-deduction-weekly-cap]. There is one exception: a single overpayment equal to ten times gross wages, caused by a misplaced decimal point, may be deducted at once [^ind-decimal-point-overpayment-deduction]. An employer may not fine an employee on any pretext and keep the fine from the employee's wages, and doing so is a Class C infraction [^ind-employer-fines-prohibited-infraction].

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Indiana's Minimum Wage Law sets no separate multi-factor contractor test, but its definition of employee excludes a person in an independently established business who is free from control or direction under the contract and in fact [^ind-wage-law-employee-definition] [^ind-independent-business-exclusion] [^ind-independent-business-free-from-control]. Other Indiana laws use different tests. Unemployment insurance treats paid service as employment unless all three listed conditions are shown [^ind-unemployment-three-condition-test]. Worker's compensation follows the Internal Revenue Service guidelines [^ind-workers-comp-irs-guidelines].

The Minimum Wage Law defines an employee as any person employed or permitted to work or perform any service for pay, or under any contract of hire (written or oral, express or implied), subject to listed exclusions. Its definition of employer excludes employers subject to the federal minimum wage provisions, so the employee definition matters only for employers outside federal minimum wage coverage [^ind-wage-law-employee-definition].

Indiana's unemployment insurance article treats paid service as employment, whether or not a common-law master and servant relationship exists, until all three listed conditions are shown to the department's satisfaction. First, the individual is free from control and direction both under the contract and in fact. Second, the service is outside the usual course of the business for which it is performed. Third, the individual either is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, profession or business of the same nature, or is a sales agent paid only by commission who is master of the individual's own time and effort [^ind-unemployment-three-condition-test].

Under Indiana's worker's compensation chapters, a person is an independent contractor and not an employee if the person is an independent contractor under the guidelines of the United States Internal Revenue Service [^ind-workers-comp-irs-guidelines].

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Indiana's Minimum Wage Law lets a covered employer (two or more employees, not subject to the federal minimum wage) pay tipped employees at least $2.13 an hour in cash, with tips making up the minimum wage [^ind-tip-credit-cash-wage] [^ind-tip-credit-employer-definition]. The employer must back up the tip credit it takes with the tips its employees report [^ind-tip-credit-reported-tips].

The Indiana cash wage is tied to the federal cash wage required on August 20, 1996, which the Indiana statute states was $2.13 an hour. The tip credit makes up the difference between that cash wage and the minimum wage [^ind-tip-credit-cash-wage]. A tip is a sum a customer gives an employee as a gift or gratuity for service, and a mandatory service charge is not a tip [^ind-tip-definition-customer-gratuity] [^ind-tip-excludes-mandatory-service-charge].

An employer may require a tipped employee to participate in a tip pooling arrangement if the tips are shared only with other tipped employees and with no nontipped employees. In that case the employer must give notice of any required contribution, may take a tip credit only for the tips each employee ultimately receives, and may not keep any tips for another purpose [^ind-tip-pool-tipped-only]. An employer that pays its employees at least the minimum wage may require a pool that includes nontipped employees, with notice of any required contribution. Under no circumstances may the employer, a manager or a supervisor receive tips from a pool [^ind-tip-pool-nontipped-minimum-wage].

Under the federal tip credit, an employer subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act pays a cash wage not less than the one required on August 20, 1996, plus an amount based on tips that makes up the difference to the federal minimum wage [^fed-tip-credit-cash-wage-formula]. The federal credit may not exceed the tips the employee actually receives [^fed-tip-credit-actual-tips-cap]. It is also unavailable unless the employer has told the employee about the subsection and the employee has kept all tips, apart from pooling among employees who customarily and regularly receive tips [^fed-tip-credit-notice-retention]. The federal act bars an employer from keeping employees' tips for any purpose, including letting managers or supervisors keep any portion, whether or not the employer takes a tip credit [^fed-employer-may-not-keep-tips].

## How is it enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Current employees and those who left voluntarily may sue under the wage payment statute, but employees proceeding under the wage claims statute, which covers employees the employer separated, must first submit their claims to the Indiana department of labor [^ind-wage-payment-court-recovery] [^ind-steele-wage-payment-current-voluntary] [^ind-steele-wage-claims-separated-suspended] [^ind-steele-claims-submitted-department-labor]. The wage claims statute also covers employees whose work has been suspended as a result of an industrial dispute [^ind-steele-wage-claims-separated-suspended]. As the Indiana Supreme Court explained in its *Steele* decision, claimants who proceed under the wage claims statute may not begin by filing a complaint with the trial court; the claim is first submitted to the Indiana department of labor [^ind-steele-claims-submitted-department-labor]. The commissioner of labor may hold hearings on such a claim and must cooperate with the employee in enforcing a claim the commissioner considers just and valid [^ind-commissioner-hearings-claim-cooperation] [^ind-commissioner-just-valid-claim-cooperation], and the commissioner may then refer the claim to the attorney general, who may bring a civil action on the claimant's behalf [^ind-commissioner-attorney-general-referral]. Separately, an employee of an employer subject to the Minimum Wage Law may sue that employer directly under that law [^ind-minimum-wage-liquidated-damages]. An employer that violates the Minimum Wage Law's wage section owes the unpaid minimum wages plus an equal amount as liquidated damages. An action to recover them may be brought within three years after the cause of action arises [^ind-minimum-wage-liquidated-damages].

In a Minimum Wage Law action, the court must allow a reasonable attorney's fee and costs on top of any judgment. No agreement between employee and employer, and no acceptance of a lower wage, is a defense [^ind-minimum-wage-fees-no-waiver]. An employer or its agent that knowingly or intentionally violates the Minimum Wage Law's wage section or its pay-statement section commits a Class A infraction [^ind-knowing-violation-class-a-infraction].

Under the wage payment statute, an employer that fails to pay wages as the statute requires is liable for the unpaid wages, which can be recovered in any court with jurisdiction [^ind-wage-payment-court-recovery]. The statute also requires the court to order a reasonable fee for the plaintiff's attorney and court costs. If the court finds the employer did not act in good faith, it must order liquidated damages of two times the wages due [^ind-wage-payment-fees-double-damages] [^ind-wage-payment-court-costs-double-damages].

Under the wage claims statute, the commissioner of labor may hold hearings to determine whether a claim is just. The commissioner must cooperate with an employee in enforcing a claim against the employer whenever the commissioner considers the claim just and valid [^ind-commissioner-hearings-claim-cooperation] [^ind-commissioner-just-valid-claim-cooperation]. The commissioner may refer wage claims to the attorney general, and the wage payment statute's damages provision applies to civil actions the attorney general or a designee brings on that referral [^ind-commissioner-attorney-general-referral]. The commissioner may take assignments of wage claims under $6,000 and sue to collect them, and may join several claimants in one claim or one cause of action [^ind-commissioner-wage-claim-assignments]. In actions the commissioner brings as assignee, the commissioner advances no court costs and posts no bond or other security [^ind-commissioner-no-costs-bond].

The Minimum Wage Law allows an action within three years after the cause of action arises [^ind-minimum-wage-liquidated-damages]. A separate statute requires an action relating to the terms, conditions and privileges of employment, including wages or salary, to be brought within two years of the act or omission, except actions based on a written contract [^ind-employment-actions-two-years]. The cited sections do not state which period controls when both could apply. The conservative course is to file any claim within two years of the act or omission.

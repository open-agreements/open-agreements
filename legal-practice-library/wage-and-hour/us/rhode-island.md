# Wage and Hour Law in Rhode Island[^about]

How Rhode Island sets a $16.00 minimum wage for 2026 that rises to $17.00 on January 1, 2027, keeps a $3.89 cash wage for tipped employees, requires weekly overtime, a 20-minute meal period in a six-hour shift and a 30-minute meal period in an eight-hour shift, pays final wages on the next regular payday, and lets employees sue for unpaid wages with liquidated damages of up to twice the amount owed.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Rhode Island's minimum wage is $16.00 an hour beginning January 1, 2026, and the statute raises it to $17.00 an hour beginning January 1, 2027. [^ri-minimum-wage-2026-2027] Both rates are above the $7.25 federal minimum wage. [^ri-federal-minimum-wage-rate] The rates are fixed in the statute rather than indexed, so any increase after 2027 would require a new law. [^ri-minimum-wage-2026-2027]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The minimum wage chapter protects any individual suffered or permitted to work by an employer, but it excludes several groups. [^ri-minimum-wage-employee-exclusions] The exclusions include federal employees, traveling and outside salespersons, newspaper deliverers, golf caddies, certain family members employed by a relative, employees working between May 1 and October 1 at a resort establishment that regularly serves meals to the public and is open no more than six months a year, and employees of organized camps that operate no more than seven months a year, other than camp employees employed on an annual, full-time basis. [^ri-minimum-wage-employee-exclusions] A nonprofit association or corporation with religious, educational, library or community service aims may pay a full-time student under 19 not less than 90 percent of the minimum wage. [^ri-student-nonprofit-90-percent]

An employer that asks or permits an employee to report at the start of a shift and does not provide at least three hours of work must pay the employee at least three times the regular hourly rate, unless both sides voluntarily agreed to a shorter shift. [^ri-reporting-pay-three-hours]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Rhode Island's general overtime rule is weekly, not daily. [^ri-overtime-forty-hours] An employer must pay one and one-half times the regular rate for all hours worked over 40 in a workweek, and an employer that pays overtime to delivery drivers or sales merchandisers may not calculate it by the fluctuating workweek method in 29 C.F.R. § 778.114. [^ri-overtime-forty-hours] Federal law also requires time and one-half for hours over 40 in a workweek for employees it covers. [^ri-federal-overtime-forty-hours] Some public-contract statutes add daily overtime. For example, for janitorial or security guard work performed for a contractor under a state purchasing entity contract of more than $1,000 whose principal purpose is building services, hours beyond eight in a day or 40 in a week must be paid at one and one-half times the standard wage. [^ri-building-service-daily-overtime] [^ri-building-service-definitions]

The state overtime rule does not apply to several groups, including employees of a summer camp open no more than six months of the year, police officers, agricultural employees, and motor carrier employees within federal Department of Transportation hours-of-service authority. [^ri-overtime-exemptions-camps-police] [^ri-overtime-exemptions-agriculture-federal] [^ri-overtime-exemptions] It also exempts an employee employed in a bona fide executive, administrative or professional capacity, as defined by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, who is paid on a salary basis of not less than $200 a week, provided the employee's wages, computed on an hourly basis, do not violate the applicable minimum wage law. [^ri-overtime-exemptions] No state exemption removes an employee's right to overtime under federal law. [^ri-overtime-exemptions-agriculture-federal] Federal law generally requires an exempt executive, administrative or professional employee to be paid a salary of at least $684 a week. [^ri-federal-white-collar-salary-684]

Except for exempt executive, administrative and professional employees, a retail employee who works on a Sunday or holiday under the holiday business statute must receive at least time and a half for that work and at least four hours of employment, and the work must be voluntary. [^ri-retail-sunday-holiday-premium] Hours paid at that premium are excluded from Rhode Island's weekly overtime calculation. [^ri-overtime-forty-hours]

## Are meal and rest breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Rhode Island requires meal periods, subject to two employer exclusions. [^ri-meal-period-six-eight-hours] The rule does not apply to an employer of a licensed health care facility or to an employer that has fewer than three people on a shift at the worksite. [^ri-meal-period-six-eight-hours] Otherwise, an employee is entitled to a 20-minute mealtime within a six-hour work shift and a 30-minute mealtime within an eight-hour work shift, and the state statute does not require the employer to pay for the mealtime. [^ri-meal-period-six-eight-hours] For an employee covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, a meal period may be unpaid only if it is a bona fide meal period in which the employee is completely relieved from duty, which ordinarily lasts 30 minutes or more; a shorter period qualifies only under special conditions. [^ri-federal-bona-fide-meal-periods] Under federal law, short rest periods of about 5 to 20 minutes must be counted as hours worked. [^ri-federal-short-rest-periods] No Rhode Island statute requires general rest breaks for adult employees.

## When are final wages due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** In Rhode Island, final wages are generally due on the next regular payday, but a 24-hour deadline applies when the separation results from specified business events. [^ri-final-pay-next-payday] When the employer separates an employee because it is liquidating, merging, disposing of or moving the business out of state, all wages are due within 24 hours of the separation. [^ri-final-pay-next-payday] In every other case, whether the employee quits or is discharged, the unpaid wages become due on the next regular payday at the usual place of payment. [^ri-final-pay-next-payday]
In an ordinary separation, an employee who has completed at least one year of service must also be paid accrued vacation pay with the final wages on the next regular payday, if the vacation pay was accrued or awarded under a collective bargaining agreement, a written or verbal company policy, or another written or verbal agreement. [^ri-final-pay-next-payday] In a separation caused by the business events listed above, an employee with at least one year of service must instead receive holiday pay, vacation pay and insurance benefits due under a collective bargaining agreement, company policy or other agreement within the same 24 hours. [^ri-final-pay-next-payday]

An employer that fails to pay final wages on time may be sued for the unpaid wages, liquidated damages of up to twice the amount owed, and attorney's fees. [^ri-final-pay-private-action] An employer that knowingly and willfully fails to pay final wages commits a felony if the wages due to the employee exceed $1,500, and each pay period of failure to pay wages on time is a separate civil violation. [^ri-wage-payment-criminal-penalty]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Rhode Island requires most private employees to be paid weekly. [^ri-weekly-pay-frequency] The weekly rule does not apply to employees of the state and its political subdivisions, to employees of religious, literary or charitable corporations, or to employees whose compensation is fixed at a biweekly, semi-monthly, monthly or yearly rate. [^ri-weekly-pay-frequency] On written petition, the Director of Labor and Training may permit an employer to pay less often than weekly, but no less than twice a month on a predesignated date, if the employer posts a surety bond or similar security and meets the other statutory conditions. [^ri-weekly-pay-frequency] An employer whose average payroll is less than twice the state minimum wage must also supply payroll details to the department and have no history of wage and hour violations. [^ri-pay-frequency-lower-payroll-petition]

Every employer must set a regular payday, and each payday must fall within nine days after the end of the payroll period for which the wages are computed, unless prevented by inevitable casualty; if the ninth day is a holiday, payment on the next business day complies. An employer must give at least three paydays' written or posted notice of a change in the payday. [^ri-regular-payday-nine-days]

## What pay statements and deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** On every regular payday, an employer must give each employee a statement of the hours worked in the pay period, except for employees exempt from overtime, and a record of all deductions with an explanation of the basis for each. [^ri-statement-of-earnings] An employer engaged only in the commercial construction industry must also show the employee's hourly regular rate. [^ri-statement-of-earnings] The statement may be electronic, but the employer must provide a printed or handwritten record at no cost if the employee asks in writing. [^ri-statement-of-earnings]

An employer may not deduct money from wages unless federal or state law or a court order authorizes it, or the employee first gives written or electronic approval. An employer may never deduct for spoilage or breakage, shortages or losses, or fines for tardiness, misconduct or quitting without notice, even with the employee's approval. [^ri-deductions-not-authorized] An employer that makes an unauthorized deduction owes the employee treble damages on the amount deducted. [^ri-deductions-not-authorized] An employer that takes a payroll deduction must transfer the money to the person entitled to it within 21 days after the end of the month in which it was deducted, unless the recipient agrees otherwise in writing. [^ri-payroll-deduction-transfer]

## How does Rhode Island decide whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Rhode Island's Department of Labor and Training investigates worker misclassification using factors consistent with federal Fair Labor Standards Act regulations. [^ri-misclassification-flsa-factors] Misclassifying a worker as an independent contractor when the worker should be treated and paid as an employee violates the Rhode Island wage payment law. [^ri-misclassification-flsa-factors] The wage payment law defines an employee as any person suffered or permitted to work by an employer, but independent contractors and subcontractors are not employees. [^ri-wage-payment-employee-definition]

An employer that misclassifies an employee owes a civil penalty of $1,500 to $3,000 for each misclassified employee for a first offense and up to $5,000 for each misclassified employee for a later offense, shared equally between the department and the aggrieved worker. [^ri-misclassification-civil-penalty] A misclassified worker may also sue under the private right of action for wage claims. [^ri-misclassification-private-action]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Rhode Island allows a tip credit. [^ri-gratuity-allowance-scope] An employer of an employee who customarily receives gratuities as part of weekly income may count tips toward the minimum wage, but the cash wage may not be less than $3.89 an hour. [^ri-gratuity-allowance-scope] [^ri-tipped-cash-wage-389] The tip credit does not apply to taxicabs and limited public motor vehicles. [^ri-gratuity-allowance-scope] An employer that wants to count tips must provide substantial evidence that the employee's tips cover the allowance, and an affected employee is entitled to a hearing on the amount of the deduction. [^ri-tipped-cash-wage-389]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee or former employee, or an organization representing one, may sue in any court of competent jurisdiction over unpaid wages or benefits or misclassification under the minimum wage and wage payment chapters. [^ri-private-action-liquidated-damages] The employee may recover unpaid wages and benefits, compensatory damages, liquidated damages of up to twice the amount owed, equitable relief including reinstatement, and reasonable attorney's fees and costs. [^ri-private-action-liquidated-damages] The claim must be brought within three years after it accrues, and it may be brought on behalf of similarly situated employees. [^ri-private-action-limitations-class] An employee who files suit before the Director of Labor and Training issues notice of an administrative hearing may proceed in court instead of, but not in addition to, the department's enforcement process. [^ri-private-action-limitations-class]

The Department of Labor and Training may investigate, hold hearings and order payment of wages found due. [^ri-dlt-enforcement-powers] [^ri-dlt-payment-order] The order must award interest at 12 percent a year from the date of nonpayment. [^ri-dlt-enforcement-interest-penalty] The order must also impose a civil penalty of up to twice the wages found due, shared equally between the department and the aggrieved employee. [^ri-dlt-enforcement-interest-penalty] An agreement to work for less than the required wage is not a defense in either a private action or a department proceeding. [^ri-private-action-limitations-class] [^ri-dlt-no-agreement-defense]

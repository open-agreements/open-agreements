# Wage and Hour Law in Minnesota[^about]

Minnesota requires an inflation-indexed minimum wage with no tip credit, overtime after 48 hours in a workweek, rest and meal breaks, prompt final pay on discharge or resignation, payment at least every 31 days and a detailed start-of-employment wage notice, enforced through commissioner orders, liquidated damages, statutory penalties and private lawsuits.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Since January 1, 2026, Minnesota employers have had to pay at least $11.41 an hour, the inflation-adjusted state minimum wage for all employers, subject to a lower 90-day training wage for workers under age 20 [^mn-minimum-wage-2026-rate]. The training wage for workers under age 20 rose to $9.31 an hour in the same adjustment [^mn-training-wage-2026-rate][^mn-minimum-wage-2026-rate]. An employer may not displace an employee in order to hire someone at the training wage, including partially through a cut in hours, wages or employment benefits [^mn-training-wage-no-displacement].
Both rates are indexed to inflation. By August 31 each year, the commissioner of labor and industry measures inflation, and the minimum wage rates increase by the lesser of five percent or the measured percentage, rounded to the nearest cent [^mn-minimum-wage-inflation-adjustment]. A rate is never reduced under this adjustment, and the new rates take effect the next January 1 [^mn-minimum-wage-inflation-adjustment].
Under the 2026 adjustment, the Department announced that the minimum wage for all employers will rise to $11.87 an hour on January 1, 2027 [^mn-minimum-wage-2027-rate]. The 90-day training wage for workers under age 20 will rise to $9.68 an hour on the same date [^mn-training-wage-2027-rate]. Until January 1, 2027, the $11.41 and $9.31 rates adopted in the January 1, 2026 adjustment remain in force [^mn-minimum-wage-2026-rate][^mn-training-wage-2026-rate].
The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry states that the 2024 legislative session eliminated the reduced minimum wages that had applied to small employers with annual gross revenues under $500,000, to youth under 18 and to J-1 visa workers [^mn-small-employer-rate-eliminated]. The Department also states that the state minimum wage is higher than the federal minimum wage, so employees covered by both laws must be paid the higher state rate [^mn-state-rate-above-federal]. Minimum-wage rates apply to all hours worked, whether part time or full time [^mn-minimum-wage-all-hours]. Minneapolis and St. Paul have their own minimum wage ordinances that may require a higher rate of pay [^mn-city-minimum-wage-ordinances].
The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act's definition of employee in section 177.23 excludes listed groups, which include individuals employed in a bona fide executive, administrative or professional capacity, salespeople who conduct no more than 20 percent of sales on the employer's premises, drivers employed by a business operating taxicabs, individuals babysitting as sole practitioners and, for overtime purposes only, individuals employed on a seasonal basis in a carnival, circus, fair or ski facility [^mn-employee-definition-exclusions].
Deductions for up to the full cost of a required uniform or equipment may not exceed $50, or, where a licensed motor vehicle dealer furnishes uniforms or clothing on an ongoing basis, the lesser of 50 percent of the dealer's reasonable expense or $25 per month, including nonhome maintenance [^mn-uniform-equipment-deduction-cap]. No deduction for the listed uniforms, equipment, consumable supplies or travel expenses may reduce wages below the minimum wage [^mn-uniform-deduction-minimum-wage-floor]. Minnesota Rules part 5200.0090 bars deductions from the minimum wage for items such as cash or merchandise shortages, uniforms, consumable supplies, travel expenses, breakage, walkouts, bad checks and disciplinary fines [^mn-minimum-wage-deductions-barred]. When employment ends, an employer must reimburse the full amount it deducted for any of the items listed in section 177.24, subdivision 4, except a motor vehicle dealer's rental and maintenance deduction for uniforms or clothing [^mn-uniform-deduction-reimbursement].

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Minnesota employers must pay at least one and one-half times the regular rate for each hour an employee works over 48 in a workweek [^mn-overtime-48-hour-workweek]. The state of Minnesota and its political subdivisions may instead grant time off at one and one-half hours for each hour worked over 48 in a week [^mn-overtime-public-compensatory-time]. Federal law on this point is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).
Section 177.25 creates a daily threshold only through an optional health care arrangement. An employer that operates a health care facility and an employee may agree, before the work is performed, to use a 14-consecutive-day work period instead of the seven-day workweek, with overtime at one and one-half times the regular rate for work over eight hours in a workday and over 80 hours in the 14-day period [^mn-overtime-health-care-14-day].
The 48-hour overtime rule is not violated for an employee working under an agreement that meets section 7(b)(2) of the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, or for a sugar beet hand laborer paid by the piece whose regular hourly rate exceeds the applicable state minimum wage by at least 40 cents [^mn-overtime-agreement-sugar-beet-exceptions]. The rule also does not apply to salespeople, parts people or mechanics primarily engaged in selling or servicing automobiles, trailers, trucks or farm implements who are paid on a commission or incentive basis by a nonmanufacturing establishment primarily engaged in selling those vehicles to ultimate purchasers [^mn-overtime-vehicle-sales-mechanics]. It does not apply to an employee constructing on-farm silos or installing appurtenant equipment on a unit or piece rate basis whose regular hourly rate exceeds the applicable state minimum wage [^mn-overtime-farm-silo-construction]. Nor does it apply to employees of air carriers subject to title II of the Railway Labor Act when the hours over 48 in a workweek are not required by the carrier but are arranged through a voluntary agreement among employees to trade scheduled work hours [^mn-overtime-air-carrier-trades].
Under Minnesota Rules, a workweek once established remains fixed, although it may be changed if the change is intended as permanent rather than as an evasion of the overtime provisions [^mn-workweek-fixed-period-rule]. If no workweek is designated, it follows the calendar week [^mn-workweek-calendar-week-default]. The regular rate is calculated by dividing the employee's remuneration in a workweek by the total hours worked [^mn-overtime-regular-rate-calculation], and overtime pay is due no later than the payday immediately following the regular payday for the pay period in which it was earned [^mn-overtime-pay-timing-rule].
Section 177.23 excludes from the definition of employee any individual employed in a bona fide executive, administrative or professional capacity [^mn-overtime-executive-exclusion-definition]. For executive, administrative and professional personnel, the employee's primary duties control, and an employer may treat the employee as exempt from overtime only where those duties meet all the criteria under a particular test [^mn-overtime-exemption-primary-duties]. Minnesota Rules treat an employee as paid a salary when an agreement with the employer guarantees a predetermined wage for each workweek [^mn-salary-predetermined-weekly-wage].

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Minnesota employers must allow each employee a rest break of at least 15 minutes, or enough time to use the nearest convenient restroom if that is longer, within each four consecutive hours of work [^mn-rest-break-four-hours]. Under Minnesota Rules, rest periods of less than 20 minutes may not be deducted from total hours worked [^mn-short-rest-periods-counted]. An employer that does not allow required rest breaks is liable for the rest break time at the employee's regular rate of pay, plus an equal additional amount as liquidated damages [^mn-rest-break-liquidated-damages]. Section 177.253 does not prohibit employers and employees from establishing different rest breaks under a collective bargaining agreement [^mn-rest-break-bargaining-agreement].
Employers must also allow each employee who works six or more consecutive hours a meal break of at least 30 minutes [^mn-meal-break-six-hours]. Apart from subdivision 4 of section 177.254, nothing in that section requires the employer to pay the employee during the meal break [^mn-meal-break-unpaid-allowed]. Subdivision 4 is the remedy: an employer that does not allow a required meal break owes the employee the meal break time at the employee's regular rate of pay, plus an equal amount as liquidated damages [^mn-meal-break-denial-remedy]. Section 177.254 likewise does not prohibit employers and employees from establishing different meal periods under a collective bargaining agreement [^mn-meal-period-bargaining-agreement]. Minnesota Rules require that the employee be completely relieved from duty to eat regular meals [^mn-meal-period-relieved-duty], and a meal period frequently interrupted by calls to duty must be counted as hours worked [^mn-interrupted-meal-hours-worked].
Under section 181.939, an employer must provide reasonable break times each day to an employee who needs to express milk [^mn-lactation-break-time-required]. Those break times may run concurrently with break times already provided to the employee [^mn-lactation-break-concurrent-allowed], and the employer may not reduce the employee's compensation for that time [^mn-lactation-break-pay-protected]. The employer must also make reasonable efforts to provide a clean, private and secure room or other location close to the work area, other than a bathroom or toilet stall, that is shielded from view, free from intrusion by coworkers and the public, and equipped with access to an electrical outlet [^mn-lactation-private-room-required]. Separate hour limits apply to minors under 16, who may not work before 7:00 a.m. or after 9:00 p.m. [^mn-minor-hours-time-of-day] or more than 40 hours a week or eight hours in any 24-hour period [^mn-minor-maximum-weekly-hours], and a high school student may not work after 11:00 p.m. on an evening before a school day or before 5:00 a.m. on a school day, subject to exceptions the statute lists [^mn-high-school-student-hours].

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** When a Minnesota employer discharges an employee, the wages or commissions actually earned and unpaid at the time of the discharge are immediately due and payable upon the employee's demand [^mn-final-pay-discharge-demand]. The demand must be in writing but need not state the precise amount owed [^mn-final-pay-written-demand]. An employee may seek and recover payment under section 181.13 even without being a party to a contract that requires the rate demanded, so long as the contract or other applicable law requires payment at that rate [^mn-final-pay-noncontracting-employee]. Wages are actually earned and unpaid if the employee was not paid for all time worked at the greater of the regular rate or the rate required by law [^mn-final-pay-earned-unpaid-meaning].
If the earned wages and commissions are not paid within 24 hours after the demand, the employer is in default [^mn-final-pay-discharge-default]. The discharged employee may then recover the unpaid wages and commissions plus a penalty equal to the employee's average daily earnings for each day of default, up to 15 days, until full payment or a satisfactory settlement [^mn-final-pay-discharge-penalty]. Where a public employer needs a governing board to approve the expenditure, the 24-hour period starts on the date of the board's first regular or special meeting after the discharge [^mn-final-pay-public-employer-timing]. Final wages are paid in the usual manner unless the employee asks for them to be mailed [^mn-final-pay-usual-manner], and mailed wages are paid as of the date of their postmark [^mn-final-pay-mailed-postmark].
When an employee quits or resigns, earned and unpaid wages or commissions must be paid in full no later than the first regularly scheduled payday after the final day of employment, unless a collective bargaining agreement provides differently [^mn-final-pay-quit-first-payday]. If that payday falls less than five calendar days after the final day, payment may be delayed to the second regularly scheduled payday, but not beyond 20 calendar days after the final day [^mn-final-pay-quit-twenty-days]. Migrant workers, as section 181.85 defines them, must be paid within three days after quitting or resigning [^mn-final-pay-migrant-three-days].
Wages not paid within the required period become immediately payable on the employee's demand [^mn-final-pay-quit-immediate-demand], and that demand must be in writing but need not state the precise amount owed [^mn-final-pay-quit-written-demand]. If they are not paid within 24 hours after that demand, the employer is liable for a penalty equal to the employee's average daily earnings for every day, not exceeding 15 days in all [^mn-final-pay-quit-penalty], in addition to the earned and unpaid wages and commissions [^mn-final-pay-quit-wages-owed].
An employer that disputes the amount claimed and makes a good-faith legal tender of the amount it believes is due is not liable for more than that amount and interest, unless the employee recovers a greater sum in court [^mn-final-pay-dispute-tender]. If the employee was entrusted with the collection, disbursement or handling of money or property during employment, the employer has ten calendar days after termination to audit and adjust the accounts, and the penalty runs only from a demand made after that period [^mn-final-pay-audit-ten-days]. Sections 181.13 to 181.171 do not apply to an employer that is bankrupt or operating under a court-directed receiver or trustee [^mn-final-pay-bankrupt-employer]. Payment or tender by a check drawn on a bank in the county where the laborer is employed is sufficient payment or tender under sections 181.13 to 181.171 [^mn-final-pay-check-tender]. An employee who hides or stays away to avoid payment, or refuses a full tender, loses the benefit of sections 181.13 to 181.171 for that time, while wages due striking employees when a strike begins do not become due until the next regular payday after it starts [^mn-final-pay-avoidance-strike-rule].
Minnesota applies narrower final-pay rules than the general discharge and resignation rules in two settings. In transitory employment that requires the employee to change the place of abode, wages must be paid within 24 hours after the employment ends by completion, discharge or quitting; if they are not paid within 24 hours, the employer must pay the employee's reasonable expenses of remaining in camp or elsewhere away from home while awaiting payment, and if they are not paid within two business days after the employment ends for any cause, the employer must in addition pay two times the employee's average daily earnings from the termination until payment in full [^mn-final-pay-transitory-24-hours]. Section 181.145 covers commission salespeople who are independent contractors and so fall outside sections 181.13 and 181.14 [^mn-commission-salesperson-definition]. Their commissions earned through the last day are due on demand within three working days when the employer ends the relationship or the salesperson resigns with at least five days' written notice [^mn-commission-salesperson-three-days], and within six working days when the salesperson resigns without that notice [^mn-commission-salesperson-six-days]. If the salesperson was entrusted with the collection, disbursement or handling of money or property, the employer has ten working days after the employment ends to audit and adjust the accounts before the salesperson can demand the commissions [^mn-commission-salesperson-audit-period], and the penalty then runs only from a demand made after that ten-working-day period [^mn-commission-salesperson-audit-penalty-start]. An employer that fails to pay those commissions on demand within the applicable period is liable, in addition to the earned commissions, for a penalty for each day, not exceeding 15 days, that it is late [^mn-commission-salesperson-late-penalty]. The daily penalty equals 1/15 of the commissions earned through the last day of employment that are still unpaid [^mn-commission-salesperson-daily-penalty]. The penalty does not apply when, in a dispute over the amount of the commissions or the audit of the account, the employer pays within the applicable period the amount it in good faith believes is owed, unless a later adjudication finds the commissions were greater than the amount paid [^mn-commission-salesperson-good-faith-dispute].

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Minnesota employers must pay all wages, including salary, earnings and gratuities, at least once every 31 days and commissions at least once every three months, on a regular payday designated in advance [^mn-pay-frequency-31-days]. Unless paid earlier, wages earned in the first half of the first 31-day pay period are due on the first regular payday after the first day of work [^mn-first-pay-period-due-date]. Wages are earned on the day an employee works [^mn-wages-earned-day-worked], and section 181.101 gives employees a substantive right to payment of wages and commissions, not only a right to be paid at certain times [^mn-pay-frequency-substantive-right]. Section 181.101 does not prevent a school district, other public school entity or other school from paying school-year wages on regular paydays as an applicable contract, collective bargaining agreement or personnel policy provides [^mn-school-year-pay-exception]. For that section, employee includes a person who performs agricultural labor as section 181.85 defines it [^mn-pay-frequency-agricultural-employees].
Shorter intervals apply in two settings. Employers on transitory projects that require employees to change their place of abode must pay at intervals of not more than 15 days [^mn-transitory-work-15-day-pay], and public service corporations must pay at least semimonthly [^mn-public-service-semimonthly-pay].
If wages or commissions are not paid, the commissioner of labor and industry may serve a demand for payment on the employee's behalf [^mn-commissioner-wage-demand]. If wages are not paid within ten days of service, the commissioner may collect the wages plus a penalty of the employee's average daily earnings for each day beyond the ten-day limit [^mn-commissioner-demand-wage-penalty]. For unpaid commissions the penalty is 1/15 of the unpaid commissions for each day beyond that limit [^mn-commissioner-demand-commission-penalty]. Money the commissioner collects goes to the employee [^mn-collected-wages-paid-employee], and the section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a wage claim [^mn-pay-frequency-private-claim-preserved].
At the end of each pay period, the employer must give each employee an earnings statement covering that pay period, in writing or electronically [^mn-earnings-statement-each-period]. Under section 181.032(b), the earnings statement may take any form the employer chooses but must include listed items, including the employee's name, the rate or rates of pay and their basis, any allowances claimed for permitted meals and lodging, total hours worked unless the employee is exempt from chapter 177, gross pay for the period, a list of deductions, net pay after all deductions, the date the pay period ends, and the employer's legal and operating names, physical address and telephone number [^mn-earnings-statement-required-items]. An employee who gives at least 24 hours' notice is entitled to written statements instead of electronic ones [^mn-earnings-statement-written-request].
At the start of employment, section 181.032 requires a written notice of the rate and basis of pay, meal and lodging allowances, paid time-off accruals, the employee's exempt status and its basis, permitted deductions, the pay period and paydays, and the employer's legal name, address and telephone number [^mn-start-employment-wage-notice]. The employer must keep a copy signed by the employee [^mn-wage-notice-signed-copy], provide the notice in a language the employee requests [^mn-wage-notice-requested-language], and give the employee written changes before they take effect [^mn-wage-notice-written-changes].
Records required by section 177.30 must be kept for three years in the premises where the employee works or, for state-funded public works projects, for three years after the contracting authority's final payment [^mn-payroll-records-three-years]. Those records must be readily available for the commissioner's inspection on demand [^mn-payroll-records-inspection].
As a general rule, an employer may not deduct from wages for lost or stolen property, damage to property or another claimed debt unless the employee voluntarily authorizes the deduction in writing after the loss or debt arises, or a court holds the employee liable [^mn-wage-deduction-written-authorization]. The employee's authorization must set forth the amount to be deducted from wages during each pay period [^mn-wage-deduction-amount-per-period]. A deduction may not exceed the amount established by law as subject to garnishment or execution on wages [^mn-wage-deduction-garnishment-limit]. An agreement between an employer and an employee contrary to section 181.79 is void, but the section does not apply where a collective bargaining agreement has a contrary provision, to an employer's disciplinary rules for commissioned salespeople covering errors or omissions in performing their duties, or where an employee, before making a purchase or taking a loan from the employer, voluntarily authorizes in writing that its cost be deducted from wages at regular intervals or on termination [^mn-wage-deduction-void-agreements-exceptions]. An employer that violates this rule is liable in a civil action for twice the amount of the deduction or credit taken [^mn-wage-deduction-double-damages].

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Outside building construction and improvement services, which section 181.723 governs separately, Minnesota's wage-and-hour rules decide whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor by weighing all factors to determine whether the worker is economically dependent on the business the worker serves [^mn-economic-dependence-test][^mn-construction-classification-scope]. Section 177.23 defines an employer broadly to include any individual, partnership, association, corporation or business trust, and any person or group acting directly or indirectly in the interest of an employer in relation to an employee [^mn-fair-labor-employer-definition].
Section 181.722 prohibits a person from failing to classify, represent or treat an employee as an employee as applicable law requires, from failing to report or disclose an employee when required, and from requiring or requesting an employee to sign an agreement or document that treats the individual as an independent contractor [^mn-misclassification-prohibited-acts]. For that section, employee status is determined using the same tests and in the same manner as under the workers' compensation and unemployment insurance laws and rules [^mn-misclassification-status-test].
A violation of section 181.722 can bring compensatory damages to the misclassified individual, including the value of minimum wage, overtime and benefits, a penalty of up to $10,000 for each individual not treated as an employee, a penalty of up to $10,000 for each violation of subdivision 1, and $1,000 for each person who delays, obstructs or otherwise fails to cooperate with the commissioner's investigation, with each day of delay, obstruction or failure to cooperate counted as a separate violation [^mn-misclassification-penalties-per-individual]. An owner, partner, principal, member, officer or agent who knowingly or repeatedly engaged in the prohibited conduct may be held individually liable [^mn-misclassification-individual-liability], and knowingly means knew or could have known with the exercise of reasonable diligence [^mn-misclassification-knowingly-defined]. A commissioner's order for prohibited conduct is also in effect against any successor person [^mn-misclassification-successor-orders].
A separate statute, section 181.723, applies only to persons providing or performing building construction or improvement services [^mn-construction-classification-scope]. In that industry, an individual who performs such services in the course of a person's trade or business is that person's employee for purposes of chapters 176, 177, 181, 181A, 182, 268 and 326B, except as subdivision 4 provides [^mn-construction-worker-employee-presumption]. The individual is an independent contractor only when operating as a business entity that meets every listed requirement at the time of the work, beginning with being established and maintained separately from and independently of the person served [^mn-construction-business-entity-requirements]. Any individual working as or for a business entity that does not meet all those requirements is the employee of the person who engaged the entity [^mn-construction-entity-workers-employees]. That rule does not make the individual the person's employee when an intervening business entity in the contractual chain meets every listed requirement, or when the person establishes that an intervening business entity treats and classifies the individual as an employee in compliance with the listed chapters [^mn-construction-intervening-entity-exception]. A person that treats a construction worker as an independent contractor must keep, for at least three years, the documentation on which that determination was based [^mn-construction-contractor-records-retention].

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** No Minnesota employer may directly or indirectly credit, apply, or utilize gratuities towards payment of the minimum wage set by state or federal law, so Minnesota allows no tip credit [^mn-gratuities-not-minimum-wage]. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry states that no employer may take a tip credit against minimum wages in Minnesota [^mn-no-tip-credit-guidance].
Any gratuity received by an employee, or left at a place of business for an employee's personal services, is the employee's sole property [^mn-gratuities-employee-property]. No employer may require an employee to share a gratuity with the employer or other employees or to contribute it to a pool, although employees may share gratuities voluntarily [^mn-gratuity-sharing-voluntary-only]. Minnesota Rules add that pooling or sharing of gratuities may not be a condition of employment [^mn-tip-pooling-not-condition]. The commissioner of labor and industry may require an employer to pay restitution in the amount of gratuities diverted [^mn-diverted-gratuities-restitution].
Gratuities include an obligatory charge that a customer might reasonably read as payment for an employee's personal services, unless the employer gives clear and conspicuous notice that the charge is not the employee's property [^mn-gratuities-definition-obligatory-charges]. Gratuities paid by debit, charge or credit card or other electronic payment are credited to the pay period in which the employee receives them [^mn-card-gratuities-pay-period-credit], and the full amount must be distributed to the employee no later than the next scheduled pay period [^mn-card-gratuities-next-pay-period].

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Minnesota wage-and-hour rights are enforced through compliance orders issued by the commissioner of labor and industry and through employees' own civil actions filed directly in district court [^mn-commissioner-compliance-orders][^mn-fair-labor-employee-civil-action][^mn-wage-payment-civil-action].
The commissioner may enter and inspect an employer's place of business, during reasonable office hours or on request, to examine books, registers, payrolls and other records relating to wages, hours and other conditions of employment [^mn-commissioner-workplace-inspection]. The commissioner may also investigate an employee's wage claims or complaints against an employer where the failure to pay a wage may violate Minnesota law or an order or rule of the department [^mn-commissioner-wage-claim-investigation]. The commissioner may order compliance with the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act and with listed chapter 181 wage payment sections, including sections 181.03, 181.032, 181.101, 181.13, 181.14, 181.722 and 181.723 [^mn-commissioner-compliance-orders]. An employer that wishes to contest an order must file a written objection with the commissioner within 15 calendar days after being served [^mn-compliance-order-objection-deadline]. Without a timely objection, the order becomes a final order of the commissioner [^mn-compliance-order-becomes-final].
When the commissioner issues an order to comply, the commissioner must order the employer to stop the violation and take corrective steps [^mn-commissioner-cease-desist-order]. The commissioner must also order back pay, gratuities and compensatory damages, plus an equal additional amount as liquidated damages [^mn-commissioner-back-pay-liquidated], and may order reinstatement and other appropriate relief [^mn-commissioner-reinstatement-relief]. An employer found to have repeatedly or willfully violated the listed sections faces an additional civil penalty of up to $10,000 for each violation for each employee [^mn-repeated-willful-civil-penalty]. The commissioner may also order reimbursement of litigation and hearing costs unless that would impose extreme financial hardship [^mn-commissioner-litigation-costs], and interest accrues on an unpaid order from the date it is signed [^mn-commissioner-order-interest].
The commissioner may fine an employer up to $10,000 for each failure to submit or deliver records on request [^mn-records-submission-fine] and up to $1,000 for each failure to maintain required records, and up to $5,000 for each repeated failure [^mn-records-maintenance-fine]. Where an employer's records are inadequate, the commissioner may determine wages due based on available evidence [^mn-inadequate-records-determination].
Under section 177.27, an employee may sue directly in district court for violations of sections 177.21 to 177.44 [^mn-fair-labor-employee-civil-action]. An employer that underpays wages or overtime under those sections is liable for the full amount of unpaid wages, gratuities and overtime, plus an equal additional amount as liquidated damages [^mn-fair-labor-underpayment-liability], and the employee may also seek the relief the commissioner can order [^mn-fair-labor-additional-relief]. An agreement to work for less than the applicable wage is not a defense [^mn-below-wage-agreement-no-defense], and the court must order an employer found in violation to pay the employee's reasonable costs, disbursements, witness fees and attorney fees [^mn-fair-labor-attorney-fees].
The chapter 181 wage payment statutes carry a separate civil action under section 181.171, which covers sections including 181.03, 181.032, 181.101, 181.13, 181.14, 181.722 and 181.723 [^mn-wage-payment-civil-action]. An employer found in violation is liable for the penalties or damages each section provides [^mn-wage-payment-section-penalties], plus compensatory damages and other appropriate relief, including injunctive relief [^mn-wage-payment-compensatory-relief]. The court must also order the employer to pay reasonable costs, disbursements, witness fees and attorney fees [^mn-wage-payment-attorney-fees].
Section 181.03 bars an employer, acting with intent to defraud, from obtaining a receipt for more wages than were paid, demanding a rebate or refund of owed wages, or making wages appear greater than those actually paid [^mn-wage-fraud-prohibited-practices]. An employer that violates section 181.03 is liable in a civil action brought by the employee for twice the amount in dispute [^mn-wage-fraud-double-damages]. An employer that violates subdivision 6 of section 181.03, its retaliation provision, is liable for a civil penalty of $700 to $3,000 per violation in addition to other remedies [^mn-wage-retaliation-civil-penalty]. An employee may also bring a civil action under section 181.172, the wage disclosure protection statute, for a violation of its paragraph (a) or (d) [^mn-wage-disclosure-civil-action].
Section 177.32 makes it a misdemeanor for an employer to, among other things, hinder the commissioner, falsify records, repeatedly fail to keep required records, pay less than the required wage, or refuse adequate time from work required by section 177.253 [^mn-fair-labor-misdemeanors]. An employer convicted of discharging or discriminating against an employee for complaining about unpaid wages, starting a proceeding, or testifying must be fined $700 to $3,000 [^mn-fair-labor-retaliation-fine].
Under section 541.07, an action to recover wages or overtime, or damages, fees or penalties, under laws respecting the payment of wages or overtime must generally be commenced within two years, or within three years if the employer fails to submit payroll records by a date the Department of Labor and Industry specifies on request or the nonpayment is willful and not the result of mistake or inadvertence [^mn-wage-claim-limitations-period]. Federal law on this point is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

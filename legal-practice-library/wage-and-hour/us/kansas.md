# Wage and Hour Law in Kansas[^about]

A plain-language guide to Kansas wage-and-hour law covering the $7.25 minimum wage, the tipped cash wage and the 46-hour overtime rule of the Kansas Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law, which does not reach employers subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, along with the Kansas Wage Payment Act rules on paydays, final pay, deductions, notices and enforcement, the child labor hours limits and the statutory definitions used to decide who counts as an employee.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Kansas employers covered by the Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law must pay covered employees at least $7.25 an hour, but employers subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act are excluded [^ks-minimum-wage-hourly-rate] [^ks-employer-definition-flsa-exclusion].
The minimum wage section also does not apply to employers and employees covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act [^ks-minimum-wage-flsa-carveout]. Federal law on this point is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

Even when the Kansas law reaches an employer, it does not cover every worker. Its definition of employee leaves out several listed groups [^ks-employee-definition-exclusions]:

- agricultural workers
- domestic workers in private homes
- bona fide executive, administrative and professional employees, and outside commission-paid salespeople, as the secretary's rules define those terms
- federal employees
- people who work without pay for a nonprofit organization
- people 18 or younger employed on an occasional or part-time basis
- some unified school district executive, administrative and professional staff

Occasional or part-time basis means working less than 40 hours a week. Students 18 or younger who work between academic terms count as part-time no matter how many hours they work [^ks-part-time-basis-definition].

The secretary may also adopt rules that allow wages below the regular rate, under permits. These rules can cover handicapped workers or patient laborers at state institutions or hospitals, and learners and apprentices, subject to the limits the secretary sets [^ks-subminimum-wage-permits].

Standards in other Kansas laws on minimum wages, maximum hours, overtime or other working conditions that were in effect when the Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law took effect, and that are more favorable to employees, continue in full force until they are specifically superseded by more favorable standards under that law or its rules [^ks-more-favorable-standards-preserved].

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Kansas employers covered by the Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law must pay covered employees one and one-half times the regular hourly rate for hours over 46 in a workweek, except under a public-safety work-period alternative, and employers subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act are excluded [^ks-overtime-after-46-hours] [^ks-overtime-employer-flsa-exclusion] [^ks-public-safety-short-work-period].
The overtime section does not cover employees covered by section 7 of the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, certain motor-vehicle salespeople, or people in corrections custody or serving a county jail sentence [^ks-overtime-section-exceptions]. The law's definition of employee excludes other listed groups [^ks-overtime-employee-exclusions]. Qualifying emergency medical, fire protection and law enforcement employees may instead be paid under subsection (b)'s work-period alternative [^ks-public-safety-short-work-period].
The official statute text prints the multiplier as a fraction, 1 1/2, which appears in the quoted text as 11/2 [^ks-overtime-after-46-hours].
The Kansas rule is weekly. It turns on hours worked in a workweek and does not set a daily overtime threshold [^ks-overtime-after-46-hours].
In addition, the law's definition of employee leaves out listed groups. Examples include agricultural workers, domestic workers in private homes, and bona fide executive, administrative and professional employees as the secretary's rules define them [^ks-overtime-employee-exclusions].
Subsection (b) is an alternative way to comply, not an automatic replacement for the 46-hour rule. For an employee covered by the overtime section who is an emergency medical service provider as K.S.A. 65-6112 defines it, or who is engaged in fire protection or law enforcement (including security personnel in a correctional institution), an employer does not violate the 46-hour rule if it pays at least one and one-half times the regular rate for tours of duty that in the aggregate exceed 258 hours in a 28-consecutive-day work period [^ks-public-safety-short-work-period]. For such an employee whose work period is at least seven but fewer than 28 days, the alternative uses the proportional number of hours: a number that bears the same ratio to the days in the work period as 258 hours bears to 28 days [^ks-public-safety-short-work-period].
For employees engaged in fire protection or law enforcement, an agreement or practice of substituting for one another on regularly scheduled tours of duty is treated as having no effect on hours of work if four conditions are met: the substitution is voluntary and not at the employer's behest, it is due to the employee's personal matter rather than the employer's business practice, the employer keeps a record of all substituted time, and the period in which time is substituted and paid back does not exceed 12 months [^ks-public-safety-shift-substitution].
Federal law on this point is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** No Kansas statute requires employers to give adult employees meal or rest breaks, and the Kansas child labor rules for children under sixteen do not require a break either [^ks-child-under-16-hour-limits].

For children under sixteen in the occupations the act covers, including hotels, restaurants and mercantile establishments, the Kansas child labor law sets these hours limits [^ks-child-under-16-hour-limits]:

- no work before 7 a.m.
- no work after 10 p.m., except on an evening that does not come before a school day
- no more than eight hours in a calendar day
- no more than 40 hours in a week

These limits do not apply to students working in school food service preparation. They also do not apply to student-learners in qualifying cooperative vocational training programs who work under a written agreement that meets the statute's conditions [^ks-child-hour-limit-exceptions].

Where children under sixteen are employed, permitted or suffered to work, the employer must keep posted in a conspicuous place near the principal entrance a notice stating the maximum hours such a child may work on each day of the week, the hours of starting and stopping work, and the hours allowed for dinner or other meals [^ks-child-hours-posted-notice]. That posting rule requires the notice to state the hours allowed for meals, but it does not itself set how long a meal period must be [^ks-child-hours-posted-notice].

Employers of children under sixteen in the covered vocations must first obtain a work permit and keep it on file and accessible to any inspector or officer charged with enforcing the act, but no permit is required if the child is enrolled in or attending a secondary school in Kansas [^ks-child-work-permit-requirement].

Separately, no child under fourteen may be employed at any occupation or trade in any business or service, except for activities the act does not treat as employment [^ks-child-under-14-employment-bar] [^ks-child-labor-exempt-activities]. Those activities include children employed by their parents in nonhazardous occupations, domestic service, casual labor in or around a private home, delivering or distributing newspapers, and agricultural pursuits [^ks-child-labor-exempt-activities]. A child attending school may not perform those exempt services during hours when the public school is in session in the district where the child resides [^ks-exempt-services-school-hours].

Within the employment the Kansas child labor act covers, that act also bars employing any child under eighteen in any occupation, trade or business that is dangerous or injurious to the minor's life, health, safety, morals or welfare [^ks-minor-hazardous-work-bar] [^ks-child-labor-exempt-activities].

Federal law on this point is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** Kansas employers must pay a departing employee's earned wages no later than the next regular payday on which the employee would have been paid if still employed, whether the employee was discharged, quit or resigned [^ks-final-pay-next-payday].
Payment may go through the regular pay channels. If the employee asks, it may instead be sent by mail postmarked within the deadline [^ks-final-pay-next-payday].
**Penalty.** When an employer willfully fails to pay wages as the final-pay rule requires, or as the regular payday rule requires, it is liable for the wages due plus a penalty [^ks-willful-nonpayment-daily-penalty]. The penalty is 1% of the unpaid wages for each day, except Sunday and legal holidays, that the failure continues after the eighth day after payment is required, or 100% of the unpaid wages, whichever is less [^ks-willful-nonpayment-daily-penalty]. For this penalty, the failure to pay is not treated as continuing in two situations: after the filing of a bankruptcy petition on which the employer is adjudicated bankrupt, and after an appeal under K.S.A. 44-322a is filed, until the decision on that appeal becomes final [^ks-final-pay-penalty-tolling].
**Disputed amounts.** When the parties dispute how much is owed, the employer must pay all wages it concedes are due, without conditions and no later than the next regular payday after the concession. The employee keeps every remedy as to any remaining balance [^ks-conceded-wages-next-payday]. Unless payment is made under a binding settlement agreement, accepting a conceded-wage payment does not release the rest of the claim, and any release an employer requires as a condition of payment violates the act and is null and void [^ks-conceded-payment-no-release].
**Deceased employees.** If the employer has no actual notice of probate proceedings, it may pay a deceased employee's wages on proper demand. Payment follows this order of preference: spouse, children 18 or older in equal shares, father, mother, sisters and brothers in equal shares, or the person owed funeral expenses [^ks-deceased-employee-wage-payment].

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Kansas employers must pay all wages due at least once each calendar month on regular paydays designated in advance, and a pay period may end no more than 15 days before payday unless state or federal law authorizes a variance [^ks-monthly-regular-payday-rule] [^ks-pay-period-fifteen-day-lag].

**Payment methods.** An employer may choose how to pay wages, but only by these methods [^ks-wage-payment-methods-list]:

- lawful money of the United States
- a check or draft negotiable in the community where the workplace is located
- electronic fund transfer or deposit to an automated clearinghouse member financial institution account the employee designates
- payroll card

An employer that pays only by electronic transfer or deposit must offer another payment method as the default for employees who do not designate an account [^ks-electronic-pay-default-option]. At least 30 days before starting a payroll program that uses only direct deposit or payroll cards, the employer must either hold one or more employee forums on using the program or distribute educational information to employees about it [^ks-electronic-pay-education-notice].

An employer that pays by payroll card must allow at least one free withdrawal per pay period, for up to the employee's full net wages [^ks-payroll-card-free-withdrawal]. It may not charge initiation, loading or other participation fees, other than the cost of replacing a lost, stolen or damaged card [^ks-payroll-card-fee-ban]. The employer keeps no interest in wages paid to an employee's payroll card account, other than the right to correct inadvertent overpayments under the rules governing direct deposit [^ks-payroll-card-no-employer-interest].

**Deductions.** The Wage Payment Act allows an employer to withhold, deduct or divert wages only in the cases it lists. Subsection (a) of the deduction section lets an employer withhold, deduct or divert wages where [^ks-wage-deduction-permitted-cases]:

- state or federal law requires or empowers the employer to make it
- it is for medical, surgical or hospital care or service, without financial benefit to the employer, and is openly, clearly and in due course recorded in the employer's books
- the employer has the employee's signed authorization for a lawful purpose that benefits the employee
- it is a contribution attributable to automatic enrollment, as K.S.A. 44-319a defines it, in a retirement plan the employer established that is described in Internal Revenue Code sections 401(k), 403(b), 408, 408A or 457

Under a signed written agreement, an employer may also deduct wages to repay a loan or advance it made to the employee during and within the scope of employment, to recover a payroll overpayment, or to cover the replacement cost or unpaid balance of employer merchandise or uniforms the employee bought [^ks-written-agreement-wage-deductions].

From final wages, after giving written notice and an explanation, an employer may withhold amounts to recover employer property provided to the employee in the course of the business, such as tools, safety equipment, computers, mobile phones, customer lists, or keys and access cards, until the employee returns the property [^ks-final-wage-deductions-notice].

The deduction section also does not prohibit withholding amounts the employee authorizes in writing to be contributed to charitable organizations, or deductions by check-off of dues to labor organizations or service fees where not otherwise prohibited by law [^ks-charity-and-union-deductions].

In every case, the amounts withheld may not reduce wages below the minimum wage required under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act or under the Kansas minimum wage section, whichever applies [^ks-deductions-minimum-wage-floor].

**Pay information.** The cited sections do not say what a pay stub must show with every paycheck. Instead, they require information when the employee asks for it. On request, the employer must tell the employee the rate of pay and the day and place of payment, in writing or as a collective bargaining agreement requires [^ks-employee-notices-on-request]. On request, the employer must also furnish the employee an itemized statement of the deductions made from the employee's wages for each pay period in which deductions are made [^ks-employee-notices-on-request]. And on request, the employer must make its policies on vacation pay, sick leave and other benefits that directly affect wages available in writing or in a posted notice [^ks-employee-notices-on-request].

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** The Kansas Supreme Court uses a 20-factor test to decide whether a Kansas worker is an employee under the Wage Payment Act, a test that includes economic reality considerations while keeping the primary focus on the employer's right to control [^ks-craig-twenty-factor-test] [^ks-craig-economic-reality-control].
In that decision, *Craig v. FedEx Ground Package System, Inc.*, the court held that the FedEx delivery drivers in that case were employees for purposes of the Wage Payment Act [^ks-craig-fedex-drivers-employees].
The Wage Payment Act itself defines an employee as any person allowed or permitted to work by an employer [^ks-wage-act-employee-definition]. The Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law, a separate act, defines employ as to suffer or permit to work [^ks-employ-suffer-or-permit].
The Wage Payment Act gives its definitions as used in that act, and the Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law gives its definitions as used in that law [^ks-wage-act-employer-definition] [^ks-employ-suffer-or-permit]. Neither statutory definition itself states a test for separating employees from independent contractors; the *Craig* decision supplies the Wage Payment Act test [^ks-wage-act-employee-definition] [^ks-employ-suffer-or-permit] [^ks-craig-twenty-factor-test].
The Wage Payment Act's definition of employer is broad. It covers individuals, partnerships, corporations, limited liability companies and other organizations. It also covers the state and its agencies, and cities, counties, school districts and other political subdivisions that employ any person [^ks-wage-act-employer-definition].
For the employment security law only, services performed for wages or under any contract of hire count as employment if the business keeps the right to control both the end result and the manner and means of reaching it [^ks-employment-security-right-to-control].

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Kansas employers covered by the Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law may count tips toward the minimum wage if tips have customarily been part of the employee's pay and the employee actually received and retained them [^ks-tips-count-toward-minimum].
For employees who receive tips and gratuities, the employer must pay a cash wage of at least $2.13 an hour [^ks-tipped-cash-wage-floor].
If that cash wage plus the tips and gratuities received comes to at least $7.25 an hour, the employer owes no further payment [^ks-tipped-wage-make-up-duty]. If the total is less than $7.25 an hour, the employer must pay the difference between $7.25 an hour and the employee's combined hourly amount [^ks-tipped-wage-make-up-duty].
A gratuity is a voluntary payment of money an employee receives from a guest, patron or customer for services rendered [^ks-gratuity-definition-voluntary].
Rules and regulations under the Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law may also define and govern allowances for board, lodging and gratuities as part of the wage rates that law requires [^ks-board-lodging-gratuity-allowances].
These tip rules are part of the Kansas minimum wage section, which does not apply to employers and employees covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act [^ks-tip-credit-flsa-carveout]. Federal law on this point is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## How is it enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Kansas workers may bring Wage Payment Act claims in any court of competent jurisdiction, and the secretary of labor enforces and administers that act and is empowered to investigate violations [^ks-wage-act-suit-any-court] [^ks-secretary-enforces-wage-act].

**Wage Payment Act.**

- **Investigations.** The secretary is empowered to hold hearings and to investigate violations or alleged violations [^ks-secretary-enforces-wage-act]. The secretary or authorized representatives are also empowered to enter places, question employees and investigate facts, conditions or matters they deem appropriate to determine whether anyone has violated the act [^ks-secretary-wage-act-entry-powers]. They may administer oaths, examine witnesses, issue compulsory process to compel witnesses to attend and to produce payrolls, records and other documents, and take depositions and affidavits [^ks-secretary-compels-wage-records].
- **Claim assignment.** When the secretary determines that an employee has a valid unpaid-wage claim of less than $10,000, the secretary must, on the employee's written request, take an assignment of the claim in trust and act to enforce or defend it [^ks-secretary-wage-claim-assignment]. For a valid claim of $10,000 or more, the secretary may take the assignment on the employee's written request and, if it is taken, must act to enforce or defend the claim [^ks-secretary-large-claim-assignment]. The fee fixed by rules and regulations for an assigned claim may be no more than $25 per claim [^ks-assigned-claim-fee-cap]. If the secretary wins on the employee's behalf, the court must award the agency a judgment equal to the cost of reasonable attorney fees [^ks-agency-attorney-fee-judgment].
- **Interest.** The presiding officer may, at their discretion, assess interest on wage claims found due, running from the date the wages were due [^ks-wage-claim-interest-assessment].
- **Individual liability.** An officer, manager, major shareholder or other person in charge of an employer's affairs may be treated as the employer under the act if that person knowingly permits violations of the payday or final-pay rules [^ks-officer-deemed-employer-liability].
- **Subcontractor wages.** A person responsible under a contract for work who subcontracts all or part of it is civilly liable to the subcontractor's employees for wages due for work covered by the contract, but only if and to the extent the subcontractor fails to pay those wages [^ks-contractor-liable-subcontractor-wages].
- **No waiver.** Except as the act's section on employee suits and claim assignments provides, no provision of the act, and no right it creates, may be contravened, set aside or waived [^ks-wage-act-no-waiver]. Unless payment is made under a binding settlement agreement, a release an employer requires as a condition of paying conceded wages is null and void [^ks-wage-act-release-null-void].

**Minimum Wage and Maximum Hours Law.**

- **Employee suits.** An employer that pays less than the minimum wages and overtime owed is liable to the employee for the full amount of wages and overtime owed, less any amount actually paid, plus costs and the reasonable attorney fees the court allows [^ks-unpaid-minimum-wage-liability]. One or more employees may bring that action in any court of competent jurisdiction [^ks-minimum-wage-suit-any-court]. An agreement to work for less than the applicable wage rate is no defense [^ks-underpayment-agreement-no-defense].
- **Claim assignment.** If an underpaid employee asks in writing, the secretary may take an assignment of the wage claim in trust and bring any legal action needed to collect it. The employer must then pay costs and the reasonable attorney fees the court allows [^ks-minimum-wage-claim-assignment]. In a suit, the secretary may join various claimants against the same employer in one action [^ks-secretary-joins-wage-claimants].
- **Inspections.** Under that law, the secretary or an authorized representative may enter and inspect a place of business or employment only after receiving a written complaint from an employee who alleges being aggrieved under the law [^ks-inspection-requires-written-complaint].
- **Criminal fines.** An employer convicted of violating that law, or of falsifying a related record, is fined $250 to $1,000 [^ks-minimum-wage-violation-fines].
- **Retaliation.** An employer may not discharge or otherwise discriminate against an employee for complaining about unpaid wages under that law, for starting or being about to start a proceeding under it, or for testifying or being about to testify in one. An employer that does so violates the law and, on conviction, is fined $250 to $1,000 [^ks-minimum-wage-retaliation-fines].
- **Records.** That law requires every employer subject to it to keep, for at least three years and in or about the premises where employees work, records of each employee's name, occupation, rate of pay, amount paid each pay period, and hours worked each day and each workweek [^ks-minimum-wage-records-three-years]. An employer covered by the Fair Labor Standards Act may keep the records that federal act requires instead [^ks-federal-records-in-lieu].

**General time limits.** Two general Kansas limitation rules may matter here. An action upon a liability created by a statute, other than a penalty or forfeiture, must be brought within three years [^ks-three-year-statutory-liability]. An action upon a statutory penalty or forfeiture must be brought within one year [^ks-one-year-statutory-penalty]. These are general rules for actions of those kinds. The wage sections cited above do not state how they apply to a particular wage claim.

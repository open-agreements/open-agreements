# Wage and Hour Law in New Hampshire[^about]

How New Hampshire ties its minimum wage to the $7.25 federal rate, lets restaurants and hotels pay tipped employees 45 percent of it, applies its own weekly overtime rule only to employers outside the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, requires a 30-minute meal period after five consecutive hours, and sets final-pay, pay-frequency, deduction, worker-classification and wage-claim rules.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** New Hampshire's minimum wage is the federal minimum wage. Unless another statute provides otherwise, no employer may pay an employee an hourly rate lower than the rate in the federal minimum wage law, as amended. [^nh-minimum-wage-federal-rate] The federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour, so the New Hampshire rate is also $7.25 an hour. [^nh-federal-minimum-wage-rate] Because the state rate follows the federal law as amended, a future federal increase would raise the New Hampshire rate without a change to the state statute. [^nh-minimum-wage-federal-rate]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The state minimum wage does not apply to employees engaged in household labor, domestic labor or farm labor, to outside salespeople, or to employees of summer camps for minors. [^nh-minimum-wage-exemptions-household-farm] Under state law, an employer may pay a person 16 years of age or under no less than 75 percent of the minimum wage, if it keeps evidence of the person's age on file. The same 75 percent floor applies to a person with less than six months' experience in an occupation, after the employer applies to the Labor Commissioner within 10 days after hire. [^nh-minimum-wage-exemptions-household-farm] These state exceptions do not affect the federal minimum wage, so an employee covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act remains entitled to $7.25 an hour unless a separate federal exception applies. [^nh-federal-minimum-wage-rate]

An employer may not pay an individual with a disability less than the state minimum wage, except under two narrow statutory provisions. [^nh-no-subminimum-wage-disability] An employer that pays less than the minimum wage commits a misdemeanor if it is a natural person, or a felony otherwise, and each week and each underpaid employee is a separate offense. [^nh-minimum-wage-criminal-penalty]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** No New Hampshire statute requires daily overtime pay. The state overtime rule requires time and one-half for all time worked over 40 hours in a week, but it excludes every employee of an employer covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act. [^nh-overtime-non-flsa-employers] For most employers, then, overtime is governed by federal law, which requires at least one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek unless a federal exemption applies. [^nh-federal-overtime-forty-hours]

The state overtime rule also excludes employees of an amusement, seasonal or recreational establishment that operates no more than seven months in a calendar year, or that earned at least 75 percent of its income within six months of the previous year. [^nh-overtime-non-flsa-employers] An employer covered by the federal law that pays overtime to delivery drivers or sales merchandisers may not calculate that overtime by the fluctuating workweek method in 29 C.F.R. § 778.114. [^nh-overtime-non-flsa-employers]

Under federal law, an exempt executive, administrative or professional employee generally must be paid on a salary basis of at least $684 a week, among other requirements. [^nh-federal-white-collar-salary-684] The federal salary rule has exceptions, which are discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us). New Hampshire separately requires that a salaried employee receive the full salary for any pay period in which the employee performs any work, without regard to the number of days or hours worked, subject to listed exceptions. [^nh-salaried-full-salary-any-work]

Subject to statutory and regulatory exceptions, an employee who reports to work at the employer's request must be paid at least two hours' pay at the regular rate, unless the employer made a good-faith effort to tell the employee not to report. The rule does not apply to county and municipal employees, or to ski and snowboard instructors at ski resorts who receive other compensation at least equal to their rate of pay. [^nh-reporting-pay-two-hours] The Department of Labor's rules add exceptions for an employee who reports and then asks to leave because of illness or a personal or family emergency, with a written explanation initialed by the employee, and for an employee hired with written notice that the job consistently requires less than two hours a day. [^nh-reporting-pay-rule-exceptions] [^nh-reporting-pay-two-hours]

## Are meal and rest breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** An employer may not require an employee to work more than five consecutive hours without a 30-minute lunch or eating period, unless eating while working is feasible and the employer permits it. [^nh-meal-period-five-hours] The meal period is not required if it is feasible for the employee to eat while working and the employer permits the employee to do so. [^nh-meal-period-five-hours] The statute does not say whether the meal period must be paid. For an employee covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, a bona fide meal period is not worktime only if the employee is completely relieved from duty, and an employee required to perform duties while eating is working. [^nh-federal-bona-fide-meal-periods] New Hampshire's Department of Labor incorporates those federal hours-worked rules, 29 C.F.R. Part 785, when it determines the wages due under the state wage payment law. [^nh-hours-worked-federal-part-785] No New Hampshire statute requires general rest breaks for adult employees.

An employer with six or more employees working in New Hampshire must provide reasonable break periods to an employee who needs to express milk for a child, for one year from the child's birth. [^nh-lactation-break-one-year] [^nh-lactation-break-definitions] A reasonable break period is an unpaid break of approximately 30 minutes for every three hours of work, and the employer may not require the employee to make up the time. [^nh-lactation-break-definitions] [^nh-lactation-break-one-year] An employer may be exempt if providing the break time and space would impose an undue hardship on its operations. [^nh-lactation-hardship-exemption]

The Department of Labor's rules require employers to keep time records showing when work began and ended, including any bona fide meal periods. [^nh-time-records-meal-periods]

## When are final wages due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** An employer that discharges an employee must pay the employee's wages in full within 72 hours. [^nh-final-pay-discharge-quit] An employee who quits must be paid no later than the next regular payday, but if the employee gives at least one pay period's notice of intent to quit, all wages earned are due within 72 hours. [^nh-final-pay-discharge-quit] An employee who is laid off, or whose work is suspended by a labor dispute, must be paid by the next regular payday. [^nh-final-pay-layoff-next-payday]
An employer that willfully and without good cause fails to pay final wages on time owes liquidated damages of 10 percent of the unpaid wages for each day, except Sundays and legal holidays, that the failure continues, or an amount equal to the unpaid wages, whichever is smaller. [^nh-final-pay-liquidated-damages]

The New Hampshire Supreme Court has read willfully in this statute to mean voluntarily, with knowledge of the obligation and despite the financial ability to pay. [^nh-ives-willfully-financial-ability] The Department of Labor's rules add that discharge pay is given in person or by mail, at the employee's choice, and that an employer that will not let a quitting employee work the notice period must pay within 72 hours. [^nh-final-pay-rule-notice-period]

Vacation pay, severance pay, personal days, holiday pay, sick pay and employee expense payments count as wages when due, if they are a matter of employment practice or policy. [^nh-fringe-benefits-are-wages] An employer may deduct an outstanding employer loan or wage overpayment from final wages only if the employee's written agreement specifically says whether the employer may do so. [^nh-deduction-loans-final-wages] The same written term is required before an employer deducts outstanding tuition repayment for non-required education from final wages. [^nh-final-pay-tuition-deduction]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** New Hampshire generally requires weekly or biweekly pay unless the Labor Commissioner authorizes less frequent payment. [^nh-pay-frequency-monthly-permission] An employer must pay all wages due within eight days after the end of the workweek for an employee paid weekly, or within 15 days after the end of the workweek for an employee paid every two weeks, on regular paydays designated in advance and at no cost to the employee. [^nh-pay-frequency-weekly-biweekly] On a written petition showing good reason, the Labor Commissioner may permit less frequent pay, but never less than once each calendar month, and always on a predesignated date. [^nh-pay-frequency-monthly-permission]

The Department of Labor grants permission to pay semi-monthly or monthly case by case. Among other conditions, the employer must have no history of wage and hour violations, and the employees must not suffer financial hardship from less frequent pay. [^nh-pay-frequency-permission-conditions]

Wages may be paid in cash, by electronic fund transfer, by direct deposit with the employee's written authorization, by payroll card with the employee's voluntary written consent, or by check on a convenient financial institution. [^nh-pay-frequency-weekly-biweekly] [^nh-pay-methods-check-option] [^nh-payroll-card-written-consent] An employer that pays electronically or by payroll card must also offer payment by check. [^nh-pay-methods-check-option] [^nh-payroll-card-written-consent]

## What pay notices and deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** No New Hampshire statute requires an itemized pay stub listing hours and pay rates. New Hampshire instead requires notices about pay and deductions. An employer must tell employees at hiring the rate of pay and the day and place of payment, tell them of changes before the changes take effect, and give each employee a statement of the deductions taken from wages for each pay period in which deductions are made. [^nh-pay-notice-deduction-statement] The Department of Labor's rules require the hiring notice to be in writing. [^nh-written-pay-rate-notice]
An employer may withhold or divert wages only on a ground the statute lists, such as a deduction required or allowed by state or federal law, or a deduction the employee has authorized in writing for a lawful purpose the statute or the Labor Commissioner's rules allow. [^nh-wage-withholding-general-rule]

Purposes the employee may authorize in writing include union dues, benefit fund contributions, charitable contributions, housing and utilities, and payments into savings funds held by someone other than the employer. [^nh-wage-withholding-general-rule] They also include any purpose the employer and employee mutually agree on that does not give the employer a financial advantage. A deduction may not be used to offset payments for items required to do the job in the ordinary course of the employer's business. [^nh-wage-withholding-written-authorization] The Department of Labor's rules bar an employer from requiring an employee or applicant to pay for a medical examination, non-required drug or alcohol testing, records the employer requires, or any item required by and for the benefit of the employer, as a condition of employment. Examinations, permits and licenses required by state or federal law are excluded. [^nh-no-charges-employer-required-items]

On the employee's written request, an employer may deduct repayment of tuition the employer paid for non-required education. The written authorization must state when payments begin and end, the amounts and whether any outstanding amount may be taken from final wages. [^nh-deduction-tuition-recovery] The Labor Commissioner may not first issue a cure warning before imposing a civil penalty for withholding wages to compensate the employer for loss or damage caused by the employee. [^nh-civil-penalty-no-warning-violations]

## Who is an employee? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** New Hampshire's wage payment law treats every person whom an employer permits, requires or directs to work for direct or indirect gain as an employee, unless the person meets all seven statutory criteria for independent status or falls within a listed exclusion. [^nh-employee-definition-seven-criteria] The criteria require that the person:
1. has or has applied for a federal employer identification number or social security number, or has agreed in writing to carry out the responsibilities imposed on employers; [^nh-employee-definition-seven-criteria]
2. controls the means and manner of the work, with the result of the work as the primary element bargained for; [^nh-employee-definition-seven-criteria]
3. controls when the work is performed, although the parties may agree on a completion schedule, a range of work hours and maximum hours; [^nh-contractor-criteria-control-assistants]
4. hires and pays any assistants and, to the extent they are employees, supervises the details of their work; [^nh-contractor-criteria-control-assistants]
5. holds himself or herself out as in business, or is registered with the state as a business, and has continuing or recurring business liabilities or obligations; [^nh-contractor-criteria-business-exclusive]
6. is responsible for satisfactory completion of the work and may be held contractually responsible for failing to complete it; and [^nh-contractor-criteria-business-exclusive]
7. is not required to work exclusively for the employer. [^nh-contractor-criteria-business-exclusive]

The minimum wage law uses the same seven criteria in its own definition of employee. [^nh-minimum-wage-employee-definition] Every employer must post an abstract of the wage payment law that includes the criteria for classifying a worker as an employee or an independent contractor. [^nh-posting-classification-criteria]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** New Hampshire allows a tip credit in listed businesses. A tipped employee of a restaurant, hotel, motel, inn, cabin or ballroom who customarily and regularly receives more than $30 a month in tips directly from customers must receive a base rate from the employer of at least 45 percent of the minimum wage. [^nh-tip-credit-45-percent-base] If the employee's wages at the end of a pay period do not reach the minimum wage for all hours worked, the employer must pay the difference. [^nh-tip-credit-shortfall-makeup]

An employer may not pay the tipped base rate for time spent on duties that do not regularly and customarily generate tips, unless the duties are incidental to the tipped employee's regular duties and generally assigned to tipped employees. [^nh-tip-credit-non-tipped-duties] Tips are wages and belong to the employee who receives them, unless the employee voluntarily and without employer coercion agrees to join a tip pool or tip-sharing arrangement. [^nh-tips-employee-property-pooling]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee may file a wage claim with the Department of Labor, or the department may file one on its own motion, no later than 36 months after the wages were due. [^nh-wage-claim-36-months] An employee may also sue in any court of competent jurisdiction to recover unpaid wages and liquidated damages, and the court may award costs and reasonable attorney's fees. [^nh-employee-wage-lawsuit] [^nh-wage-lawsuit-attorney-fees] An employee paid less than the minimum wage may recover the shortfall, costs and reasonable attorney's fees, and an agreement to work for less is no defense. [^nh-minimum-wage-civil-action]

Corporate officers and managing agents who knowingly permit the corporation to violate the pay-frequency or final-pay statutes are treated as the employers of the corporation's employees. [^nh-officers-deemed-employers] In addition to criminal penalties, the Labor Commissioner may, after a hearing, impose a civil penalty of up to $2,500 for a violation of the wage payment and minimum wage laws, unless another law authorizes a different amount. [^nh-civil-penalty-2500] The commissioner ordinarily gives one written warning and 30 days to cure, but gives none for failing to pay wages in full and on time or failing to pay final wages. [^nh-civil-penalty-no-warning-wage-claims]

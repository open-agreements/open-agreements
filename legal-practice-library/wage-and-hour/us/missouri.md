# Wage and Hour Law in Missouri[^about]

A plain-language guide to Missouri wage-and-hour statutes covering the $15.00 state minimum wage and its coverage limits, workweek overtime, child labor hour and break rules, final pay on discharge and the service letter, payday and deduction-statement rules, worker classification definitions, the tipped-wage credit, and the remedies and penalties the statutes authorize.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Missouri workers covered by the Minimum Wage Law are entitled to a minimum wage of $15.00 an hour, effective January 1, 2026 [^mo-minimum-wage-fifteen-dollar-rate]. If the federal minimum wage rate is ever higher than the Missouri rate, the higher federal rate immediately becomes the Missouri minimum wage [^mo-higher-federal-rate-controls]. Federal law is addressed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).
From 2008 on, the rate went up or down each January 1 with the cost of living, but those adjustments ended December 31, 2024 [^mo-cost-of-living-adjustments-ended].
Beginning August 28, 2025, the minimum wage section also applies to public employers with respect to their employees [^mo-public-employer-coverage-date]. A public employer for this purpose is the state or a political subdivision of the state, including state departments, agencies, boards and commissions, and cities, counties, towns, villages and school districts [^mo-public-employer-definition-scope].
The Minimum Wage Law's definition of employee leaves out a list of groups. That list includes individuals employed in a bona fide executive, administrative or professional capacity; individuals paid wholly or partly by sales commissions whose hours and places of work the employer does not substantially control; and individuals employed by a retail or service business with annual gross volume of sales made or business done under $500,000 [^mo-minimum-wage-employee-exclusions]. The list also includes, among others, people employed on a casual basis to babysit and individuals employed for less than four months a year at a resident or day camp for children or youth [^mo-minimum-wage-employee-exclusions].
The Minimum Wage Law also does not apply to certain employees or employers engaged in agriculture [^mo-minimum-wage-agriculture-exclusions]. The excluded situations include an employee whose employer did not, during any calendar quarter of the preceding calendar year, use more than five hundred man-days of agriculture labor; an employee who is the parent, spouse, child or other member of the employer's immediate family; and an employee principally engaged in the range production of livestock [^mo-minimum-wage-agriculture-exclusions].
After a public hearing of which affected employees must be given reasonable notice, the director provides by regulation for employing learners and apprentices at wages below the otherwise applicable rate, where the director finds it appropriate to prevent curtailment of employment opportunities [^mo-learner-apprentice-wage-rate]. That learner rate may not be less than 90 cents below the minimum wage, and the provision may not be used to evade the spirit and meaning of the Minimum Wage Law [^mo-learner-apprentice-wage-rate]. A learner or apprentice is an individual under 20 years of age who has not finished the required training for a particular job. That status generally ends after three months of training, unless the director finds after investigation that the occupation's minimum proficiency cannot be acquired in three months. In no case does it last beyond six months of training for a particular employer or job [^mo-learner-apprentice-definition-limits].
After a public hearing, the director must provide by regulation for employing individuals whose earning capacity is impaired by physical or mental deficiency at wages below the otherwise applicable rate [^mo-impaired-earning-capacity-regulation].
No political subdivision may establish, mandate or otherwise require an employer to provide an employee a minimum or living wage rate or employment benefits that exceed state laws, rules, or regulations [^mo-local-wage-benefit-mandate-preemption]. For this rule, employer does not include a public employer, and employment benefits include health, retirement and profit-sharing benefits and paid or unpaid days off [^mo-local-wage-benefit-mandate-preemption]. The Minimum Wage Law also preempts and nullifies political subdivision ordinances, rules and regulations, whether in effect now or enacted later, relating to the establishment or enforcement of a minimum or living wage or the provision of employment benefits that exceed state laws, rules, or regulations [^mo-local-wage-ordinances-preempted].
Nothing in the Minimum Wage Law diminishes employees' right to bargain collectively, through representatives of their own choosing, for wages or other conditions of work above the minimum [^mo-collective-bargaining-right-preserved].

"Thereafter, the minimum wage established by this subsection shall be increased by $1.25 per hour, to $15.00 per hour, effective January 1, 2026."[^mo-minimum-wage-fifteen-dollar-rate]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Missouri employers must pay employees covered by the Minimum Wage Law at least one and one-half times their regular rate for hours worked beyond forty in a workweek, subject to two exceptions in the statute [^mo-overtime-forty-hour-workweek] [^mo-overtime-covered-employee-exclusions]. Employees of an amusement or recreation business that meets criteria the statute borrows from federal law are owed time and a half only after fifty-two hours in a one-week period [^mo-overtime-amusement-recreation-fifty-two], and, apart from those amusement or recreation employees, employees exempt from federal minimum wage or overtime requirements are not covered by the forty-hour rule [^mo-overtime-federal-exempt-employees-excluded]. The Minimum Wage Law's overtime rule is measured by the workweek, not by the day [^mo-overtime-forty-hour-workweek].
Unless the Minimum Wage Law provides otherwise, the overtime section is interpreted in accordance with the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Portal to Portal Act and the regulations issued under them [^mo-overtime-flsa-portal-interpretation]. Federal law is addressed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).
A separate, much older Missouri statute makes eight hours a legal day's work but lets the parties to a contract for work agree on a longer or shorter time, and it sets no overtime pay [^mo-eight-hour-legal-day]. That eight-hour section does not apply to persons hired by the month or to laborers or farm hands working for farmers or others engaged in agriculture [^mo-legal-day-monthly-farm-exclusion].
Missouri's prevailing wage law requires that workers employed by or on behalf of a public body in the construction of public works, exclusive of maintenance work, be paid at least the prevailing hourly rate of wages for similar work in the locality or the public works contracting minimum wage, whichever applies [^mo-public-works-prevailing-rate-base]. For overtime work on public works construction, those workers must be paid at least one and one-half the prevailing hourly rate of wages or the public works contracting minimum wage, whichever applies, and overtime work includes work over ten hours in one day and work over forty hours in one calendar week [^mo-public-works-overtime-rate]. Only workers directly employed by contractors or subcontractors in actual construction work on the site of the building or construction job are deemed employed upon public works [^mo-public-works-onsite-workers-only]. Workers who haul materials or equipment are also deemed employed directly on public works when the hauling includes some phase of construction other than mere transportation to the site [^mo-public-works-hauling-dual-capacity]. The prevailing wage sections do not apply to the construction of public works for which the engineer's estimate or the bid the public body accepted for the total project cost is seventy-five thousand dollars or less [^mo-public-works-small-project-threshold]. When a change order raises the total project cost of such a project above seventy-five thousand dollars, those sections apply only to the portion of the project in excess of seventy-five thousand dollars [^mo-public-works-change-order-overtime-scope].

"No employer shall employ any of his employees for a workweek longer than forty hours unless such employee receives compensation for his employment in excess of the hours above specified at a rate not less than one and one-half times the regular rate at which he is employed."[^mo-overtime-forty-hour-workweek]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** No Missouri statute generally requires employers to give workers aged sixteen or older meal periods or rest breaks. Under Missouri's child labor law, a child may not work more than three hours on a school day, eight hours on a nonschool day, or more than six days or forty hours in a week [^mo-child-daily-weekly-hour-limits]. The child labor chapter defines a child as an individual under sixteen years of age, unless otherwise specified [^mo-child-defined-under-sixteen].
No child under fourteen may be employed or permitted to work at any occupation at any time, except as the child labor chapter provides [^mo-child-under-fourteen-work-bar]. A child's normal work hours may not begin before 7 a.m. or run past 9 p.m., except as the evening-hours subsection provides [^mo-child-normal-work-hours-window]. The director may waive those limits in full or in part, in writing to the employer. The waiver exempts only the entertainment-industry employment that the child labor law covers in a separate section [^mo-child-hours-director-waiver] [^mo-entertainment-industry-definition].
From Labor Day to June 1, a child may not work after 7 p.m. From June 1 to Labor Day, a child may not work after 9 p.m. A child who is past his or her fourteenth birthday but under sixteen may work at a regional fair from June 1 to Labor Day until 10:30 p.m., if supervised by an adult and with parental consent, but the regional fair exception does not apply to entities covered by the Fair Labor Standards Act [^mo-child-evening-work-limits]. These evening limits do not apply to children who have been permanently excused from school [^mo-child-evening-limits-school-excused].
Section 294.022 governs children employed in the entertainment industry, which it defines to include performers, extras and other performing roles in motion picture, television, radio, theatrical and modeling productions, horse shows, rodeos and musical performances, and no child may be employed in that industry without an entertainment work permit issued by the director [^mo-entertainment-industry-definition] [^mo-entertainment-work-permit-required]. For children working in the entertainment industry, meal periods must last at least one-half hour and no more than one hour. Meal periods do not count toward the hours a child may be at the place of employment, and a child may not be there longer than five and one-half hours without a meal break [^mo-entertainment-child-meal-periods]. Such a child also receives a fifteen-minute rest period after each two hours of continuous work [^mo-entertainment-child-rest-period], and a twelve-hour rest break between the end of one workday and the start of the next for the same employment [^mo-entertainment-child-twelve-hour-rest]. Meal-break periods are not included in overall work time, but rest breaks are [^mo-entertainment-meal-rest-work-time].
For workers on public works projects covered by Missouri's prevailing wage law, that law requires a thirty-minute lunch period on each calendar day for each worker, and that time is not counted as time worked [^mo-public-works-daily-lunch-period]. The prevailing wage sections do not apply to the construction of public works for which the engineer's estimate or the bid the public body accepted for the total project cost is seventy-five thousand dollars or less [^mo-public-works-small-project-lunch-threshold]. When a change order raises the total project cost of such a project above seventy-five thousand dollars, those sections apply only to the portion of the project in excess of seventy-five thousand dollars [^mo-public-works-change-order-lunch-scope].
Federal law is addressed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

"In no event may a child be at the place of employment for a period longer than five and one-half hours without a meal break."[^mo-entertainment-child-meal-periods]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** When a Missouri employer discharges an employee or refuses to employ the employee further, the employee's unpaid earned wages become due on the day of the discharge or refusal, with an exception for certain commissioned employees [^mo-final-pay-discharge-deadline] [^mo-final-pay-commission-exception]. That exception covers an employee paid primarily by commission whose duties include collecting accounts, caring for stock or merchandise, or similar activities, where an audit is necessary or customary to determine the net amount due [^mo-final-pay-commission-exception]. The wages are owed at the contract rate, without abatement or deduction [^mo-final-pay-discharge-deadline].
The employee may ask a foreman or timekeeper, in writing, to send the money or a valid check to any station or office where a regular agent is kept. If it does not arrive within seven days of the request, the employee's wages continue as a penalty from the date of discharge, at the same rate, until paid. The penalty runs for no more than sixty days [^mo-final-pay-discharge-deadline]. The section addresses discharge or refusal to further employ. Section 290.110 does not state a deadline for employees who quit [^mo-final-pay-discharge-deadline].
An employee who hides or stays away to avoid payment, or refuses payment that is fully tendered, loses the benefit of these provisions for the time payment is avoided [^mo-final-pay-avoided-payment-forfeiture].
An employee hired for a definite period and discharged without cause before that period ends may also sue for damages caused by the wrongful discharge, and may join that claim with the claim for unpaid wages and penalty [^mo-definite-term-discharge-damages].
A corporation doing business in Missouri with seven or more employees has a service-letter duty. It applies when an employee with at least ninety days of service is discharged or quits and, within a reasonable time but no later than one year from the date the employee was discharged or quit, sends a written request by certified mail to the corporation's superintendent, manager or registered agent that specifically refers to the statute. Within forty-five days of receiving the request, the corporation must issue a signed letter stating the nature, character and duration of the service and truly stating the cause, if any, for the discharge or quitting [^mo-service-letter-corporate-duty]. A corporation that violates this duty is liable for compensatory but not punitive damages. If it issued no letter at all, it may be liable for nominal and punitive damages, but punitive damages may not be based on the content of a letter [^mo-service-letter-damages-limits].

"No such servant or employee who secretes or absents himself to avoid payment to him, or refuses to receive the same when fully tendered, shall be entitled to any benefit under sections 290.110 and 290.120 for such time as he so avoids payment."[^mo-final-pay-avoided-payment-forfeiture]

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Missouri corporations, and persons operating railroads or railroad shops in the state, must pay employees at least semimonthly, within sixteen days of the close of each payroll period, subject to a monthly-pay option for some employees [^mo-semimonthly-payday-corporations-railroads]. At the employer's option, executive, administrative and professional employees, and salespeople and others paid wholly or partly on commission, may be paid monthly [^mo-semimonthly-payday-corporations-railroads].
At least once a month, these same employers must give each employee a statement showing the total amount of deductions for the period. The statement may be part of the check, draft or voucher, or it may be separate [^mo-monthly-deduction-statement-required]. A corporation or person that violates this section is guilty of a misdemeanor and, on conviction, faces a fine of not less than fifty dollars nor more than five hundred dollars for each offense [^mo-payday-statute-misdemeanor-fine].
Operators of manufactories in Missouri, including plate glass manufactories, must pay employees in full, in lawful money, at least once every fifteen days. On any payday they may hold back no more than the employee's earnings for the five days just before it [^mo-manufactory-fifteen-day-payday]. An operator that fails and refuses to pay as required becomes liable to the employee for double the sum due, recoverable in a civil action. An employee does not waive these rights by signing a contrary contract [^mo-manufactory-double-wages-no-waiver].
A railway, mining, express, telegraph, manufacturing or other company or corporation doing business in Missouri that wants to reduce wages must give the affected employees thirty days' notice [^mo-wage-reduction-thirty-day-notice]. The notice may be given by posting a written or printed handbill, naming the class of employees whose wages are to be reduced and the amount of the reduction, in a conspicuous place where those employees work, or by mailing each employee a copy [^mo-wage-reduction-notice-method-forfeiture]. A company or corporation that violates the section forfeits and pays each affected party fifty dollars, recoverable by civil action with costs [^mo-wage-reduction-notice-method-forfeiture].
Every employer subject to the Minimum Wage Law must keep records for at least three years. The records must show each employee's name, address and occupation, rate of pay, amount paid each pay period, hours worked each day and each workweek, and any goods or services the employer provides to the employee as wages [^mo-minimum-wage-records-three-years]. Records kept outside Missouri must be made available to the director on demand. Records and information obtained by the department of labor and industrial relations are confidential and may be disclosed only on a court order [^mo-records-out-of-state-confidential].

"Such corporations and persons either as a part of the check, draft or other voucher paying the wages or separately, shall furnish the employee at least once a month a statement showing the total amount of deductions for the period."[^mo-monthly-deduction-statement-required]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** In *Karnes v. Happy Trails RV Park, LLC*, a federal court applying Missouri's wage and hour law treated employee status under that law the same as under the Fair Labor Standards Act, because the Missouri law is generally interpreted in accordance with the federal act [^mo-karnes-mwhl-follows-flsa]. The court weighed six factors: the degree of control the alleged employer exercised over the business operations, the relative investments of the alleged employer and employee, the degree to which the employer determined the worker's opportunity for profit and loss, the skill and initiative the job required, the permanency of the relationship, and the degree to which the worker's tasks were integral to the employer's business [^mo-karnes-six-economic-factors]. The court described those factors as only a guide, explaining that the economic reality of the situation, not any technical concept, determines the outcome [^mo-karnes-economic-reality-controls].
Missouri's Minimum Wage Law defines an employee as any individual employed by an employer, subject to listed exclusions, and its definitions do not themselves state a multi-factor or control test [^mo-minimum-wage-employee-definition] [^mo-minimum-wage-employer-wage-definitions]. Under the same law, an employer includes any person acting directly or indirectly in the interest of an employer in relation to an employee. A wage is compensation due to an employee because of the employment, payable in United States legal tender or in checks convertible into cash on demand at full face value [^mo-minimum-wage-employer-wage-definitions]. Unless the Minimum Wage Law provides otherwise, its overtime section is interpreted in accordance with the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Portal to Portal Act and their regulations [^mo-classification-flsa-interpretation-rule]. Federal law is addressed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).
Missouri's employment security law takes a different approach. Under that law, service performed for pay is deemed employment unless the division is satisfied that an independent contractor performed it [^mo-employment-security-presumed-employment]. For that law only, the common law of agency right to control test treats the individual as an employee if the alleged employer keeps the right to control the manner and means of accomplishing the results [^mo-employment-security-right-to-control]. If only the results are controlled, the individual is an independent contractor [^mo-employment-security-results-only-contractor].
Missouri's workers' compensation chapter defines an employee as every person in the service of an employer under any contract of hire, express or implied, oral or written, or under any appointment or election, including executive officers of corporations [^mo-workers-compensation-employee-definition].
A separate misclassification law applies only to employers that have or had five or more individuals performing public works. Under that law, an employee is an individual whose services indicate an employer-employee relationship under the factors in Internal Revenue Service Revenue Ruling 87-41 [^mo-public-works-misclassification-definitions]. An employer knowingly misclassifies a worker if it fails to claim the worker as an employee while knowing the worker is one [^mo-knowing-misclassification-defined]. If a court finds knowing misclassification, it must enter judgment for the state and award penalties of fifty dollars per day per misclassified worker, up to fifty thousand dollars [^mo-misclassification-daily-penalty].

"If only the results are controlled, the individual performing the service is an independent contractor."[^mo-employment-security-results-only-contractor]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Missouri employers may pay tipped employees as little as fifty percent of the minimum wage rate, which the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations puts at $7.50 an hour, but total compensation must reach at least the minimum wage [^mo-tipped-wage-half-minimum] [^mo-tipped-cash-wage-floor-guidance]. The employer makes up any difference [^mo-tipped-wage-half-minimum].

"No employer of any employee who receives and retains compensation in the form of gratuities in addition to wages is required to pay wages in excess of fifty percent of the minimum wage rate specified in sections 290.500 to 290.530, however, total compensation for such employee shall total at least the minimum wage specified in sections 290.500 to 290.530, the difference being made up by the employer."[^mo-tipped-wage-half-minimum]

## How is it enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A Missouri employer that pays an employee less than the Minimum Wage Law requires is liable for the full wage plus twice the unpaid wages as liquidated damages, less amounts paid, plus costs and reasonable attorney fees [^mo-unpaid-wage-liquidated-damages].
The employee may bring any legal action necessary to collect the claim [^mo-employee-wage-collection-action]. An agreement between employee and employer to work for less than the wage rate is no defense [^mo-underpayment-agreement-no-defense]. Actions to collect a wage deficiency must be started within three years after the cause of action accrues [^mo-wage-claim-three-year-limit].
Under the Minimum Wage Law, the director has authority to investigate and ascertain the wages of persons employed in any occupation the law covers [^mo-director-wage-investigation-authority].
Under the Minimum Wage Law, an employer commits a class C misdemeanor if it hinders the director's enforcement through any of the listed acts. Those acts are refusing the director entry, failing to keep or falsifying required records, refusing access to records or a sworn statement, and failing to post the required summary. They also include discharging or discriminating against an employee who notified the director of unpaid wages, started a proceeding, or testified or is about to testify in one [^mo-minimum-wage-class-c-misdemeanor]. The list also covers paying or agreeing to pay less than the applicable rate, where each week or part of a week is a separate offense as to each employee, and otherwise violating the law [^mo-minimum-wage-class-c-misdemeanor], where each day is a separate offense [^mo-each-day-separate-offense].
Records required under the Minimum Wage Law must be open to the director's inspection by appointment [^mo-records-director-inspection-appointment]. On demand, each employer must give the director a sworn statement of time records on forms the director prescribes or approves [^mo-sworn-time-records-on-demand]. Every employer subject to the law must keep a director-approved summary of the law, along with copies or a summary of applicable wage regulations, posted in a conspicuous and accessible place where employees work [^mo-minimum-wage-posting-requirement].
Missouri's older wage payment statutes carry their own consequences. A violation of the semimonthly payday and deduction-statement section is a misdemeanor punishable by a fine of fifty to five hundred dollars per offense [^mo-payday-violation-criminal-fine]. A manufactory operator that fails and refuses to pay as required owes the employee double the sum due, recoverable by civil action [^mo-manufactory-nonpayment-double-liability].
Under the public-works misclassification law, the attorney general is authorized to investigate suspected violations and to serve and enforce subpoenas [^mo-attorney-general-misclassification-investigation]. The attorney general may also seek an injunction in the circuit court of any county where a violation is occurring or about to occur [^mo-attorney-general-misclassification-injunction], and may enter into consent judgments with alleged violators [^mo-attorney-general-misclassification-consent-judgment].

"Any agreement between the employee and the employer to work for less than the wage rate shall be no defense to the action."[^mo-underpayment-agreement-no-defense]

# Wage and Hour Law in Louisiana[^about]

A plain-language guide to Louisiana wage-and-hour law covering the absence of a state minimum wage and overtime statute, the bar on local minimum wage ordinances, final-pay deadlines and penalty wages, payday rules and the payday notice, limits on fines and deductions, child-labor hours and meal intervals, the hour and rest rules for work in compressed air, the worker-classification definitions in the workers' compensation and employment security chapters, and how wage claims are enforced.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** No Louisiana statute sets a state minimum wage rate for private employers. No local governmental subdivision, such as a parish or municipality, may set a minimum wage rate that a private employer would be required to pay [^la-local-minimum-wage-preemption]. The same provision bars local governments from setting a mandatory minimum number of vacation or sick leave days, whether paid or unpaid [^la-local-minimum-wage-preemption]. The legislature declared that decisions on minimum wage and employee benefit policy must be made by the state [^la-minimum-wage-state-decision].

This guide covers Louisiana law only; the [wage and hour practice guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us) covers federal law, including the federal minimum wage, overtime and tip credit.

The legislature based this statewide approach on its conclusion that a business must work in a uniform environment with respect to minimum wage rates and minimum vacation or sick leave days [^la-minimum-wage-uniform-environment-finding]:

"The legislature concludes from these findings that, in order for a business to remain competitive and yet to attract and retain the highest possible caliber of employees, and thereby to remain sound, an enterprise must work in a uniform environment with respect to minimum wage rates and mandatory, minimum number of vacation or sick leave days."[^la-minimum-wage-uniform-environment-finding]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Louisiana's labor title (Title 23 of the Revised Statutes) sets no overtime pay rule, daily or weekly, for private employers. Its hour rules instead limit how long young workers may work, other than minors employed in agriculture or in domestic service in private homes: no minor under the age of sixteen may work more than eight hours in any one day, or more than six consecutive days in any one week [^la-minor-daily-hours-limit] [^la-minors-part-agriculture-domestic-exclusion]. A minor under sixteen who has not graduated from high school may not work more than forty hours in any one week [^la-minor-weekly-forty-hour-limit].

Tighter limits apply while school is in session. Minors under sixteen may not work more than three hours on a school day, or more than eighteen hours in a school week [^la-minor-school-day-hours-limit]. Whether a given day or week counts as a school day or school week depends on the calendar of the school the minor attends, or the public school calendar for the district where the minor lives [^la-minor-school-calendar-rule].

Louisiana also limits night work for minors who have not graduated from high school. Those under sixteen may not work between 7:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m., except that from June first through Labor Day the permitted hours extend to 9:00 p.m. [^la-minor-under-sixteen-night-work]. Sixteen-year-olds may not work between 11:00 p.m. and 5:00 a.m. before the start of any school day [^la-minor-sixteen-night-work], and seventeen-year-olds may not work between 12:00 a.m. and 5:00 a.m. before the start of any school day [^la-minor-seventeen-night-work]. Minors employed in the dairy industry are exempt from these night-work and forty-hour rules [^la-minor-dairy-industry-exemption].

For these night-work and forty-hour rules, a minor who has taken and passed the General Education Development test (GED) and been awarded a high school Equivalency Diploma from the Louisiana Department of Education is treated as having graduated from high school [^la-minor-ged-counts-as-graduated].

A separate hours cap applies to work in compressed air: the maximum total hours in twenty-four that one may work fall as the pressure rises, from eight hours at normal pressure up to eighteen pounds per square inch down to one hour at forty-eight to fifty pounds [^la-compressed-air-maximum-daily-hours].

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Louisiana requires a meal interval for minors under sixteen, other than minors employed in agriculture or in domestic service in private homes, who may not work any five-hour period without at least thirty minutes for meals, subject to two statutory de minimis tolerances [^la-minor-meal-interval-five-hours] [^la-minors-meal-agriculture-domestic-exclusion]. A work period before the interval that runs past five hours by ten minutes or less, and a meal break that lasts at least twenty minutes, are each treated as de minimis (too small to count) and are not violations [^la-minor-meal-start-de-minimis] [^la-minor-meal-length-de-minimis]. That interval is thirty minutes and is not counted as part of the working hours of the day [^la-minor-meal-interval-not-working-hours]; the cited section does not otherwise state whether the interval is paid.
Louisiana's labor title (Title 23) sets no general meal or rest break requirement for adult employees; a separate break rule found there covers people who work in compressed air, whose working time in any twenty-four hours is divided into two shifts with an interval in open air [^la-compressed-air-two-shifts-open-air]. A person who has not previously worked in compressed air may work only one shift during the first twenty-four hours [^la-compressed-air-first-day-one-shift]. The minimum rest interval in open air between those shifts depends on the pressure, from one hour at the lowest pressures listed to six hours at forty-eight to fifty pounds per square inch [^la-compressed-air-open-air-rest-interval]. A person who violates the compressed-air provisions may be fined not more than five thousand dollars, imprisoned not more than six months, or both [^la-compressed-air-violation-penalty].
Louisiana's pregnancy-accommodation law applies only to an employer that employs more than twenty-five employees in the state for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year [^la-pregnancy-accommodation-employer-coverage]. That law makes it unlawful for a covered employer to fail or refuse to make reasonable accommodations for an applicant or employee with covered limitations, unless the employer can demonstrate that the accommodation would impose an undue hardship on the operation of its business [^la-pregnancy-reasonable-accommodation-duty]. The employer need not create an additional employment opportunity or a new position, including a light duty position, or discharge an employee, transfer an employee with more seniority, or promote an employee who is not qualified, unless it does so for other employees who need a reasonable accommodation [^la-pregnancy-reasonable-accommodation-duty]. Covered limitations are medical needs causing limitations arising from pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions, where the employer knows of those limitations [^la-pregnancy-covered-limitations-definition]. The reasonable accommodations listed include scheduled and more frequent or longer compensated break periods, more frequent bathroom breaks, and a private place, other than a bathroom stall, for expressing breast milk [^la-pregnancy-accommodation-break-periods].

For minors, the break must be documented in the employer's normal timekeeping system [^la-minor-meal-break-timekeeping]. When a minor fails to clock in or out for a work period or meal break and a time edit is needed, the edit must be documented and acknowledged in writing by both the minor and the manager who performs it [^la-minor-time-edit-written-acknowledgment].

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** Unless a collective bargaining agreement provides otherwise, a Louisiana employer must pay a discharged employee the amount then due on or before the next regular payday or no later than fifteen days after the discharge, whichever occurs first [^la-final-pay-discharge-deadline] [^la-final-pay-collective-bargaining-exception]. When an employee resigns, the amount then due must be paid on or before the next regular payday for the pay cycle during which the employee was working at the time of separation, or no later than fifteen days following the resignation, whichever occurs first [^la-final-pay-resignation-deadline] [^la-final-pay-collective-bargaining-exception].
An employer that fails or refuses to comply with the final-pay section is liable to the employee, subject to a good-faith rule, for penalty wages: either ninety days wages at the employee's daily rate of pay, or full wages from the employee's demand for payment until the employer pays or tenders the unpaid wages, whichever is less [^la-penalty-wages-ninety-days]. That penalty applies when the court determines that the employer's failure or refusal to pay was not in good faith [^la-penalty-wages-not-good-faith]. If the court instead finds that the employer's dispute over the amount was in good faith, but the employer still owes the disputed amount, the employer is liable only for the disputed wages plus judicial interest from the date the suit was filed [^la-penalty-wages-good-faith-dispute]. In a dispute over the amount due, the employer must still pay the undisputed portion by the final-pay deadline [^la-final-pay-undisputed-portion]. The court must also award reasonable attorney fees, charged to the employer as costs, when an employee files a well-founded suit for unpaid wages more than three days after making a first demand following discharge or resignation [^la-final-pay-attorney-fees].

Final pay is made at the place and in the manner customary during the employment. It may also be sent by United States mail with postage prepaid, addressed to the employee's current address in the employer's records [^la-final-pay-method-mail]. When final pay is mailed, the employer is deemed to have paid when it is mailed [^la-final-pay-mail-deemed-paid], and the timeliness of the mailing may be shown by an official United States postmark or other official documentation from the United States Postal Service [^la-final-pay-mailing-postmark-proof]. For interstate common carriers by rail, a legal holiday is not counted in computing the fifteen-day period [^la-final-pay-rail-carrier-holidays].

Some forms of pay have their own rules. Vacation pay counts as an amount then due only if, under the employer's stated vacation policy, the employee is eligible for and has accrued the right to take paid vacation time, and has not taken or been paid for that time as of the discharge or resignation [^la-final-pay-vacation-amount-due]. Those vacation-pay provisions may not be read to allow forfeiture of any vacation pay an employee actually earned under the employer's policy [^la-final-pay-vacation-no-forfeiture].

A commission, incentive pay or bonus counts as an amount then due only if, at the time of separation, it has been earned and not modified under a written policy addressing that pay [^la-final-pay-commission-bonus-due]. Two kinds of policy are expressly lawful: one adjusting a commission for changes to the order that generated it, and one providing that a payment is not earned until the employer has received the payment that generates the commission, incentive pay or bonus [^la-final-pay-lawful-commission-policies]. For a bonus that depends on annual, quarterly or other periodic financial results, the employer has a reasonable time, not more than one hundred twenty calendar days after the end of the period, to determine whether a bonus is due and how much it is [^la-final-pay-periodic-bonus-timing].

No employer may require employees to sign contracts under which they forfeit their wages if they are discharged or resign before the contract is completed. In every such case the employees are entitled to the wages actually earned up to the time of separation [^la-no-wage-forfeiture-contracts]. Neither the final-pay section nor that bar on forfeiture contracts applies to a profits interest granted or issued by an entity taxed as a partnership for federal income tax purposes [^la-final-pay-profits-interest-exclusion].

One narrow exception covers medical examinations and drug tests. An employer may require an employee to sign a contract allowing the cost of a preemployment medical examination or drug test to be withheld from wages if the employee resigns within ninety working days of the first day of work. This applies only when the employee is paid at least one dollar above the existing federal minimum wage and is not a part-time or seasonal employee. The withholding is also not allowed if the resignation is attributable to a substantial change the employer made to the employment [^la-medical-exam-cost-withholding-contract].

## How often must workers be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** A Louisiana employer that fails to designate paydays must pay its employees on the first and sixteenth days of the month or as near as practicable, subject to the section's employee exclusions and its separate industry rule [^la-default-paydays-first-sixteenth]. The section does not cover employees in a bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory or professional capacity or employees exempt under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act [^la-payday-rule-exempt-employees], it does not apply to the clerical force or to salesmen except at public service corporations, and listed manufacturing, oil-boring, mining and public service employers are subject to a separate twice-monthly rule [^la-twice-monthly-listed-industries].
The twice-monthly rule reaches only listed industries. It covers every person engaged in manufacturing of any kind, or in boring for oil and in mining operations, employing ten or more employees, and every public service corporation. These employers must pay in full at least twice each calendar month, with paydays two weeks apart as near as practicable. Payment must cover all amounts due for the payroll period and is payable no later than the payday at the end of the next payroll period. Except for public service corporations, the rule does not apply to the clerical force or to salesmen [^la-twice-monthly-listed-industries].
No Louisiana wage-payment statute prescribes pay-stub contents; the duty R.S. 23:633(A) does impose is to tell employees at hire their wages, the method of payment and how often they will be paid, along with any later changes [^la-pay-terms-disclosure-at-hire]. Employers must also post, in the same location as other employee notices required by state or federal laws, a notice provided by Louisiana Works that restates this duty and explains how an employee may complain about a failure to pay as agreed [^la-payday-notice-posting-duty].

Louisiana also limits what may come out of a paycheck. An employer may not assess fines against employees or deduct fines from their wages [^la-no-fines-from-wages]. That bar does not apply when an employee wilfully or negligently damages goods, works or the employer's property, or has been convicted of or pled guilty to theft of employer funds. Even then, the fine may not exceed the actual damage done [^la-fines-damage-theft-exception]. Separately, an employer may not collect any amount from employees, by wage deduction or otherwise, to pay or reimburse premiums on liability or compensation insurance [^la-no-insurance-premium-deductions]. The premium bar does not prevent an employer from carrying its own insurance for its own employees, and it does not apply to an employer qualified under Louisiana law to engage in the liability insurance business [^la-premium-bar-employer-own-insurance]. Nor does it prevent an independent contractor who is a sole proprietor, and who has elected by written agreement not to be covered by the workers' compensation chapter, from entering into a contract with the principal under which the contractor is responsible for securing insurance or self-insurance for that chapter's benefits [^la-premium-bar-sole-proprietor-contractor].

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Louisiana's final-pay statute does not define who is an employee or an independent contractor. In *Knight v. Tucker*, a wage claim under R.S. 23:631, the Louisiana Second Circuit Court of Appeal said that Louisiana law provides no definition of employee in the pertinent section, which speaks only of any laborer or other employee of any kind whatever [^la-knight-no-statutory-employee-definition]. Applying the principal-and-independent-contractor factors, the court concluded that the plaintiff was an employee of the firm and entitled to the benefits due to persons so classified under Louisiana law [^la-knight-hickman-contractor-factors] [^la-knight-employee-conclusion]. Citing *Hickman v. Southern Pacific Transport Co.* [^la-hickman-independent-contractor-elements], the court in *Knight v. Tucker* treated five factors as relevant to that question: a valid contract between the parties; work of an independent nature, so that the contractor may employ nonexclusive means in accomplishing it; a contract calling for specific piecework as a unit, done by the contractor's own methods without the principal's control and direction except as to the result; a specific price agreed for the overall undertaking; and a specific duration not subject to termination or discontinuance at the will of either side without a corresponding liability for breach [^la-knight-hickman-contractor-factors]. In *Hickman*, the Louisiana Supreme Court called the control over the work reserved by the employer the principal test, and explained that what matters is not the supervision and control actually exercised but whether, from the nature of the relationship, the right to control exists [^la-hickman-right-of-control-test].
For purposes of its workers' compensation chapter only, Louisiana defines an independent contractor as a person who renders service, other than manual labor, for a specified recompense for a specified result, under the principal's control as to results only and not as to the means [^la-workers-comp-independent-contractor-definition]. Under that same definition, a contractor who spends a substantial part of work time in manual labor carrying out the contract is expressly covered by the workers' compensation chapter [^la-workers-comp-independent-contractor-definition].
For purposes of its employment security (unemployment insurance) chapter only, Louisiana treats services performed for wages or under any contract of hire as employment unless and until it is shown to the administrator's satisfaction that three conditions are met [^la-unemployment-employment-three-part-test]. First, the individual has been and will continue to be free from control or direction over the services, both under the contract and in fact [^la-unemployment-employment-three-part-test]. Second, the service is outside the usual course of the business, or is performed outside all of its places of business [^la-unemployment-employment-three-part-test]. Third, the individual is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, profession or business [^la-unemployment-employment-three-part-test]. Notwithstanding any contrary provision of that chapter, R.S. 23:1711.1 creates a rebuttable presumption of an independent contractor relationship when the individual or entity controls the performance, methods or processes used to perform services and meets at least six of the listed criteria, which include operating an independent business that provides services for or in connection with the contracting party and representing the services as self-employment available to others [^la-unemployment-contractor-presumption-six-criteria]. Any contracting party or independent contractor may rely on that section to establish an employment or independent contractor relationship [^la-unemployment-contractor-presumption-reliance]. The presumption does not apply to listed services, including a motor carrier performing services as a motor carrier under a contract with an owner operator, and any service excluded from the term employment under R.S. 23:1472(12)(H) [^la-unemployment-contractor-presumption-exclusions].

The workers' compensation chapter also has trucking-specific rules. Operating a truck tractor or truck tractor trailer, including fueling, driving, connecting lines and hoses, hooking and unhooking trailers, and vehicle inspections, is not manual labor under that chapter [^la-workers-comp-truck-operation-not-manual-labor]. An owner operator, and the drivers it provides, are not employees of a common carrier or exempt hauler for that chapter's purposes if the owner operator has a written agreement with the carrier or hauler identifying itself as an independent contractor [^la-workers-comp-owner-operator-contractor]. For that chapter, an owner operator does not include an individual driver who purchases equipment from the carrier or hauler and then directly leases it back to the carrier or hauler [^la-workers-comp-owner-operator-leaseback-excluded].

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** No Louisiana statute sets a state minimum wage rate for private employers, so no Louisiana statute provides a tip credit against one. No parish or municipality may set a minimum wage rate that a private employer must pay [^la-tip-credit-local-wage-preemption].

## How is it enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A Louisiana employee owed wages under the final-pay section has the right to file an action to enforce the wage claim and proceed under Code of Civil Procedure Article 2592 [^la-wage-claim-private-action]. Besides any other proper court, employees may sue for wages due in the district court of the parish where the work was performed [^la-wage-suit-parish-venue]. An action to recover compensation for services rendered, including salaries, wages and commissions, is subject to a liberative prescription (a time bar) of three years [^la-wage-action-three-year-prescription].
The wage-payment chapter's remedies include penalty wages of ninety days wages at the employee's daily rate of pay or full wages from the employee's demand until the employer pays or tenders the unpaid wages due, whichever is less [^la-enforcement-penalty-wages-amount], which apply when a court determines that the employer's failure or refusal to pay was not in good faith [^la-enforcement-penalty-wages-not-good-faith]. They also include reasonable attorney fees, taxed as costs against the employer, for a well-founded wage suit filed more than three days after the first demand following discharge or resignation [^la-enforcement-attorney-fees-wage-suit].
No employer may require employees to sign contracts that forfeit their wages if they are discharged or resign before the contract is completed [^la-enforcement-no-wage-forfeiture]. A person who violates that bar, or the bar on fines against employees, may be fined not less than twenty-five dollars nor more than one hundred dollars, or imprisoned for not less than thirty days nor more than three months [^la-forfeiture-fines-criminal-penalty] [^la-enforcement-no-fines-from-wages].
A violation of the payday and disclosure rules may be fined not less than twenty-five dollars nor more than two hundred fifty dollars for each day's violation [^la-payday-violation-daily-fine], and a second violation may add imprisonment of not less than ten days [^la-payday-second-violation-imprisonment]. A person who violates the bar on collecting insurance premiums from employees may be fined not more than five hundred dollars, imprisoned with or without hard labor for not more than one year, or both [^la-premium-deduction-criminal-penalty] [^la-enforcement-premium-collection-bar]. That person is also assessed civil penalties by the workers' compensation judge of five hundred to five thousand dollars, payable to the employee, plus reasonable attorney fees [^la-premium-deduction-civil-penalty]. Restitution must be ordered up to the amount collected from the employee's wages, salary or other compensation [^la-premium-deduction-restitution-order].

The notice text set out in the payday section tells an employee whose employer fails, for reasons within its control, to pay as agreed to first lodge a complaint with the employer [^la-posted-notice-complain-to-employer-first]. Notice language set out in the payday section tells an employee that an unresolved violation may be reported to the office of workforce development within Louisiana Works [^la-posted-notice-report-to-louisiana-works].

Child-labor rules have a separate enforcement scheme, distinct from the wage-payment remedies above. The secretary is empowered to enforce the child-labor Part's civil provisions, adopt rules and conduct investigations [^la-child-labor-secretary-enforcement-power]. A person who violates the Part, where no other penalty is provided, may be fined one hundred to five hundred dollars, imprisoned for thirty days to six months, or both [^la-child-labor-criminal-penalty]. A violator is also liable for a civil penalty of up to five hundred dollars [^la-child-labor-civil-penalty]. Louisiana Works may impose those civil penalties only by a ruling of the secretary after an adjudicatory hearing under the Administrative Procedure Act [^la-child-labor-civil-penalty-hearing]. The secretary may bring civil proceedings in the Nineteenth Judicial District Court to enforce those rulings [^la-child-labor-enforce-rulings-court], and may also seek an injunction in that court to restrain violations [^la-child-labor-injunctive-relief].

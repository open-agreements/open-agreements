# Wage and Hour Law in Hawaii[^about]

How Hawaii sets a $16.00 minimum wage that rises to $18.00 in 2028, requires weekly overtime after 40 hours, sets no general adult break rule, requires final pay at discharge, and sets pay-frequency, pay-statement, deduction, worker-classification, tip-credit and wage-claim rules.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Hawaii's minimum wage is $16.00 an hour, effective January 1, 2026, and it rises to $18.00 an hour on January 1, 2028. Section 387-2 of the Hawaii Revised Statutes sets these steps on a fixed schedule rather than by index. [^hi-minimum-wage-schedule] The Wage Standards Division of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations lists the same rates. [^hi-dlir-minimum-wage-page] The state rate is above the federal minimum wage of $7.25 an hour. [^hi-federal-minimum-wage-rate]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The chapter's definition of employee leaves out several groups. One exclusion is for an individual paid a guaranteed compensation of $4,000 or more a month, however often it is paid. [^hi-guaranteed-compensation-exclusion] Bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees, outside salespeople and outside collectors are also excluded. [^hi-white-collar-exclusion]

An employee whose minimum wage or overtime is set by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act is also outside the chapter, but the Hawaii minimum wage still applies whenever it is higher than the federal rate, and the Hawaii 40-hour overtime week applies whenever the federal week is longer. [^hi-flsa-covered-employee-exclusion] Because the state rate is above the federal rate, an employee covered by the federal Act who does not fall within another chapter 387 exclusion is still owed the Hawaii minimum wage. [^hi-flsa-covered-employee-exclusion] [^hi-minimum-wage-schedule]

The chapter's definition of employer does not include the State, its political subdivisions or the United States. [^hi-employer-definition-excludes-state]

The Director of Labor and Industrial Relations may, by rule, allow learners, apprentices, some part-time students and paroled wards of the Hawaii youth correctional facility to be paid less than the minimum wage under special certificates. [^hi-special-minimum-wage-certificates]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Hawaii requires overtime at one and one-half times the regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek, and it has no general daily overtime rule. [^hi-overtime-forty-hours] A workweek is a fixed and regularly recurring period of seven consecutive days. [^hi-workweek-definition] When an employee does two or more different kinds of work for the same employer, the total earnings for the pay period are treated as earned for one kind of work. [^hi-overtime-multiple-kinds-of-work]

The statute sets how to turn a salary into a regular rate. For a weekly salary, the salary plus the reasonable cost of any board, lodging or other facilities furnished is divided by 40. [^hi-regular-rate-weekly-salary]

Some agricultural and seasonal processing employers need not pay overtime after 40 hours during 20 workweeks they select each year beginning July 1, but they must pay overtime after 48 hours in those weeks. [^hi-seasonal-overtime-forty-eight-hours]

Except in an extraordinary emergency, an employer may not schedule split shifts unless all shifts within 24 hours fall within 14 consecutive hours. [^hi-split-shift-fourteen-hours]

The same exclusions from the definition of employee apply to overtime, including the $4,000-a-month guaranteed compensation exclusion and the exclusion for bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees. [^hi-overtime-guaranteed-compensation-exclusion] [^hi-overtime-white-collar-exclusion]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Hawaii's statutes do not require employers to give adult employees general meal or rest breaks. The review for this guide of chapters 387, 388 and 390 of the Hawaii Revised Statutes found no such provision. Two narrower statutory break rules apply. An employer must give an employee reasonable break time to express breast milk for one year after the child's birth, and a private location other than a restroom. [^hi-lactation-break] Minors aged 14 and 15 may not work more than five hours continuously without an interval of at least 30 consecutive minutes for a rest or lunch period. [^hi-minor-rest-period]

When an employer chooses to give breaks, the Wage Standards Division treats a meal period of 30 minutes or more as unpaid if the employee is completely relieved of duty, and counts rest breaks of five to twenty minutes as compensable hours worked. [^hi-dlir-break-pay-guidance]

An employer with fewer than 20 employees is exempt from the lactation-break requirement if it shows the requirement would impose an undue hardship by causing significant difficulty or expense. [^hi-lactation-small-employer-hardship]

Minors aged 14 and 15 are also limited to work between 7:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m., with a later window during authorized school breaks, and to no more than six consecutive days. [^hi-minor-hours-window]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** When an employer discharges an employee, with or without cause, it must pay the employee's wages in full at the time of discharge, or no later than the next working day if the discharge happens at a time and under conditions that prevent immediate payment. [^hi-final-pay-discharge] An employee who quits must be paid in full by the next regular payday, unless the employee gave at least one pay period's notice, in which case all wages earned are due at the time of quitting. [^hi-final-pay-quit]

When work is suspended because of a labor dispute, or an employee is temporarily laid off, wages earned at the time of the suspension or layoff are due by the next regular payday. [^hi-final-pay-layoff]

When the amount is disputed, the employer must pay without condition all wages it concedes are due, within the time the chapter sets, and the employee keeps all remedies for the balance. A release required as a condition of that payment is void. [^hi-undisputed-wages-unconditional] [^hi-conceded-wages-no-release]

Late final pay carries the general wage-payment penalty. An employer that fails to pay wages under chapter 388 without equitable justification owes the employee, on top of the wages due, a sum equal to the unpaid wages plus interest at six percent a year, and owes a separate civil penalty. [^hi-final-pay-late-civil-liability]

If an employee dies, the employer must, within 30 days and on application, pay up to $2,000 of the wages, vacation or sick leave pay due to the surviving spouse or reciprocal beneficiary, or if none, to an adult child. [^hi-deceased-employee-wages]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Subject to statutory exceptions, Hawaii requires an employer to pay all wages due at least twice each calendar month, on regular paydays designated in advance. [^hi-semimonthly-payday] The exceptions are a monthly schedule that employees elect by secret ballot and a less frequent schedule or later payment that the director approves on application. [^hi-monthly-pay-election] [^hi-director-pay-frequency-variance] Earned wages are due within seven days after the end of each pay period. [^hi-wages-due-seven-days]

Employees may instead be paid monthly if a majority of the employer's employees, or of a recognized bargaining unit, vote by secret ballot under procedures the director approves; elections may be held no more than once every two years and each is valid for two years. [^hi-monthly-pay-election] On an application showing good and sufficient reasons, the director may also let an employer pay less often than twice a month, as long as employees are paid in full at least monthly, or pay earned wages within 15 days after the end of each pay period. [^hi-director-pay-frequency-variance]

Direct deposit is allowed only if the employee has voluntarily authorized it in writing or by electronic signature, among other conditions. [^hi-direct-deposit-authorization]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** Hawaii requires an itemized pay statement every pay period. Section 387-6 lists 14 items, including the employer's name, address and telephone number, total hours worked, regular and overtime hours, straight-time and overtime compensation, gross pay, the amount and purpose of each deduction, net pay, the pay date, the pay period and the rates of pay. [^hi-pay-statement-items] Chapter 388 separately requires a pay statement on every payday and requires the employer to keep a copy for at least six years. [^hi-pay-statement-six-years] An electronic statement is allowed with the employee's written authorization. [^hi-pay-statement-items]
An employer may deduct from wages only when federal or state law or court process requires it, or when the employee authorizes the deduction in writing. Even with authorization, an employer may not make the employee bear fines, shared-till cash shortages, shortages in a till under the employee's sole control when the employee had no opportunity to account for the money at the start and end of the shift, fines, penalties or replacement costs for breakage, or losses from dishonored checks the employee had discretion to accept or reject. Nor may it make the employee bear losses from faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property, property damage or customer nonpayment, unless the loss is attributable to the employee's willful or intentional disregard of the employer's interest. [^hi-deductions-written-authorization]

At hiring, the employer must tell each employee in writing the rate of pay and the day, hour and place of payment. [^hi-hiring-pay-notice] The employer must also keep contemporaneous records of each employee's hours worked each day and each workweek and of the amounts paid. [^hi-records-hours-worked]

For the deductions rule only, wages include tips and gratuities of any kind. [^hi-wages-include-tips-for-deductions]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Hawaii's wage laws define employment by a broad suffer-or-permit standard. [^hi-employ-suffer-permit-387] [^hi-employee-suffer-permit-388] Chapter 387 says that to employ includes to permit or suffer to work. [^hi-employ-suffer-permit-387] Chapter 388, the wage payment law, uses the same language and defines an employee as any person suffered or permitted to work. [^hi-employee-suffer-permit-388] The review for this guide found no separate statutory test in either chapter for telling an employee from an independent contractor, and no Hawaii appellate decision fixing one for these chapters, so this guide does not assign Hawaii a classification test.

For private construction work not subject to the public works law, a general contractor is liable for unpaid wages owed by a subcontractor at any tier for labor included in the general contractor's contract with the owner. That liability covers unpaid wages and interest only, not penalties or liquidated damages. [^hi-general-contractor-wage-liability]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Hawaii allows a tip credit only if the tipped employee's combined wages and tips are at least $7.00 more than the applicable minimum wage. [^hi-tip-credit] When that condition is met, the employer may pay the employee up to $1.25 an hour below the minimum wage. [^hi-tip-credit] At the $16.00 minimum wage, the cash wage may not fall below $14.75 an hour, and the employee must receive at least $23.00 an hour in wages and tips combined. [^hi-tip-minimum-wage-base] [^hi-tip-credit] The maximum credit rises to $1.50 an hour on January 1, 2028. [^hi-tip-credit] A tipped employee is one in an occupation in which the employee customarily and regularly receives more than $20 a month in tips. [^hi-tipped-employee-definition]

A hotel or restaurant that adds a service charge to food or beverage sales, or a hotel that adds one for porterage, must either distribute it directly to employees as tip income or clearly disclose to the customer that it is used for costs other than employees' wages and tips. [^hi-service-charge-disclosure]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** The Director of Labor and Industrial Relations enforces both the minimum wage and overtime chapter and the wage payment chapter, and employees may also sue. [^hi-director-enforces-387] [^hi-director-enforces-388] [^hi-minimum-wage-collection-suit] [^hi-wage-payment-private-action] An employer that fails to pay minimum wage or overtime without equitable justification owes the employee, on top of the wages due, an equal sum plus interest at six percent a year, and owes a civil penalty of at least $500, or $100 for each violation if greater. [^hi-minimum-wage-civil-liability] The wage payment chapter carries the same liability for unpaid wages, including late final pay. [^hi-wage-payment-civil-penalty]
One or more employees may sue on behalf of themselves and similarly situated employees to recover unpaid minimum wages or overtime, and a prevailing plaintiff recovers reasonable attorney's fees and costs. [^hi-minimum-wage-collection-suit] Under the wage payment chapter, a court must award interest, costs and reasonable attorney's fees in addition to any judgment for the plaintiff. [^hi-wage-payment-suit-fees]

The director may take an assignment of an employee's wage claim and sue to collect it, but the director may not accept a claim more than one year after the wages were due. [^hi-director-assignment-one-year]

Provisions of the wage payment chapter cannot be waived by private agreement, except as section 388-11 provides. [^hi-no-waiver-by-agreement]

Nonpayment can also be a crime. An employer that does not pay wages as chapter 388 requires, or a corporate officer who knowingly permits that failure, commits a class C felony. [^hi-wage-payment-felony] Under the wage payment chapter, discharging or discriminating against an employee for complaining about unpaid wages, starting a proceeding or testifying is punishable by a fine of $100 to $10,000, imprisonment for up to one year, or both. [^hi-wage-payment-retaliation]

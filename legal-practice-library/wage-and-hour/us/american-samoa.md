# Wage and Hour Law in American Samoa[^about]

How federal law sets industry-by-industry minimum wages in American Samoa that stay below $7.25 and rise by up to $0.40 every three years, and how the territory's Wages and Hours chapter adds weekly overtime, a 14-hour daily cap, immediate final pay on discharge and deduction limits for the workers it covers.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Federal law sets industry- and classification-specific minimum wages in American Samoa. In 2007 Congress made the federal minimum wage section of the Fair Labor Standards Act apply to American Samoa, [^as-federal-minimum-wage-applies] while keeping each industry's rate in effect on September 29, 2015 and raising it by $0.40 an hour on September 30, 2015 and on September 30 of every third year after that, until it equals the federal minimum wage. [^as-federal-schedule] The federal rate itself is $7.25 an hour. [^as-federal-minimum-wage-rate] The U.S. Department of Labor lists rates effective September 30, 2024 that run from $5.78 an hour in garment manufacturing to $7.19 in stevedoring, lighterage and maritime shipping agency work, with $5.80 for any activity not in a listed industry. [^as-dol-garment] [^as-dol-shipping-transport] [^as-dol-miscellaneous]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The Department of Labor lists the following rates, effective September 30, 2024:

The next scheduled increase falls on September 30, 2027, because the federal schedule raises each rate every third September 30 after 2015. Each rate rises by $0.40 an hour or, if less, by the amount needed to reach $7.25, so the finance and insurance rate and the Classification A and B shipping rates will rise by less than $0.40. [^as-federal-schedule] The Department of Labor's industry definitions decide which rate applies; the miscellaneous rate covers every activity not included in another listed industry. [^as-dol-miscellaneous]

The territory's own Wages and Hours chapter has not been updated to the federal rates. Its minimum wage section still requires only 70 cents an hour, and by its terms applies only to employees not covered by the Merit System Law and the federal minimum wage schedule. [^as-territorial-minimum-wage] The chapter's definition of an employee also leaves out anyone whose minimum wage or maximum hours without overtime the federal Fair Labor Standards Act prescribes, along with farm workers of employers with fewer than five people, domestic workers in private homes, listed close relatives of the employer, students employed by their nonprofit school and newspaper carriers. [^as-territorial-employee-definition] In *Moea'i v. Reid*, the High Court of American Samoa found it doubtful that the territorial chapter's liquidated damages for willful failure to pay the minimum wage are available where the federal minimum wage schedule applies. [^as-moeai-federal-schedule]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** American Samoa's Wages and Hours chapter contains no daily overtime premium requirement. The federal Fair Labor Standards Act requires covered employers to pay overtime of at least one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek, [^as-federal-overtime] and the territory's Wages and Hours chapter sets the same 40-hour weekly rule for the employees it covers. [^as-territorial-overtime] The chapter separately bars employing an employee for more than 14 hours in any 24-hour period, except in an extraordinary emergency. [^as-fourteen-hour-cap]

In *Moea'i v. Reid*, the High Court treated the territorial overtime rate as one and one-half times the employee's ordinary rate whether or not a contract set that rate, but held that liquidated damages under the chapter require a willful failure to pay. [^as-moeai-overtime]

Federal overtime exemptions and hours-worked rules are discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** American Samoa's Wages and Hours chapter, A.S.C.A. §§ 32.0301 to 32.0351, contains no meal-period or rest-break requirement. This guide did not review the American Samoa Administrative Code for one. Under the federal rules, short rest periods of about 5 to 20 minutes that an employer chooses to give must be counted and paid as hours worked, [^as-federal-rest-periods] while a bona fide meal period during which the employee is completely relieved from duty is not worktime. [^as-federal-meal-periods]

Federal law separately requires reasonable break time and a private place, other than a bathroom, for an employee to express breast milk for one year after a child's birth. [^as-federal-nursing-breaks]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** For employees covered by American Samoa's Wages and Hours chapter, earned wages are due immediately when the employer discharges the employee, with or without cause, and on the next regular payday when the employee quits. [^as-final-pay-timing] If the amount is disputed, the employer must tell the employee how much it concedes is due and pay that amount without conditions by the same deadline, and the employee's acceptance does not release the disputed balance. [^as-disputed-final-pay] The chapter sets no separate damages for late final pay, but a person able to pay who knowingly refuses to pay wages due when demanded, or who violates the final-pay or deduction sections, commits a class B misdemeanor. [^as-wage-payment-misdemeanor]

The chapter's definitions apply throughout the chapter, and its definition of an employee leaves out anyone whose minimum wage or overtime the federal Fair Labor Standards Act prescribes, so the territorial final-pay rule does not by its terms reach those workers. This guide found no High Court decision applying the final-pay sections. [^as-fp-employee-definition] Under federal rules, overtime earned in a workweek must generally be paid on the regular payday for the period in which that workweek ends. [^as-federal-overtime-payday]

When a business is suspended by a writ of execution or attachment or placed with a receiver, trustee or assignee for creditors, wage claims of up to $300 per claimant earned in the previous six months must be paid ahead of taxes and other debts, apart from a mortgage recorded before the wages were earned. [^as-wage-priority]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** American Samoa's Wages and Hours chapter sets no pay-frequency rule. The chapter does require employers to record the amount paid to each employee each pay period. [^as-employer-records] This guide did not find a pay-frequency rule elsewhere in the American Samoa Code Annotated or the Administrative Code, but it did not review either one in full. Under federal rules, overtime must generally be paid on the regular payday for the period in which the workweek ends. [^as-pf-federal-overtime-payday]

Federal law treats wages as paid only when they are paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear. [^as-federal-free-and-clear]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** The territory's Wages and Hours chapter requires records rather than a pay statement given to the employee: each employer must keep each employee's name, address and occupation, the amount paid each pay period and the hours worked each day and workweek. [^as-ws-employer-records] No fine may be collected, deducted or kept out of an employee's earned compensation. [^as-no-fines-deducted] Any other deduction is unlawful unless a federal or territorial statute or court process requires it or the employee authorizes it in writing, and fines may not be authorized that way. [^as-wage-deductions-limit]

When an inspection finds an illegal fine or deduction, the Wage and Hour Board may secure restitution, and restitution bars prosecution under A.S.C.A. § 32.0349. [^as-deduction-restitution-board]

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** American Samoa's Wages and Hours chapter specifies no employee-versus-contractor classification test. It provides that to employ includes to permit or suffer to work. [^as-employ-definition] The federal Fair Labor Standards Act uses the same suffer-or-permit wording, [^as-federal-employ] and federal regulations decide employee status by looking at the economic realities of the relationship, asking whether the worker is economically dependent on the potential employer for work or in business for themself. [^as-federal-economic-realities]

The chapter's definition of an employer reaches individuals, businesses and the territorial government, but not the United States government. [^as-employer-definition]

## Can tips count toward the minimum wage? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** The territory's Wages and Hours chapter defines a wage to exclude tips and gratuities of any kind, so tips do not count toward the territorial minimum wage for the employees that chapter covers. [^as-tips-not-wages] For employees covered by the federal Act, a qualifying employer may count tips toward the minimum wage up to the difference between the section 6(a)(1) minimum wage and a cash wage of at least $2.13 an hour, [^as-federal-tip-credit-formula] but only if it first informs the employee of the rules and lets the employee keep all tips, apart from a pool among customarily tipped employees. [^as-federal-tip-credit-conditions] This guide found no Department of Labor statement on how that formula applies to the American Samoa industry rates.

A tipped employee is one who customarily and regularly receives more than $30 a month in tips. [^as-federal-tipped-employee] An employer may not keep employees' tips for any purpose, including letting managers or supervisors share them, whether or not it takes a tip credit. [^as-federal-tips-kept]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Under the territory's Wages and Hours chapter, an employer that violates the minimum wage or overtime section owes the affected employees their unpaid minimum wages or overtime, plus an additional equal amount as liquidated damages for a willful violation. [^as-employer-liability] One or more employees may bring that claim before the Office of the Administrative Law Judge for themselves and others similarly situated, and a prevailing employee recovers a reasonable attorney's fee and costs. [^as-alj-actions] Under the federal Act, an employer owes unpaid minimum wages or overtime plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, [^as-federal-liability] which a court may reduce or deny only if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds for believing it was complying. [^as-federal-good-faith]

A federal claim must be brought within two years, or three years for a willful violation. [^as-federal-limitations] This guide did not identify the limitations period for a claim under the territorial chapter, which sets none of its own.

The Wage and Hour Board may also take an assignment of an underpaid employee's claim in trust and sue to collect it, with the employer paying costs and attorney's fees if the Board prevails. [^as-alj-actions] The Board may seek an injunction before the Administrative Law Judge against an employer engaged in a practice the chapter prohibits. [^as-board-injunction-action] An employer that discharges or discriminates against an employee for complaining, starting a proceeding or testifying under the chapter commits a class B misdemeanor, with each day a separate offense. [^as-retaliation-misdemeanor]

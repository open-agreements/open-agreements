# Wage and Hour Law in Kentucky[^about]

How Kentucky sets a $7.25 minimum wage tied to the federal rate, requires weekly overtime after 40 hours and time and a half on a seventh workday, requires a lunch period and paid rest breaks, and sets final-pay, pay-frequency, deduction-statement, worker-classification, tip-credit and wage-claim rules.

## What is the minimum wage? {#minimum-wage}

**Short answer.** Kentucky's minimum wage is $7.25 an hour. KRS 337.275 requires every employer to pay each employee at least $7.25 an hour beginning July 1, 2009, and if the federal minimum wage under 29 U.S.C. 206(a)(1) rises above the state rate, the state rate rises to the same amount on the same date. [^ky-minimum-wage-federal-ratchet] An automatic increase brings in only the federal hourly rate, not the federal law's other rates, conditions, exclusions or exceptions. [^ky-minimum-wage-federal-ratchet] An employer covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act must also pay the federal minimum wage, which is currently the same $7.25. [^ky-federal-minimum-wage-rate]
Federal minimum wage law is discussed in the [federal wage and hour guide](/practice-guides/wage-and-hour/us).

The minimum wage, overtime, recordkeeping and posting sections, and the employee remedies in KRS 337.385 to 337.405, use a narrower definition of employee than the rest of the chapter. That definition excludes, among others, individuals employed in agriculture, bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees, outside salespeople and outside collectors, federal employees, and domestic workers in a private home unless more than one domestic servant is regularly employed there. [^ky-minimum-wage-employee-exclusions] It also excludes employees of retail stores, service industries, hotels, motels and restaurants whose average annual gross sales volume for the five preceding years is less than $95,000, and members of the employer's immediate family. [^ky-small-business-family-exclusion] Babysitters in the employer's home, companions to a sick, convalescing or elderly person whose principal duties do not include housekeeping, and newspaper carriers delivering to consumers are excluded as well. [^ky-babysitter-companion-newspaper-exclusion]

The commissioner may certify a learner, apprentice, worker with a disability, sheltered workshop employee or student for employment below the minimum wage, at the rate and for the period stated in the certificate. [^ky-certificate-subminimum-wage]

For the chapter's other sections, such as the pay-frequency, final-pay, deduction and break statutes, an employee is any person employed by or suffered or permitted to work for an employer. A franchisee and its employees are not employees of the franchisor under the chapter, and a franchisor and its employees are not employees of the franchisee. [^ky-employee-definition-franchise]

For employees within the narrower definition described above, every employer must keep a record of the amount paid each pay period and the hours worked each day and each week, and must keep those records for at least one year. [^ky-records-amount-paid-hours] [^ky-records-one-year] [^ky-minimum-wage-employee-exclusions] Employers covered by the minimum wage provisions must post the state's summaries of the wage and hour laws in a conspicuous and accessible place. [^ky-posting-wage-hour-summaries]

## When is overtime owed? {#overtime}

**Short answer.** Kentucky requires overtime pay for covered, nonexempt employees at one and one-half times their hourly wage rate for hours worked over 40 in a work week. [^ky-overtime-forty-hours-weekly] The overtime statute measures overtime by the work week, not by the day. [^ky-overtime-forty-hours-weekly] Kentucky also has a separate seventh-day rule: an employer that permits an employee to work seven days in one workweek must pay time and a half for the time worked on the seventh day. [^ky-seventh-day-time-and-a-half] The seventh-day rule does not apply when the employee is not permitted to work more than 40 hours that workweek, and the employer may credit overtime already paid for the same hours under another law or contract. [^ky-seventh-day-forty-hour-limit]

The weekly overtime rule in KRS 337.285 does not apply to employees of retail stores engaged in work connected with selling, purchasing and distributing merchandise, or to employees of restaurant, hotel and motel operations. It also does not apply to employees exempt from federal overtime under listed subsections of 29 U.S.C. 213(b). [^ky-overtime-retail-restaurant-exemptions] Third-party employees providing in-home companionship services to a sick, convalescing or elderly person are also exempt from the weekly rule. [^ky-overtime-companionship-exemption] The exclusions in the minimum wage definition of employee, such as the exclusion for bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees, also apply to overtime. [^ky-white-collar-exclusions-overtime]

A Kentucky regulation defines the bona fide executive exemption to require, among other things, a salary of at least $455 a week. [^ky-executive-exemption-455-salary] Kentucky's exemptions do not displace federal law. An employer covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act must pay covered employees overtime at one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek unless a federal exemption applies. [^ky-federal-overtime-forty-hours] A federal executive, administrative or professional exemption generally requires a salary of at least $684 a week. [^ky-federal-white-collar-salary-684] An employee who meets Kentucky's $455 salary test, or who works in a restaurant or retail job exempt under KRS 337.285, may therefore still be owed federal overtime.

The seventh-day rule has its own exceptions. It does not cover telephone exchanges with fewer than 500 subscribers, stenographers, bookkeepers or technical assistants of licensed professions, employees subject to the federal Railway Labor Act, seamen and boat operators on navigable streams, people icing railroad cars, or common carriers supervised by the Department of Vehicle Regulation. [^ky-seventh-day-exceptions] It also does not cover an officer, superintendent, foreman or supervisor whose duties are principally limited to directing or supervising other employees. [^ky-seventh-day-supervisor-exclusion] KRS 337.050 remains in force; it was last amended in 1974. [^ky-seventh-day-supervisor-exclusion]

A county or city employee, a Trooper R Class or a CVE R Class may, on a written request made freely and without coercion and a written agreement reached before the work, receive compensatory leave instead of pay for extra hours. [^ky-public-comp-leave-written-request] For an employee not exempt from the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, that compensatory time must be at least one and one-half hours for each hour worked over 40 in a work week. [^ky-public-compensatory-time] A county or city employee in public safety, emergency response or seasonal work may accrue up to 480 hours, and other covered employees up to 240 hours, before further overtime must be paid in cash. [^ky-compensatory-time-caps]

Under the commissioner's regulation on hours worked, short rest periods of about five to 20 minutes count as hours worked, while a bona fide meal period during which the employee is completely relieved from duty is not work time. [^ky-rest-periods-hours-worked] [^ky-bona-fide-meal-not-worktime]

## Are breaks required? {#meal-rest-breaks}

**Short answer.** Kentucky requires both a lunch period and paid rest periods, subject to exceptions for Railway Labor Act employers and for certain agreements. [^ky-lunch-period-third-to-fifth-hour] [^ky-rest-period-ten-minutes-paid] Neither rule applies to employers subject to the federal Railway Labor Act; the lunch rule does not override a collective bargaining agreement or a mutual agreement between the employee and employer; and the rest rule does not apply where a collective bargaining agreement provides at least 10 minutes for each four hours of work. [^ky-lunch-period-third-to-fifth-hour] [^ky-lunch-period-agreements] [^ky-rest-period-ten-minutes-paid] An employer must grant a reasonable lunch period as close to the middle of the scheduled shift as possible, and may not require an employee to take it sooner than three hours or later than five hours after the shift starts. [^ky-lunch-period-third-to-fifth-hour] An employer also may not require an employee to work without a rest period of at least 10 minutes during each four hours worked, in addition to the lunch period, and may not reduce pay for the rest period. [^ky-rest-period-ten-minutes-paid]

The lunch statute does not say the lunch period must be paid. Under the commissioner's hours-worked regulation, a bona fide meal period during which the employee is completely relieved from duty is not work time, and an employee who must perform any duties while eating is not relieved. [^ky-bona-fide-meal-unpaid-breaks] [^ky-meal-not-relieved-duties]

An employer that violates the rest-period statute faces a civil penalty of $100 to $1,000. [^ky-rest-period-civil-penalty]

## When is final pay due? {#final-pay}

**Short answer.** In Kentucky, an employee who quits or is discharged must be paid all wages earned by the later of the next normal pay period after separation or 14 days after separation. [^ky-final-pay-later-of-two-dates] An employer may not secure an exemption from this rule by any means. [^ky-final-pay-no-exemption] An employer that pays late is liable for the unpaid wages plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, with costs and reasonable attorney's fees, because the liability statute covers wages owed under KRS 337.020 to 337.285, a range that includes the final-pay statute. [^ky-unpaid-wages-liquidated-damages] That liquidated-damages remedy uses the chapter's narrower definition of employee, which excludes, among others, agricultural workers, bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees, outside salespeople and outside collectors, and federal employees, although those employees are still owed timely final pay. [^ky-narrow-employee-definition-final-pay] The state may also assess a civil penalty of $100 to $1,000 for each offense, and the employer must pay the employee in full. [^ky-final-pay-civil-penalty]

An employee who is absent when final wages are paid, or is not paid then for any other reason, must be paid at any time afterward or within 14 days of a demand. [^ky-final-pay-no-exemption] Earned, vested vacation pay, severance or dismissal pay and earned bonuses fall within the chapter's definition of wages. [^ky-wages-definition-vacation-bonuses]

A court may reduce or deny liquidated damages if the employer shows it acted in good faith and had reasonable grounds for believing it was not violating the law. [^ky-liquidated-damages-good-faith]

## How often must wages be paid? {#pay-frequency}

**Short answer.** Kentucky requires employers to pay wages at least semimonthly, except to bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees, outside salespeople and outside collectors. [^ky-pay-frequency-right-of-action] [^ky-white-collar-exclusions-pay-frequency] Every employer doing business in the state must, as often as semimonthly, pay each employee all wages earned up to a day no more than 18 days before the payment date. [^ky-semimonthly-eighteen-day-lag] An employee who is absent on payday, or is not paid then for another reason, must be paid at any time afterward on six days' demand, and an employer may not secure an exemption from the rule by any means. [^ky-pay-frequency-demand-no-exemption]

Each employee has a right of action against the employer for the full amount of wages due on each regular payday. [^ky-pay-frequency-right-of-action]

Wages must be paid in United States legal tender, checks on banks, direct deposit or payroll card accounts convertible into cash at full face value. A payroll card may not carry an activation fee and must allow at least one free withdrawal per pay period of up to the full balance. [^ky-wages-payment-methods-payroll-card]

A violation of the semimonthly pay statute carries a civil penalty of $100 to $1,000, and each failure to pay an employee wages when due is a separate offense. [^ky-pay-frequency-civil-penalty]

## What must a pay statement show, and what deductions are allowed? {#wage-statements}

**Short answer.** Kentucky employers with at least 10 employees that deduct from wages must, at each payment, give each employee a paper or electronic statement showing the amount of each deduction and its general purpose. [^ky-deduction-statement-ten-employees] An employer that provides an electronic statement must give the employee access to a computer and printer to review and print it. [^ky-electronic-statement-computer-access] An employer may not withhold any part of the agreed wage unless the law requires or empowers it, or the employee expressly authorizes a listed type of deduction in writing. [^ky-withholding-limits-authorized-deductions]

Some deductions are barred even with the employee's consent. An employer may not deduct fines, cash shortages in a till shared by two or more people, breakage, losses on dishonored checks the employee had discretion to accept, or losses from faulty workmanship, lost or stolen property, property damage, customer credit default or customer nonpayment that are not caused by the employee's willful or intentional disregard of the employer's interest. [^ky-prohibited-deductions-list] An employer that violates the deduction rules owes a civil penalty of $100 to $1,000 and is liable to the employee for the amount withheld plus 10 percent annual interest. [^ky-deduction-violation-interest] Failure to provide the deduction statement carries a civil penalty of $100 to $1,000 for each offense, and each day it continues is a separate offense. [^ky-deduction-statement-daily-penalty]

Expense reimbursement and deductions for business costs are discussed in the [Kentucky expense reimbursement guide](/practice-guides/expense-reimbursement/us/kentucky).

## Employee or independent contractor? {#worker-classification}

**Short answer.** Kentucky courts apply an economic-realities test to decide whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor under the Kentucky Wage and Hour Act. [^ky-mouanda-economic-realities-six-factors] In *Mouanda v. Jani-King International*, 653 S.W.3d 65 (Ky. 2022), the Kentucky Supreme Court adopted that test for the Act, and the court later described its six factors as the permanency of the relationship, the degree of skill required, the worker's investment in equipment or materials, and the worker's opportunity for profit or loss depending on skill [^ky-mouanda-economic-realities-six-factors], along with the alleged employer's right to control the manner in which the work is performed and whether the service is an integral part of the alleged employer's business. [^ky-mouanda-control-integral-factors] The central question is the worker's economic dependence on the business for which the worker labors, and courts look at the practical, not just contractual, realities of the relationship. [^ky-mouanda-economic-dependence-central] The chapter itself defines an employee as any person employed by or suffered or permitted to work for an employer. [^ky-suffer-or-permit-employee-definition]

The Kentucky Supreme Court later extended the *Mouanda* test to workers' compensation in *Oufafa v. Taxi, LLC* (Ky. 2023). [^ky-oufafa-extends-test-workers-comp]

The commissioner's regulation on the employment relation frames the inquiry somewhat differently. It calls control over how the work is performed the principal test, with the method of payment and how freely the possible employer can replace the worker as additional considerations. [^ky-classification-control-principal-test] It names as significant factors, none of them controlling, how integral the services are to the business, the worker's investment, the worker's opportunity for profit and loss, and the initiative, judgment or foresight the worker's independent enterprise requires. [^ky-classification-significant-factors]

The regulation states that the employment relation under the chapter is broader than the common-law master and servant relation because the definition reaches anyone the employer suffers or permits to work. Mere knowledge by an employer of work done for it by another is enough to create the relation. [^ky-suffer-permit-broader-common-law]

Some facts do not matter under the regulation: a government license held by the worker, how compensation is measured or labeled, the fact that a worker relies entirely on tips, the place where the work is performed, and the absence of a formal employment agreement. [^ky-classification-immaterial-factors]

## Is a tip credit allowed? {#tip-credit}

**Short answer.** Kentucky lets an employer pay a tipped employee the same minimum cash wage that federal law sets for tipped employees. [^ky-tip-credit-federal-tipped-rate] For an employee who customarily and regularly receives more than $30 a month in tips, the employer may pay as a minimum the hourly cash wage federal law requires for tipped employees under 29 U.S.C. 203, and must show by its records that, for each week the credit is taken, wages plus tips equaled at least the federal tipped-employee minimum. [^ky-tip-credit-federal-tipped-rate] Federal law sets that cash wage at no less than the cash wage required on August 20, 1996, with tips making up the difference to the federal minimum wage. [^fed-tip-credit-cash-wage-formula] An employer may not use any part of employees' tips toward the minimum wage, apart from the credit itself. [^ky-tip-credit-no-use-of-tips]

Since April 10, 2026, Kentucky's definition of a tipped employee requires both more than $30 a month in tips and work that directly supports or is itself the service function for which a customer would tip, or that directly supports a service function involving direct customer interaction or performed in the customers' direct line of sight. [^ky-tipped-employee-definition-2026] [^ky-tipped-employee-customer-interaction] The commissioner's regulation applies the credit only for hours worked in an occupation in which the employee qualifies as a tipped employee. If tips fall short of the credit, the employer must pay the balance. [^ky-tip-credit-shortfall-balance]

An employer may not require an employee to hand over any tip to the employer, except to withhold amounts federal or state law requires. [^ky-no-remittance-of-gratuities] An employer may mandate a tip pool and may hold the pooled funds for safekeeping if the account is properly identified, kept separate from its other business records and open to examination by the pool's participants. [^ky-tip-pool-custodial-account] A tip pool may include tipped employees but may not include salaried employees, managers or supervisors. [^ky-tip-pool-excludes-managers] An employer subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act can take the federal tip credit only if it has informed the employee of the federal tip-credit provisions and the employee keeps all tips, except for pooling among employees who customarily and regularly receive tips. [^fed-tip-credit-notice-retention-kentucky] Federal law separately bars an employer from keeping employees' tips for any purpose, including letting managers or supervisors keep them. [^fed-employer-may-not-keep-tips]

## How are wage claims enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Kentucky enforces wage and hour law through employee lawsuits, the commissioner of the Department of Workplace Standards and civil penalties. [^ky-employee-action-no-agreement-defense] [^ky-commissioner-assignment-wage-claim] [^ky-underpayment-civil-penalty] An employer that pays less than the wages and overtime owed under KRS 337.020 to 337.285 is liable for the unpaid amount, an equal amount as liquidated damages, and costs and reasonable attorney's fees. [^ky-liquidated-damages-enforcement-action] One or more employees may bring the action in any court of competent jurisdiction, and an agreement to work for less than the applicable wage rate is no defense. [^ky-employee-action-no-agreement-defense] At an employee's written request, the commissioner may take an assignment of the wage claim and sue to collect it. [^ky-commissioner-assignment-wage-claim] These lawsuit and assignment remedies use the chapter's narrower definition of employee, which excludes, among others, agricultural workers, bona fide executive, administrative, supervisory and professional employees, outside salespeople and outside collectors, and federal employees. [^ky-narrow-employee-definition-remedies] An action under the chapter that has no other express limitations period must be commenced within three years after the cause of action accrued. [^ky-three-year-limitations]

The court may reduce or deny liquidated damages if the employer proves good faith and reasonable grounds for believing it was not violating the law. [^ky-good-faith-liquidated-damages-enforcement] Since July 15, 2024, if the court finds the employer subjected the employee to forced labor or services, it must award punitive damages of at least three times the unpaid wages and overtime, plus costs and reasonable attorney's fees. [^ky-forced-labor-treble-punitive]

The Education and Labor Cabinet assesses a civil penalty of $100 to $1,000 on an employer that pays or agrees to pay less than the minimum wage or overtime rate. [^ky-underpayment-civil-penalty] Civil penalties for the seventh-day rule follow the same range. [^ky-seventh-day-civil-penalty] After all appeals are exhausted, a lien for unpaid wages, penalties and 12 percent interest may be placed on the property of an employer assessed civil penalties, and it continues for 10 years from recording unless sooner released or otherwise discharged. [^ky-wage-lien-employer-property]

An employer may not discharge or discriminate against an employee for complaining that the employee has not been paid minimum wage or overtime, or for bringing or testifying in a wage proceeding. [^ky-anti-retaliation-complaint]

# Small-Business Asset Purchase Agreements: Price and Payment[^about]

How the agreement defines the revenue figures, and how deposits, deferred payments, escrows and holdbacks, closing price adjustments, earnouts and the tax allocation of the purchase price work.

## How should the agreement define the revenue figures? {#screenshots}

**Short answer.** The agreement can define which revenue measure counts, for which period and on what accounting basis, as one filed agreement did by requiring the buyer to prepare a computation of revenue under GAAP for its computation period and a separate computation showing each adjustment made to reach adjusted revenue. [^p4-grove-revenue-statement]

The definition matters most where part of the price turns on the figure: the same agreement reduced the purchase price if its minimum revenue target was not attained for the computation period. [^grove-revenue-shortfall] It also required the buyer to keep separate accounting books and records for the business during that period. [^grove-vitamedica-separate-books]

The definition can state whether revenue is gross or net of listed deductions: the same agreement started its adjusted revenues from gross revenues of the business and listed each adjustment, [^d1-grove-adjusted-revenues] and another filed agreement measured a profit figure from net revenue less bad debt and customer chargebacks and credits. [^d1-bankrate-chargebacks]

The seller's historical statements may be unaudited, or prepared on the seller's own accounting basis rather than under GAAP, and the financial-statements representation can say which kind of statements the buyer relied on: one filed agreement included an unaudited inventory analysis among the statements covered by its financial-information representation. [^p4-grove-unaudited-listing] The same agreement represented that its financial statements followed the seller's historical accounting policies and practices, consistently applied, and differed from GAAP in a material respect only as set out in a schedule. [^p4-grove-historical-basis]

The [acquisition financial-model guide](/practice-guides/transaction-workbooks/building-an-auditable-acquisition-workbook) shows how to carry verified figures into the buyer's model and keep purchase consideration, financing and operating cash separate.

## Should the buyer pay a deposit when the purchase agreement is signed? {#buyer-deposit}

**Short answer.** A deposit at signing can protect the seller, because, as Anthony Wilkinson explains, without one a buyer who later terminates may walk away without financial consequence. [^p5-wilkinson-walk-away] The deposit must still be returned to the buyer when the purchase agreement allows the buyer to terminate under specific conditions. [^p5-wilkinson-deposit-returned]

In one filed $700,000 e-commerce asset purchase, the buyer delivered a $175,000 deposit to an escrow agent when the agreement was fully signed, [^p5-igourmet-deposit] and the deposit was credited to the price and released to the seller if the closing occurred. [^p5-igourmet-deposit-credit] The same agreement paid $525,000 at closing, made up of a $350,000 closing payment and the deposit, and the last $175,000 thirty days after closing. [^p5-igourmet-closing-payment] Paying part of the price after closing is covered under [deferred payment](#escrow-holdback-and-deferred-payment).

Anthony Wilkinson describes the deposit as usually held by an independent third party until closing or until the agreement allows the funds to be released. [^p5-wilkinson-escrow-holder] The choice of holder can affect how the process unfolds, particularly when communication or timing becomes important. [^d2-wilkinson-holder-choice] When the deposit is paid to the seller instead, the buyer's protection is the seller's obligation to refund it, and one filed brewery sale secured that refund obligation with equipment finance agreements until closing or termination. [^p5-amerbrew-refund-secured]

What happens to the deposit depends on how the agreement addresses closing, termination or buyer default. [^d2-wilkinson-depends-on-agreement] One brewery sale returned the advance deposit if the closing conditions were not satisfied by the outside date despite the buyer's good-faith efforts and the parties did not agree to extend it. [^p5-amerbrew-deposit-refund] A sale of assets out of bankruptcy let the sellers keep the deposit as a credit at closing or after terminating for the buyer's breach, and otherwise returned it to the buyer after termination, subject to setoff for the buyer's breach. [^p5-ecotality-deposit-retained] The sellers' termination right in that agreement covered the buyer's material breach of a representation or covenant. [^p5-ecotality-termination-grounds] Anthony Wilkinson observes that many agreements let the buyer recover the deposit during an initial diligence period, after which it may become non-refundable if the buyer refuses to close without a valid contractual termination right. [^p5-wilkinson-diligence-period]

For the buyer, the agreement should define the situations in which the deposit is recovered, tied to the [conditions and termination rights](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-signing-to-closing/us#between-signing-and-closing). [^d2-wilkinson-define-recovery] The seller, in turn, may be entitled to keep the deposit if the buyer refuses to close after the agreement's conditions have been satisfied. [^d2-wilkinson-seller-keeps] An agreement may also make the deposit the seller's liquidated damages if the buyer defaults. [^d2-wilkinson-liquidated-damages] In California, for example, a contract provision liquidating damages for breach is valid unless the party challenging it shows it was unreasonable under the circumstances when the contract was made, except in the consumer and residential-lease cases the statute treats separately. [^p5-cal-civ-1671b] Anthony Wilkinson estimates that the deposit in many business transactions ranges from about 1 percent to 10 percent of the price, varying with the size of the transaction, the level of risk and the competition for buyers; that is a practitioner's estimate, and this guide selects no amount. [^d2-wilkinson-deposit-range]

## Should part of the purchase price be paid after closing? {#escrow-holdback-and-deferred-payment}

**Short answer.** Paying part of the price after closing suits a deal in which the buyer wants security for claims that may surface later, since, as Conor Meyers observes, an unsecured indemnity from an individual seller who has spent the proceeds is worth far less than its face value. [^meyers-individual-seller] A holdback, however, leaves the seller carrying the risk that the buyer cannot pay when the holdback falls due. [^dbl-holdback-credit-risk]

The forms differ mainly in who holds the money and what it secures: an escrow, for example, places part of the price with a third party, usually a bank or a law firm, until contractual obligations are met or as security for the seller's representations and warranties. [^d2-dbl-escrow-defined] The table compares the main forms:

| Form | Who holds the money until it is paid | Risk each side keeps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Escrow | A third-party escrow agent under an escrow agreement. [^luna-escrow-fund] | The seller waits for release. [^d2-dbl-escrow-defined] |
| Holdback | The buyer, with no third-party agent. [^dbl-holdback-vs-escrow] | The seller bears the buyer's credit risk. [^dbl-holdback-credit-risk] |
| Fixed deferred payment | The buyer, until the agreed date. [^ivfh-post-closing-payment] | The seller waits for payment until the agreed date. [^ivfh-post-closing-payment] |
| Seller note | The buyer, which owes the balance under a promissory note that may be secured by the purchased assets. [^ratschko-promissory-note] | A senior lender may require the seller's note to be subordinated. [^elkhoury-seller-note-subordination] |

One filed product-line sale held back $320,000 of the closing price for twelve months, subject to reduction under its indemnity article. [^softech-closing-price-holdback] A $700,000 e-commerce asset purchase deferred $175,000 of the price to thirty days after closing. [^ivfh-post-closing-payment] A larger transaction held its escrow amount as security for the seller's indemnification obligations. [^luna-escrow-fund] An online-business sale run through a broker gave the buyer fourteen days after migration to inspect the assets before the price was released. [^jeffs-inspection-period] These are examples of negotiated terms; Wright Lewis estimates that around 10 to 25 percent of the price is typically held back or placed in escrow, a practitioner's estimate rather than a measured survey. [^d2-dbl-typical-percent]

The amount of an indemnity holdback is usually based on a percentage of the price, though it may be increased for a specific known risk of an indemnity claim. [^d2-schwabe-holdback-amount] The holdback period depends largely on the survival periods for indemnity claims, [^schwabe-holdback-survival] and a seller may negotiate release in stages over the holdback or escrow period. [^dbl-graduated-release] The parties also decide what the reserve secures: buyers generally prefer one escrow for indemnity claims and price adjustments so that more funds are available, while sellers prefer separate accounts that isolate their exposure and allow separate release dates. [^d2-schwabe-one-or-separate-escrow] A reserve can also secure a specific tax obligation: in California, a buyer of a business must withhold enough of the price to cover the seller's sales or use tax until the seller produces a receipt showing payment or a certificate that nothing is due, as the [successor-liability question](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-structure-and-parties/us#successor-liability) explains. [^d2-ca-rtc-6811-withhold]

A seller note has its own tradeoffs, and when a bank or SBA loan also finances the purchase, Andrew Elkhoury observes that the lender will almost always require the seller note to be subordinated to its senior debt. [^elkhoury-seller-note-subordination] A note or earnout gives the buyer something to set claims off against, which makes an escrow less important to the buyer, [^dbl-note-setoff] but setoff against a note is less useful than cash when a large loss arises. [^dbl-setoff-weaker] An express setoff right can be drafted to fit the note: one filed agreement let the buyers set off indemnity claims against amounts payable under the seller's convertible note even if disputed, and provided that a good-faith setoff, whether or not ultimately justified, would not be an event of default under the notes. [^d2-grove-setoff] Another filed agreement allowed setoff against the seller's note only for amounts the seller agreed were due or a court had adjudged. [^d2-salem-setoff] A [deposit paid at signing](#buyer-deposit) is a separate term that protects the seller before closing.

## Should the price adjust for inventory or prepaid customer obligations at closing? {#closing-price-adjustment}

**Short answer.** For inventory, one approach to a closing price adjustment, as Barlow & Williams describe it, states an assumed inventory amount in the asset purchase agreement and then changes the price at closing for the difference between that amount and the actual inventory value shown by an inventory report. [^barlow-inventory-baseline][^barlow-inventory-trueup]

An adjustment accounts for changes in the business's financial condition between signing and closing, [^d2-schwabe-adjustment-purpose] and the need is especially acute when several months pass between signing and closing, as when closing awaits regulatory or third-party approvals. [^d2-schwabe-adjustment-delay] For an e-commerce or other product business, the parties may agree to value inventory before closing to determine the final price. [^ratschko-inventory-valuation] Prepaid customer obligations are the mirror image, because they are liabilities the buyer takes on; Barlow & Williams advise that a seller with many prepaid annual subscriptions should expect the buyer to ask for a price reduction for those ongoing liabilities. [^d2-barlow-prepaid-reduction] One filed agreement for an online-service business subtracted customers' deferred revenue from the price, [^sitestar-deferred-revenue] and a filed e-commerce agreement adjusted its deferred payment for revenue the seller received before closing on orders the buyer fulfilled afterward. [^ivfh-prepaid-orders] The [customer-obligations question](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-after-closing/us#customer-obligation-cutoff) covers how to allocate those obligations.

Specifying the procedures for determining the adjustment as precisely as possible reduces the risk of a dispute, [^barlow-specific-procedures] and Barlow & Williams describe a seller review period for the closing statement that is commonly 30 days and may be shorter in smaller transactions. [^barlow-review-period] The measurement need not happen at the moment of closing, but there does need to be an exact time. [^d2-barlow-exact-time] Normally the buyer prepares the closing statement supporting the adjustment, [^d2-barlow-buyer-prepares] and the clause should specify which accounting principles apply. [^d2-schwabe-accounting-principles] Referring a dispute to an independent accountant is one option; one filed agreement limited the accountant's determination to the range between the parties' positions. [^bankrate-accountant-range]

A working-capital adjustment of the kind used in larger acquisitions measures current assets such as receivables and inventory against current liabilities; one larger filed agreement defined net working capital that way, excluding assets and liabilities that did not transfer. [^p5-luna-net-working-capital] Many small online businesses have little of that to measure: most software-as-a-service and e-commerce businesses do not have accounts receivable, while businesses on deferred payment terms may see adjustments based on the buyer's ability to collect. [^d2-barlow-no-receivables]

A [deferred payment](#escrow-holdback-and-deferred-payment) can be the source of payment for an adjustment owed to the buyer: the buyer may demand that part of the price be escrowed so that the seller has funds to pay it, [^d2-schwabe-adjustment-escrow] and one filed agreement made the seller's convertible note subject to offset for a negative working-capital adjustment. [^d2-grove-nwc-offset] An adjustment also changes the [tax allocation](#purchase-price-allocation), because an increase or decrease in consideration after the purchase date must be allocated among the assets. [^d2-irs-8594-reallocation-cpa]

## Should part of the price depend on future performance? {#price-mechanisms}

**Short answer.** If the parties choose an earnout, the agreement can give the seller a way to verify the payment, as one filed asset purchase agreement did by giving the seller and its accountants reasonable access to the buyer's books, records, workpapers and personnel to verify the earnout calculation. [^earnout-review-price-mechanisms]

An earnout, an indemnity holdback and a closing adjustment each provide a way to adjust the price to reflect the business's value more accurately, [^d2-pm-schwabe-three-mechanisms] but they respond to different events. An earnout provides for an upward adjustment based on the business's performance after closing. [^d2-pm-schwabe-earnout] An indemnity holdback temporarily reduces the amount paid at closing and is held in escrow to cover the seller's indemnity obligations. [^d2-pm-schwabe-holdback] A closing adjustment accounts for changes in the business's financial condition between signing and closing. [^d2-pm-schwabe-adjustment] The [deferred-payment question](#escrow-holdback-and-deferred-payment) and the [closing-adjustment question](#closing-price-adjustment) cover the other two mechanisms.

Andrew J. Schultheis and Dylan Lowe describe earnouts as among the most frequently litigated provisions in acquisitions. [^dwt-earnout-litigated] In their account, a well-drafted earnout addresses how performance is measured, how payments are made, how the seller's interests are protected during the earnout period and how disputes are resolved, [^d2-dwt-four-areas] with the accounting standards, measurement periods and any inclusions or exclusions defined clearly. [^d2-dwt-define-accounting] They explain that sellers often prefer revenue metrics because a buyer can less easily reduce revenue through spending decisions or expense allocations, [^dwt-earnout-revenue] and recommend that sellers seek at least a covenant to operate the business in the ordinary course, [^dwt-earnout-ordinary-course] while buyers want flexibility to run and integrate the business. [^dwt-earnout-buyer-flexibility] If the parties cannot agree the calculation, an independent accountant can decide; one filed agreement limited that determination to the range between the parties' positions. [^bankrate-earnout-accountant-range] Schultheis and Lowe report that earnout periods commonly range from one to three years, a practitioners' estimate rather than a measured survey; this guide supplies no amount, target, period or performance guarantee. [^d2-dwt-earnout-period]

## How does the purchase-price allocation affect each side's taxes? {#purchase-price-allocation}

**Short answer.** The purchase-price allocation sets the buyer's tax basis in each acquired asset, which governs the buyer's later deductions such as the 15-year amortization of goodwill and other section 197 intangibles, and sets the seller's gain or loss on each asset transferred. [^irs-8594-allocation-purpose][^irc-197d-covenant][^irc-197a-amortization]

For the buyer, a covenant not to compete entered into in connection with the acquisition of a trade or business is a section 197 intangible, like goodwill, [^irc-197d-covenant] and an amortizable section 197 intangible is amortized ratably over 15 years, subject to the section's eligibility conditions and exceptions. [^irc-197a-amortization] Software has its own exception: section 197 excludes computer software that is readily available to the general public, nonexclusively licensed and not substantially modified, and other software not acquired in a transaction involving the acquisition of a trade or business. [^d2-irc-197e3-software]

For the seller, each asset in the sale of a business is generally treated as sold separately, and the sale of inventory results in ordinary income or loss while the sale of capital assets results in capital gain or loss. [^d2-irs-p544-sale-of-business] The Internal Revenue Code excludes inventory and property held primarily for sale to customers from the definition of a capital asset. [^p5-irc-1221-inventory]

The parties cannot allocate freely: under the IRS instructions, the amount allocated to an asset other than a Class VII asset cannot exceed its fair market value on the purchase date, [^p5-irs-8594-fmv-cap] and Class VII assets are goodwill and going concern value. [^p5-irs-8594-class-vii]

Whether an allocation must be reported depends on the transaction: the IRS instructions require both the seller and the purchaser of a group of assets that makes up a trade or business to use Form 8594 if goodwill or going concern value attaches, or could attach, and the purchaser's basis is determined only by the amount paid. [^irs-8594-filing-conditions] The instructions also list exceptions, including an exchange of the group of assets for like-kind property under section 1031 and the transfer of a partnership interest. [^d2-irs-8594-exceptions]

For an applicable asset acquisition, the Internal Revenue Code makes a written agreement between buyer and seller on the allocation, or on the fair market value of any asset, binding on both of them unless the IRS determines that it is not appropriate. [^irc-1060a-binding-allocation] An agreement can therefore set a process for agreeing the allocation, as one filed brewery sale did by having the buyer prepare an allocation schedule before closing in accordance with IRS pronouncements. [^p5-amerbrew-allocation-schedule] It can also commit both parties to report consistently: another filed agreement required each party to file its returns on the basis of the agreed allocation, barred any inconsistent return position and required each party to use the allocation for its Form 8594. [^d2-sitestar-consistent-reporting] A later increase or decrease in consideration that changes the seller's amount realized or the purchaser's basis must itself be allocated among the assets, [^d2-irs-8594-reallocation] and if the amount allocated to any asset increases or decreases after the year of the sale, the affected party files a supplemental Form 8594 with the return for the year in which the change is taken into account. [^p5-irs-8594-supplemental]

The tax advisers determine applicability and reconcile the allocation, payment structure and reporting responsibilities with the agreement, and this guide supplies no preferred allocation or nationwide tax solution. [^irs-8594-allocation-purpose]

Transfer taxes are a separate economic choice that the agreement can allocate: one filed agreement made the buyer bear all sales, value added, use, transfer, registration, stamp and similar taxes imposed in connection with the sale. [^d2-greenfield-transfer-taxes] Tax clearance for the seller's unpaid sales taxes is addressed under [successor liability](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-structure-and-parties/us#successor-liability).

# Connecticut Asset Protection Trusts: Qualified Dispositions, Creditors, Divorce and Land[^about]

How Connecticut's Qualified Dispositions in Trust Act works, which creditors can still reach a self-settled trust, and how divorce and land rules apply.

## What does a Connecticut self-settled trust need to qualify for protection under the 2019 Act? {#qualifying-trust}

**Short answer.** A transfer is protected under the Connecticut Qualified Dispositions in Trust Act only if it is made to at least one qualified trustee under a written, irrevocable trust instrument with a spendthrift clause and, with one exception, a clause expressly choosing Connecticut law. [^ct-487k-trust-instrument-three-requirements] [^ct-487m-nonqualified-trustee-transfer-exception]
The one exception covers a transfer from a trustee who is not a qualified trustee to a qualified trustee, which does not fail to qualify solely because the instrument lacks the Connecticut governing-law clause. [^ct-487m-nonqualified-trustee-transfer-exception] The governing-law clause must make Connecticut law govern the trust's validity, construction and administration, and the spendthrift clause must bar transfer of the interest of the transferor (the person who funds the trust) or any other beneficiary before distribution. [^ct-487k-trust-instrument-three-requirements] The Act was enacted by Public Act 19-137 and took effect January 1, 2020. [^ct-487j-short-title-pa-19-137] Its operative sections apply to qualified dispositions made on or after that date. [^ct-487s-dispositions-on-or-after-2020]
The transferor can keep listed benefits and powers without making the trust revocable, including a veto over distributions, a will-exercised power of appointment that cannot benefit the transferor or the transferor's estate or creditors, and the right to income. [^ct-487n-veto-appointment-income] The transferor can also receive principal when a qualified trustee acts in its discretion, under a standard that gives the transferor no substantially unfettered right to principal, or at the direction of a trust director acting in that way. [^ct-487n-principal-trustee-discretion] Separately, the statute presumes that a qualified trustee has discretion over principal unless the instrument expressly denies it, but by its text that presumption applies only for purposes of the subparagraph in which it appears, § 45a-487n(6)(C), the provision on a trustee acting at a trust director's direction. [^ct-487n-principal-trustee-discretion] Beyond the rights the instrument confers, the transferor has no rights over the trust property, and any side agreement granting greater rights is void. [^ct-487o-side-agreement-void]
Apart from the claims § 45a-487q preserves, which are support, alimony or property-division debts already owed under an agreement or court order at the time of the transfer and claims for death, injury or property damage occurring on or before it [^ct-487q-family-debts-preserved-qualifying] [^ct-487q-pre-transfer-torts-preserved-qualifying], a creditor's challenge to a transfer runs through the Uniform Fraudulent Transfer Act remedy in § 52-552h, which § 45a-487p(a) makes the only route, with each element proved by clear and convincing evidence. [^ct-487p-ufta-only-route-clear-convincing]

"The transferor shall have only such powers and rights as are conferred by the trust instrument."[^ct-487o-side-agreement-void]

## Can the person who creates a Connecticut asset protection trust, or a relative, serve as its trustee? {#trustee}

**Short answer.** The person who funds a Connecticut qualified disposition trust (the transferor) cannot be its qualified trustee, but the Act does not otherwise require that trustee to be independent of the transferor. [^ct-487k-qualified-trustee-excludes-transferor]
A qualified trustee is a Connecticut-resident individual other than the transferor, or a state or federally chartered bank or trust company that has a Connecticut place of business and is authorized to do trust business in Connecticut. [^ct-487k-qualified-trustee-residence-and-activities] The same definition lists Connecticut administration activities: custody of some trust property in Connecticut, Connecticut trust records, Connecticut fiduciary income tax returns, or other material participation in administering the trust in Connecticut. [^ct-487k-qualified-trustee-residence-and-activities] The sentence can be read to apply that activity list to every qualified trustee or only to banks and trust companies, because § 45a-487k(9)(A) strings the residence test and the activity list together without separating them, and no Connecticut decision construing it was found in our review. A conservative reading is that an individual trustee also performs at least one of the listed activities in Connecticut. [^ct-487k-qualified-trustee-residence-and-activities]
The related-or-subordinate test of 26 U.S.C. § 672(c) appears in the Act only as a limit on the transferor's retained power to remove a trustee or director and appoint a replacement. [^ct-487n-removal-power-not-related-party] A transferor can serve as a trust director, but only with the veto over distributions. [^ct-487l-transferor-director-veto-only] In an action against a trustee of a trust funded wholly or partly by a qualified disposition, if the court declines to apply Connecticut law to the trust's validity, construction or administration or to the effect of its spendthrift provision, the trustee immediately ceases to be trustee. A successor then succeeds under the trust instrument; if the instrument provides no successor and the trust would otherwise be without a trustee, the court with jurisdiction appoints one on a beneficiary's application. [^ct-487p-trustee-ceases-if-ct-law-rejected]
Independence also matters in litigation. In *Netter v. Netter*, the Appellate Court rejected a trial-court finding that a settlor controlled his self-settled trusts because a disinterested trustee had to approve every distribution, although the court treated the trusts as divisible marital property on other grounds. [^netter-disinterested-trustee-defeats-control-finding] [^netter-trusts-divisible-despite-trustee-approval]

## How long does a creditor have to challenge a transfer to a Connecticut asset protection trust? {#challenge-deadline}

**Short answer.** Apart from the family-debt and pre-transfer injury claims that § 45a-487q preserves, a creditor whose claim arose before a transfer to a Connecticut qualified disposition trust must sue within four years after the transfer or, if later, within one year after it was or could reasonably have been discovered. [^ct-487p-two-limitation-periods] [^ct-487q-family-debts-preserved-deadline] [^ct-487q-pre-transfer-torts-preserved-deadline] A creditor whose claim arose after the transfer has a flat four years. [^ct-487p-two-limitation-periods]
The suit must be brought under § 52-552h of Connecticut's Uniform Fraudulent Transfer Act, and the creditor must prove each element by clear and convincing evidence. [^ct-487p-clear-convincing-burden] [^ct-ufta-short-title] That route and these deadlines are qualified by § 45a-487q, which states that the Act's sections shall not apply to defeat claims for support, alimony or property-division debts already owed under an agreement or court order at the time of the transfer, or claims for death, injury or property damage occurring on or before it. [^ct-487q-family-debts-preserved-deadline] [^ct-487q-pre-transfer-torts-preserved-deadline] If the creditor wins, the transfer is undone only to the extent needed to pay that creditor's debt, plus costs and attorney's fees the court allows. [^ct-487r-avoidance-only-to-extent-of-debt] After avoidance, a beneficiary who did not act in bad faith keeps distributions the trustee properly made in the exercise of a trust power or discretion before the creditor sued to avoid the transfer. [^ct-487r-beneficiary-retains-good-faith-distributions] To defeat that protection, the creditor generally must prove the beneficiary's bad faith by clear and convincing evidence, but must prove a transferor-beneficiary's bad faith only by a preponderance of the evidence. [^ct-487r-transferor-beneficiary-bad-faith-standard] This lower standard concerns that bad-faith showing; the elements of the avoidance action itself still require clear and convincing proof under § 45a-487p(a). [^ct-487p-clear-convincing-burden] A creditor has no claim against the trustee, a trust director, or anyone involved in counseling, drafting, preparing, executing or funding the trust. [^ct-487p-no-claim-against-trustee-or-advisers]
Section 52-552e(a), the Connecticut provision on transfers made with actual intent to defraud, is headed and written for creditors whose claims arose before the transfer. [^ct-52-552e-present-creditors-only] In *Daly v. Parete (In re Carrozzella & Richardson)*, the Bankruptcy Court for the District of Connecticut said it was not at all clear that § 52-552e(a)(1) would apply to actual intent to defraud future creditors. [^carrozzella-future-creditors-doubt] Read together, § 45a-487p(b)(2) assumes a creditor whose claim arose after the transfer can sue, but the only cause of action § 45a-487p(a) allows may not reach that creditor. No Connecticut appellate decision resolving that gap was found in our review, so on this reading a later creditor faces a real risk of having no avoidance claim, while a creditor whose claim predates the transfer has a clear statutory path. [^ct-487p-two-limitation-periods] [^ct-52-552e-present-creditors-only]

"(2) The creditor's claim against the transferor arose subsequent to the qualified disposition, unless the action is brought within four years after the qualified disposition is made."[^ct-487p-two-limitation-periods]

## Which creditors can still reach a Connecticut asset protection trust? {#exception-creditors}

**Short answer.** The Connecticut Act does not defeat two kinds of claims against the transferor: support, alimony or property-division debts already owed under an agreement or court order when the transfer is made, and claims for death, injury or property damage that occurred on or before the transfer. [^ct-487q-support-alimony-property-division] [^ct-487q-pre-transfer-torts]
The support and alimony exception covers debts owed to the transferor's spouse, former spouse or children, and the property-division exception covers debts owed to a spouse or former spouse. Each applies only to the extent of the debt. [^ct-487q-support-alimony-property-division] The tort exception covers injury caused in whole or in part by the transferor or by someone for whom the transferor is vicariously liable, whenever the cause is determined, but only to the extent of the claim. [^ct-487q-pre-transfer-torts] For both exceptions, a spouse or former spouse means only a person married to the transferor at or before the transfer. [^ct-487k-spouse-married-at-or-before]
Read literally, § 45a-487q(1) covers only support, alimony or property-division debts already owed under an agreement or court order on or before the transfer, so a claimant whose debt arose afterward would have to use the fraudulent transfer route and its deadlines. [^ct-487q-support-alimony-property-division] A divorce court, however, separately has power to assign to either spouse all or part of the other spouse's estate, and the divorce question on this page explains how the Appellate Court has used that power against self-settled trusts. [^ct-46b-81-assign-estate]
The Act also excludes from qualified dispositions any transfer that would defeat a state or federal Medicaid recovery claim under 42 U.S.C. chapter 7, subchapter XIX, or that is weighed in a Medicaid eligibility determination. [^ct-487k-medicaid-carve-out] The Act's exception list contains no other category, such as a general exception for state claims. [^ct-487q-pre-transfer-torts]

## Will a Connecticut divorce court divide assets in a self-settled trust governed by another state's law? {#divorce}

**Short answer.** In *Netter v. Netter*, decided October 21, 2025, the Connecticut Appellate Court held that three self-settled spendthrift trusts governed by South Dakota law violated Connecticut public policy and were divisible marital property in a Connecticut divorce. [^netter-self-settled-trusts-violate-public-policy] [^netter-south-dakota-law-governs-interpretation]
The court held that the trust agreements were not valid trust instruments under the Connecticut Act. [^netter-not-valid-trust-instruments] The Act's definition requires an instrument that expressly makes Connecticut law govern the trust's validity, construction and administration. [^ct-487k-governing-law-requirement-divorce] Read together with the court's agreement that South Dakota law governed its interpretation of the agreements [^netter-south-dakota-law-governs-interpretation], the defect was the agreements' choice of South Dakota law rather than Connecticut law. For an existing trust from another state, § 45a-487m(c) allows a trustee who is not a qualified trustee to transfer the assets to a Connecticut qualified trustee, and that transfer does not fail to qualify solely because the instrument lacks a Connecticut governing-law clause. [^ct-487m-transfer-to-qualified-trustee]
The court stated the rule that decided the case: a self-settled spendthrift trust that does not satisfy the Act, or whose enforcement would substantially prejudice the parties, falls back on Connecticut law as it stood before the Act and is void as a matter of public policy. [^netter-noncompliant-trust-void-rule] The court also relied on the trial court's unchallenged findings that the settlor had moved most of the marital assets into the trusts when he knew a divorce was imminent. [^netter-assets-moved-before-divorce] On that record it upheld the trial court's conclusion that the three self-settled trusts were divisible marital property. [^netter-self-settled-trusts-divisible-conclusion]
A spendthrift trust created for the settlor by his father was treated differently. [^netter-father-created-spendthrift-trust] The settlor had no presently enforceable right to its assets, so it was not property the court could divide under § 46b-81. [^netter-third-party-trust-not-divisible] *Netter* is a property-division ruling, and the court reversed the financial orders and remanded for a new trial on all financial issues. [^netter-remand-financial-orders]
*Netter* does not decide how a divorce court treats a trust that complies with the Act and was funded after January 1, 2020, in a proceeding begun after that date. The prejudice provision the court cited, § 45a-487t(a)(3), applies by its terms to judicial proceedings commenced before January 1, 2020. [^ct-487t-pre-2020-proceedings-prejudice] Read together, the prejudice route may not be available against a compliant trust in a later proceeding, and whether a court would reach a similar result for such a trust through its general power to divide marital property is untested in the authorities found in our review.

"Thus, if a self-settled spendthrift trust agreement does not satisfy the Connecticut act’s requirements, or if a court finds that sustaining a self-settled spendthrift trust would substantially prejudice the rights of the parties, the law as it existed in Connecticut prior to the legislation is applicable; see General Statutes § 45a-487t (a) (3); and the trust will be void as a matter of public policy."[^netter-noncompliant-trust-void-rule]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A governing-law clause selecting another state's law, without a qualifying transfer under § 45a-487m(c), can leave the trust outside the Act and expose its assets to division in a Connecticut divorce. [^ct-487k-governing-law-requirement-divorce] [^ct-487m-transfer-to-qualified-trustee] [^netter-not-valid-trust-instruments] [^netter-self-settled-trusts-divisible-conclusion]

## Can creditors reach a Connecticut settlor's self-settled trust that does not meet the Act's requirements? {#nonqualifying-trusts}

**Short answer.** Under *Greenwich Trust Co. v. Tyson* (1942), a settlor's creditors can reach trust income payable to the settlor from a self-settled trust outside the Connecticut Act, whether the settlor is the sole income beneficiary or the trustee has discretion to pay income to the settlor. [^tyson-settlor-sole-income-reachable] [^tyson-discretionary-income-reachable]
*Tyson* explained that the public policy supporting spendthrift trusts created for someone else is overborne when the settlor is the beneficiary. [^tyson-self-settled-policy-overborne] The discretionary-income rule applies even when the trustee may also pay or use the income for others. [^tyson-discretionary-income-reachable] Principal the trustee could distribute to the settlor is also reachable under the rule applied in *In re Brooks* (Bankr. D. Conn. 1998). That court quoted the Restatement rule that a settlor's creditors can reach the maximum amount of income or principal the trustee could pay to the settlor [^brooks-discretionary-principal-rule-nonqualifying], applied it to trusts giving the debtor all income and discretionary principal [^brooks-rule-applied-income-and-principal-nonqualifying], and held the transferred property part of the bankruptcy estate. [^brooks-transferred-property-in-estate] *Brooks* is a federal bankruptcy court decision applying Connecticut law, so it does not bind Connecticut courts. Whether creditors can reach principal that the trustee has no power to distribute to the settlor is not established by the authorities found in our review. As context, the Appellate Court in *Netter v. Netter* (2025), a divorce property-division ruling, treated pre-Act law as the rule for a self-settled spendthrift trust that does not satisfy the Act and stated that such a trust is void as a matter of public policy. [^netter-pre-act-law-governs-noncompliant-trust] Connecticut's trust code also withholds its own limits on beneficiaries' creditors from creditors of a settlor who is a beneficiary. [^ct-499nn-settlor-creditors-excluded]
Routing the settlor's property through another person may not avoid this result. In *Brooks*, the debtor transferred property to his spouse under a plan for the spouse to place it in spendthrift trusts for the debtor's benefit. [^brooks-transfer-through-wife-plan] The court determined that the trusts were self-settled and held the spendthrift provisions unenforceable under Connecticut law. [^brooks-trusts-determined-self-settled] [^brooks-spendthrift-unenforceable-ct-law]

"If in such a trust the settlor is the sole person entitled to the income, that income can be reached by his creditors."[^tyson-settlor-sole-income-reachable]

## If a trust governed by another state's law owns Connecticut land, which state's law decides whether creditors can reach it? {#connecticut-land}

**Short answer.** Whether a settlor's creditors can reach Connecticut land held in another state's self-settled trust is probably decided by Connecticut law, because federal courts following the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws apply the law of the state where the land is. [^huckaby-situs-law-creditor-reach] [^anselmi-situs-law-beneficial-interest-in-land] No Connecticut appellate decision on the question was found in our review.
In *United States v. Huckaby*, a federal district court in California applied federal common-law choice-of-law rules that follow the Restatement [^huckaby-federal-choice-of-law-restatement] and found its § 280 persuasive on whether a beneficiary's interest in a trust of land can be reached by creditors, quoting it as sending that question to the law the situs courts would apply. [^huckaby-situs-law-creditor-reach] The settlors were also the trustees and sole lifetime beneficiaries [^huckaby-settlors-trustees-beneficiaries], and they argued that Nevada law governed because the trust's terms designated it a Nevada spendthrift trust. [^huckaby-nevada-designation-argument] Because the land was in California, the court applied California law. [^huckaby-california-land-california-law] It held the judgment liens enforceable against the property to the extent of the debtor's interest. [^huckaby-liens-enforceable] The order is dated March 3, 2026, and is a federal trial-court order that does not bind Connecticut courts. As of this guide's October 5, 2026 review, the latest record found in the case was a May 26, 2026 order, which recites that the government moved for an order of judicial sale on April 20, 2026. [^huckaby-judicial-sale-motion]
In *In re Anselmi* (Bankr. D. Wyo. 1985), the court stated that when the trust property is real property, the law of the situs of the land decides whether a beneficiary's interest is exempt from creditors' process, citing Restatement § 280. [^anselmi-situs-law-beneficial-interest-in-land] *F.P.P. Enterprises v. United States* (D. Neb. 1986) stated that the validity of a trust of an interest in land is determined by the law the situs courts would apply, citing § 278. [^fpp-validity-situs-law] *Hartsfield v. Lescher* (E.D. Ark. 1989) quoted § 279 for the same rule on administration of a trust of land. [^hartsfield-administration-situs-law] These federal decisions do not bind Connecticut courts.
The only Connecticut statement found in our review is dictum in a 1960 Superior Court decision, *Stetson v. Morgan Guaranty Trust Co.*, that the validity of a trust of realty has generally been determined by the law of the situs, and that courts tend to respect the settlor's chosen law. [^stetson-realty-situs-dictum] [^stetson-settlor-choice-tendency] Connecticut's trust code honors a trust's chosen law for the meaning and effect of its terms unless that choice is contrary to a strong public policy of the jurisdiction with the most significant relationship. [^ct-499g-governing-law-public-policy]
Read together, the situs authorities suggest that a Connecticut court asked about Connecticut land held directly in another state's self-settled trust would probably apply Connecticut law to the creditor question, unless the trust's own choice of law were honored; that prediction is an inference, not a holding. Under Connecticut law outside the Act, *Greenwich Trust Co. v. Tyson* holds that a settlor's creditors can reach the trust income when the settlor is the sole person entitled to it [^tyson-settlor-income-reachable-situs], and the same applies to income the trustee may pay to the settlor in its discretion. [^tyson-discretionary-income-situs] *Netter v. Netter*, a divorce property-division ruling, states that a self-settled spendthrift trust that does not satisfy the Act is governed by prior Connecticut law and void as a matter of public policy. [^netter-noncompliant-trust-void-situs] *In re Brooks*, a federal bankruptcy decision applying Connecticut law that does not bind Connecticut courts, applied the rule that a settlor's creditors can reach the maximum amount of income or principal the trustee could pay to the settlor. [^brooks-discretionary-principal-rule-land] [^brooks-rule-applied-income-and-principal-land] Read together, principal the trustee could distribute to the settlor is likely reachable as well. Whether creditors can reach principal outside any power to distribute it to the settlor, or the land itself, is not established by the authorities found in our review.

## What happens to a Connecticut joint tenancy when one owner deeds a share to a trust? {#joint-ownership-deeds}

**Short answer.** When one joint tenant conveys his or her interest to a trustee, Connecticut law severs the joint tenancy as to that interest, and the trustee holds it as a tenant in common with the remaining owner. [^ct-47-14c-conveyance-severs-joint-tenancy]
Read with that rule, the conveyed share no longer carries the joint tenants' right of survivorship, because the trustee holds it as a tenant in common rather than as a joint tenant. [^ct-47-14c-conveyance-severs-joint-tenancy] Connecticut does not recognize tenancy by the entirety. A deed to two grantees as tenants by the entirety creates a joint tenancy with right of survivorship [^ct-47-14a-entirety-deed-creates-joint-tenancy], and in *New Haven Trolley & Bus Employees Credit Union v. Hill* (1958) the Connecticut Supreme Court said Connecticut early refused to recognize that estate. [^new-haven-trolley-no-tenancy-by-entirety] During a joint tenant's life, that owner's interest can be attached, liened or sold on execution as if it were held as a tenant in common. [^ct-47-14f-joint-tenant-interest-attachable] This suggests that moving one spouse's share of Connecticut land into a trust gives up no entireties-style creditor protection, because Connecticut joint tenancy does not provide it.
A Connecticut deed must be in writing, signed by the grantor, acknowledged, and attested by two witnesses. [^ct-47-5-deed-two-witnesses] It must also state the grantee's current mailing address. [^ct-47-5-grantee-mailing-address] The statute quoted here is the text revised to January 1, 2025; this guide's October 5, 2026 review found no later amendment to § 47-5 in the 2026 Supplement or in the Public Acts of the 2026 regular session.
Because the trustee becomes the holder of the conveyed share [^ct-47-14c-conveyance-severs-joint-tenancy], whether an existing owner's title insurance policy continues to protect the trustee depends on that policy's terms, which this guide does not review. The title insurer that issued the policy is the source for that confirmation.

## Does holding Connecticut land through an LLC owned by the trust change creditors' remedies or transfer taxes? {#llc-holding}

**Short answer.** For a Connecticut limited liability company, a judgment creditor of a member can collect from the member's interest only through a charging order, and foreclosure of that interest is unavailable whether the company has one member or more. [^ct-34-259b-charging-order-exclusive]
A charging order is a lien that entitles the creditor only to distributions the member would otherwise have received. [^ct-34-259b-charging-order-distributions-only] The charging-order limit applies when a judgment creditor enforces a judgment against the member or transferee that holds the interest. [^ct-34-259b-charging-order-exclusive] Whether a trust or its trustee is a member of a trust-owned LLC depends on admission under the LLC Act: at formation, a person becomes a member as agreed with the organizer or among the initial members, and afterward as the operating agreement provides, through an entity transaction, with the consent of all members, or in the other case the statute lists. [^ct-34-255-becoming-a-member] Our reading is that a creditor of the trust's settlor needs a separate legal basis to reach a trust-held interest at all, such as the self-settled trust rules discussed in the question on self-settled trusts that do not meet the Act's requirements; the charging-order limit then governs how a judgment against the holder of the interest is enforced. [^ct-34-259b-charging-order-distributions-only] Connecticut law governs the internal affairs of a Connecticut LLC and whether its members are liable for its debts, while the law of the formation state governs those matters for a foreign LLC. [^ct-34-243c-ct-llc-governing-law] [^ct-34-275-foreign-llc-governing-law] Whether a charging-order limit counts as an internal affair, and so follows the formation state when a Connecticut creditor sues, is not settled by those sections.
Holding land through an LLC may not change which state's law applies to a Connecticut settlor's trust. In *In re Brooks*, a bankruptcy court in Connecticut applied Connecticut law to certificates the debtor had routed into Bermuda and Jersey spendthrift trusts [^brooks-stock-in-offshore-trusts-llc] and held the spendthrift provisions unenforceable. [^brooks-spendthrift-unenforceable-llc] Read together, this suggests that converting land into an LLC interest does not by itself take a Connecticut settlor's self-settled trust outside Connecticut law.
Connecticut's real estate conveyance tax applies only when the consideration is at least $2,000, at a state rate starting at 0.75 percent plus a base municipal portion of 0.25 percent. [^ct-12-494-conveyance-tax-consideration] A targeted investment community, or a municipality with properties designated as manufacturing plants under § 32-75c, may impose an additional tax of up to 0.25 percent of the consideration on the same conveyances. [^ct-12-494c-additional-municipal-tax] Deeds for less than $2,000, deeds between spouses, and transfers that are a mere change of identity or form of ownership with no change in beneficial ownership are exempt. [^ct-12-498-conveyance-tax-exemptions] Whether a deed into a self-settled trust, or into an LLC the trust owns, is a mere change of form with no change in beneficial ownership is not answered by the statute text. The regulation on what counts as consideration, including an assumed mortgage, was not reviewed for this guide.
Connecticut also taxes the sale or transfer of a controlling interest, meaning more than 50 percent, in an entity that possesses, directly or indirectly, an interest in Connecticut real property whose present true and actual value is at least $2,000. [^ct-12-638b-controlling-interest-tax-rate] The tax is 1.11 percent of the present true and actual value of that direct or indirect interest in Connecticut real property, and a trust counts as an entity for this purpose. [^ct-12-638a-controlling-interest-includes-trust] [^ct-12-638b-controlling-interest-tax-rate] Section 12-638b(a)(1) itself does not mention consideration [^ct-12-638b-controlling-interest-tax-rate], but the Department of Revenue Services describes the tax as imposed on the sale or transfer for consideration of a controlling interest. [^ct-drs-controlling-interest-for-consideration] Read together, a genuinely gratuitous transfer of a controlling LLC interest to the trust appears to fall outside the tax on the Department's reading, while a transfer made for noncash consideration may still be a transfer for consideration under that description. The mere-change-of-form exemption also applies to this tax, and its application to a self-settled trust is untested in the authorities reviewed for this guide. [^ct-12-638b-mere-change-of-form-exemption]

## Can spouses share one Connecticut asset protection trust, and who is the transferor when one spouse's will funds the other's trust? {#spouses}

**Short answer.** The Connecticut Act's transferor definition makes each spouse who directly or indirectly makes or causes a transfer the transferor of that property, so each spouse's contributions to a trust would be tested as that spouse's own dispositions. [^ct-487k-transferor-direct-or-indirect] The Act does not address joint trusts for spouses.
Each disposition has its own limitation period. [^ct-487p-limitation-periods-each-disposition] When several dispositions are made under one trust instrument, a later disposition does not revive a claim already barred as to an earlier one, and any distribution is treated as coming from the latest disposition. [^ct-487p-multiple-dispositions-same-instrument] Read with that definition, in a single joint trust each spouse would usually be both a transferor and a beneficiary of the other spouse's contributions. *In re Brooks* shows the risk of that mixing: the court treated trusts funded by one spouse, with property the other spouse had transferred to the funding spouse under a shared plan, as self-settled by the spouse who supplied the property. [^brooks-wife-funded-trusts-plan-spouses] The court also relied on the Restatement rule that a trust is self-settled when the beneficiary paid the purchase price for the transfer into trust. [^brooks-purchase-price-settlor-rule] This suggests that separate trusts, each funded only with the funding spouse's own property, keep the transferor of each asset easier to establish. [^ct-487k-transferor-direct-or-indirect]
When one spouse's will leaves property to the trustee of the other spouse's qualified trust, a literal reading makes the deceased spouse the transferor of that property: the deceased spouse owned it and, through the will and the executor's transfer, indirectly caused the disposition, which the definition covers. The Act does not mention wills or executors, and no Connecticut decision testing this reading was found in our review. [^ct-487k-transferor-direct-or-indirect] If so, the surviving spouse's interest in that share is a third-party interest, which general Connecticut spendthrift law protects. *Netter v. Netter* restated that a spendthrift trust is one that creates a fund for another and places it beyond the beneficiary's creditors. [^netter-spendthrift-trust-definition] A share that simply requires income to be paid to the surviving spouse, with no accumulation, withholding or support terms, leaves that income reachable by all of the survivor's creditors under § 52-321(a). [^ct-52-321-mandatory-income-reachable]
That treatment could change if the survivor gave something for the bequest, such as a waiver of statutory share rights, reciprocal wills or a contract to make a will. Under the purchase-price rule *Brooks* applied, the survivor could then be treated as the settlor of that share, making it self-settled. [^brooks-purchase-price-settlor-rule] No Connecticut decision on this point was found in our review, and Connecticut's statutory share provisions were not reviewed for this guide.

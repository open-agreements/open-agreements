# School Policy Compliance in New Jersey: Discrimination Law and Religious Employers[^about]

How New Jersey and federal discrimination law applies to an independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school: which laws cover it, whether an EEO policy is required, the Law Against Discrimination's religious-association and religious-tenets provisions, the ministerial exception, off-duty conduct rules and student admissions.

## Which federal and New Jersey discrimination laws cover a private school, and at what employee count? {#discrimination-law-coverage}

**Short answer.** The New Jersey Law Against Discrimination defines an employer to include all persons, with no minimum number of employees, while Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act reach employers with fifteen or more employees and the ADEA reaches employers with twenty or more. [^lad-employer-all-persons] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen] [^usc-12111-ada-employer] [^usc-2000gg-pwfa-employer] [^usc-630-adea-employer]

As amended by P.L.2025, c.307, approved January 20, 2026, the Law Against Discrimination's employment provision protects race, creed, color, national origin, ancestry, age, marital status, civil union status, domestic partnership status, affectional or sexual orientation, genetic information, pregnancy or breastfeeding, sex, gender identity or expression, disability, atypical hereditary cellular or blood trait, service or liability for service in the Armed Forces, and nationality. [^lad-12a-protected-classes] [^pl-2025-c307-approved] The same subsection makes it an unlawful employment practice to require employees or prospective employees to consent to a shortened statute of limitations or to waive any of the Law Against Discrimination's protections. [^lad-12a-no-waiver] Each federal definition is limited to a person engaged in an industry affecting commerce and carries its own exclusions, and the federal headcount counts employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen] [^usc-12111-ada-employer] [^usc-630-adea-employer]

The Law Against Discrimination's provision on hiring co-religionists is narrower than Title VII's, as explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission).

## Does New Jersey or federal law require an independent school to adopt a written equal employment opportunity policy? {#eeo-policy-required}

**Short answer.** Neither Title VII nor the New Jersey Law Against Discrimination requires a private school to adopt a general written equal employment opportunity policy. Title VII instead requires a covered employer to post the EEOC's notice, [^usc-2000e-10-posting-eeo] and the Division on Civil Rights requires every employer covered by the Law Against Discrimination to display the Division's official employment poster and to give each employee a written copy every year. [^njac-13-8-1-2-employment-poster]

The Division's rule requires the written copy annually, on or before December 31, and on an employee's first request, and it may be delivered by email, as printed material such as an attachment to an employee manual, or through an internet or intranet site open to all employees with notice of the posting. [^njac-13-8-1-2-employment-poster] The Law Against Discrimination separately bars an employer from circulating any statement, advertisement or application form, or making any pre-employment inquiry, that expresses a limitation as to a characteristic listed in subsection (c) unless it rests on a bona fide occupational qualification. [^lad-12c-advertisements] The Division's employment-advertising rule states that the phrases "Equal Opportunity Employer"[^njac-13-11-1-1-eeo-language] and "All qualified applicants encouraged to apply"[^njac-13-11-1-1-eeo-language] do not violate the Act, provided the advertisement does not otherwise exclude qualified applicants. [^njac-13-11-1-1-eeo-language]

Under Title VII and the Law Against Discrimination, an equal employment opportunity statement in a school's handbook or job postings therefore describes obligations the school already has rather than a separately mandated document. Federal contractor rules differ: a covered contractor's affirmative action program under 41 C.F.R. § 60-741.44(a) must include an equal opportunity policy statement, which the contractor must also post. [^cfr-60-741-44-policy-statement] A religious school's statement can also describe the religious-affiliation qualification and religious-tenets criteria that N.J.S.A. 10:5-12(a) permits a religious association or organization to use, as explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^lad-12a-religious-provisions-eeo]

## May a religious school in New Jersey prefer co-religionists or require employees to follow its religious tenets? {#religious-preference-and-mission}

**Short answer.** New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination lets a religious association or organization use religious affiliation as a uniform qualification only for clergy, religious teachers and other employees engaged in its religious activities, and it also lets the organization follow its religion's tenets in setting criteria for employment of an employee. [^lad-12a-religious-provisions]

The affiliation clause and the tenets clause reach different groups of employees. The affiliation clause is limited to clergy, religious teachers and other employees engaged in the association's religious activities, and the word "uniform"[^lad-12a-religious-provisions] requires the qualification to be applied consistently; the tenets clause speaks of "criteria for employment of an employee"[^lad-12a-religious-provisions] without that limit. [^lad-12a-religious-provisions] The Supreme Court of New Jersey held in *Crisitello v. St. Theresa School* that the tenets clause is an affirmative defense, which the employer must plead and prove and on which it bears the burden of production and persuasion. [^crisitello-affirmative-defense] To prove it, the employer must show that the challenged decision relied solely on employment criteria adopted under its religious tenets. [^crisitello-relied-solely] Where the record leaves a genuine factual question whether the employer relied exclusively on a violation of a religious tenet, the case is not ripe for summary judgment, and deciding whether the employer acted on criteria it had established in following its religious tenets is within a court's purview and requires only neutral principles of law. [^crisitello-neutral-principles] Section 10:5-12(a) uses the phrase "religious association or organization"[^lad-12a-religious-provisions] without defining it, and an employer relying on either clause must qualify as one. [^lad-12a-religious-provisions]

Federal law is broader on co-religionist hiring. Section 702 of Title VII exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities, with no limit to religious roles, and § 703(e)(2) separately permits a school that is, in whole or in substantial part, owned, supported, controlled or managed by a particular religion or by a particular religious corporation, association or society, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a particular religion, to hire and employ employees of that religion. [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer] [^usc-2000e-2-e-2-religious-school] The ADA likewise does not prohibit a religious educational institution from preferring individuals of a particular religion, and it lets a religious organization require all applicants and employees to conform to its religious tenets. [^ada-12113-d-religious-entities] The EEOC's Compliance Manual states that the § 702 exemption applies only to organizations whose purpose and character are primarily religious, judged on all the facts, [^eeoc-religious-organization-test] and that religious organizations remain subject to Title VII's prohibitions on race, color, sex and national origin discrimination and to the ADEA and ADA. [^eeoc-religious-employers-other-bases]

Because a New Jersey school answers to both statutes, a co-religionist requirement for a non-religious role that Title VII permits can still face a Law Against Discrimination claim unless the requirement fits the tenets clause. [^lad-12a-religious-provisions] [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer] The conservative course is to confine a religious-affiliation requirement to clergy, religious teachers and staff engaged in the school's religious activities, to ground any conduct or belief standard for other staff in identified religious tenets, and to apply each standard uniformly.

## Which religious school teachers and staff fall within the ministerial exception to discrimination claims? {#ministerial-exception-teachers}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception bars employment discrimination claims challenging a religious school's decisions about employees who perform important religious functions, such as educating students in the faith, and whether an employee qualifies turns on what the employee does. [^olg-what-employee-does] [^hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized]

In *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, the Supreme Court recognized the exception, reasoning that requiring a church to accept or retain an unwanted minister intrudes on more than an employment decision. [^hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized] The teacher in that case taught a religion class four days a week, led her students in prayer and devotional exercises each day, and attended a weekly school-wide chapel service, which she led herself about twice a year. [^hosanna-tabor-facts-duties] The grounds the school gave for her termination included insubordination and disruptive behavior and the damage she had done to her working relationship with the school by threatening to take legal action. [^hosanna-tabor-facts-termination] The Court balanced society's interest in enforcing discrimination statutes against a religious group's interest in choosing who will teach its faith. [^hosanna-tabor-interest-balance] *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru* involved two elementary school teachers at Catholic schools whose teaching responsibilities were similar to those of the teacher in the earlier case, and the Court held that their claims fell within the same rule even though they did not have the title of minister and had less religious training. [^olg-facts-teachers] The Court said there that educating young people in their faith lies at the core of a private religious school's mission. [^olg-what-employee-does] The EEOC's Compliance Manual describes the exception as reaching lay employees and even employees who are not co-religionists. [^eeoc-ministerial-lay-employees]

New Jersey courts apply the exception to claims by school employees who are ministers. *Hyman v. Rosenbaum Yeshiva of North Jersey* involved a rabbi hired in 1988 as a Judaic Studies teacher at an Orthodox Jewish school, where his role as a rebbe, a rabbi who is an elementary school teacher, reflected his training as a rabbi. [^hyman-appdiv-facts-rebbe] [^hyman-nj-facts-letter] In 2019, after investigating claims of misconduct and consulting legal counsel and authorities on Jewish law, the school terminated his employment, and the Head of School told parents and faculty members in a letter that the school did so because it deemed his conduct unacceptable and inconsistent with how a rebbe at the school should interact with students. [^hyman-nj-facts-letter] The Appellate Division held that the exception barred his defamation claims because he conceded that he was a minister and the claims were connected to the school's decision to terminate him. [^hyman-appdiv-conceded-minister] The school emailed the letter two days after the termination, and the court found that the letter related to the employment decision and the school's reasons for it. [^hyman-appdiv-letter-timing] In 2024 the Supreme Court of New Jersey unanimously readopted its 2002 ministerial-exception standard with two refinements to conform it to recent United States Supreme Court decisions, and because the justices were equally divided on whether discovery was required, the Appellate Division's judgment was affirmed. [^hyman-nj-mckelvey-readopted] In *Crisitello v. St. Theresa School*, the Supreme Court of New Jersey decided the case on the Law Against Discrimination's religious tenets exception and declined to rule on the First Amendment arguments, so it did not decide whether the employee was a minister. [^crisitello-no-constitutional-ruling]

Because the test asks what the employee does, a teacher whose duties include teaching the faith is closer to the exception than an employee whose role has no religious function. [^olg-what-employee-does] For a role without religious duties, the statutory provisions described in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission) are the school's basis for religious employment criteria. [^lad-12a-religious-provisions-minister]

## Can a New Jersey religious school discipline or dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that conflicts with its religious teachings? {#off-duty-conduct-religious-school}

**Short answer.** Under the Law Against Discrimination, a New Jersey religious school may dismiss an employee for violating a religious-tenets criterion made a condition of employment if the decision relied solely on that criterion, and in *Crisitello* the state Supreme Court held that the defense precluded pregnancy and marital-status claims. [^crisitello-relied-solely-conduct] [^crisitello-claims-pregnancy-marital] [^crisitello-tenets-precludes-recovery]

The school in *Crisitello* was a Roman Catholic elementary school operating within the Archdiocese of Newark. [^crisitello-facts-school] In 2014, when the principal raised the possibility of the employee teaching art full time, the employee, who was unmarried, said that she was pregnant; a few weeks later the principal told her that she had violated the Code of Ethics by engaging in premarital sex and could not remain on staff, and she was terminated. [^crisitello-facts-termination] Her claims alleged employment discrimination based on pregnancy and marital status. [^crisitello-claims-pregnancy-marital] The Court found that she had acknowledged that the school required her, as a condition of employment, to abide by the tenets of the Catholic faith, including that she abstain from premarital sex, and that she knowingly violated Catholic law. [^crisitello-facts-tenet-acknowledged] It found no evidence of discrimination based on pregnancy or marital status: the principal learned of the pregnancy only when the employee disclosed it while negotiating her salary, the school did not terminate pregnant teachers who were married, and it employed both married and unmarried teachers. [^crisitello-facts-no-pretext] On those facts, the Court held that the employer had validly asserted the tenets exception as an affirmative defense and that the employee raised no genuine dispute of material fact about it, so the employer was entitled to summary judgment. [^crisitello-validly-asserted] With no evidence that the stated reason for the termination was false, the tenets exception precluded recovery, and the Court did not reach the constitutional questions. [^crisitello-tenets-precludes-recovery] The defense therefore depends on proof that the decision relied solely on criteria adopted under the school's religious tenets. [^crisitello-relied-solely-conduct]

For staff outside the ministerial exception, federal courts have taken different routes on Title VII sex-discrimination claims against religious employers. In *Curay-Cramer v. Ursuline Academy*, a teacher at a private Catholic school was fired after she signed her name to a pro-choice advertisement in the local newspaper, and she alleged that she was fired for conduct less egregious under Catholic doctrine than conduct of male employees who were treated less harshly. [^curay-cramer-facts] The Third Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in New Jersey, held that her Title VII count alleging that she was fired because she is a woman and that male employees were treated less harshly for comparable conduct failed, because resolving it would require comparing the relative severity of violations of religious doctrine and Congress had not clearly expressed an intention to apply Title VII to such a claim against a religious employer. [^curay-cramer-count-three-allegation] [^curay-cramer-count-three] [^curay-cramer-holding] The same opinion recalled the Third Circuit's earlier conclusion that applying Title VII to a religious-discrimination claim by an employee terminated from a Catholic school for marrying in violation of canon law would raise serious constitutional questions. [^curay-cramer-little] The Fourth Circuit held in October 2026 that Title VII's § 702 exemption does not cover a religiously motivated decision challenged as sex, race or national origin discrimination, a decision that does not bind courts in New Jersey. [^doe-crs-702-not-sex]

Separate statutes protect particular off-duty conduct. New Jersey's cannabis statute bars an employer from refusing to hire or discharging a person, or taking adverse action, because the person does or does not use cannabis items, subject to the drug-testing and workplace provisions in the same section. [^creamma-24-6i-52-adverse-action] For a conduct rule applied to staff outside the ministerial exception, the conservative course is to tie the rule to a stated religious tenet, make it a written condition of employment, apply it uniformly, and treat a decision that cannot be traced solely to that tenet as subject to the ordinary New Jersey and federal discrimination laws.

## Does New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination reach a private school's admissions and treatment of students? {#student-admissions-public-accommodation}

**Short answer.** The Law Against Discrimination's public accommodation provisions list kindergartens, primary and secondary schools and academies as places of public accommodation, but that definition does not apply to any educational facility operated or maintained by a bona fide religious or sectarian institution. [^lad-5l-schools-and-religious-proviso]

For a school within the definition, the Act bars refusing, withholding or denying any of its accommodations, advantages, facilities or privileges, or discriminating in furnishing them, on account of race, creed, color, national origin, ancestry, marital status, civil union status, domestic partnership status, pregnancy or breastfeeding, sex, gender identity or expression, affectional or sexual orientation, disability, service or liability for service in the Armed Forces, or nationality. [^lad-12f-public-accommodation] The same definition preserves admissions discretion for a private secondary school, which may use in good faith criteria other than race, creed, color, national origin, ancestry, gender identity or expression, or affectional or sexual orientation in admitting students. [^lad-5l-schools-and-religious-proviso] A place of public accommodation that is in its nature reasonably restricted exclusively to individuals of one sex, including a school or educational institution restricted exclusively to one sex, may refuse admission to individuals of the opposite sex on the basis of sex, provided that individuals are admitted based on their gender identity or expression. [^lad-12f-single-sex]

The definition also excludes any institution "which is in its nature distinctly private"[^lad-5l-schools-and-religious-proviso], and the statute does not say when a school falls within that phrase; because the definition names kindergartens, primary and secondary schools and academies, a secular independent school is most safely treated as covered. [^lad-5l-schools-and-religious-proviso] Places of public accommodation must display the Division on Civil Rights' official public accommodation poster where it is easily visible to persons seeking or using the accommodations. [^njac-13-8-1-4-public-accommodation-poster] The religious-institution proviso concerns the public accommodation provisions; it does not displace the employment rules for the same school's staff described in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^lad-12a-religious-provisions-admissions]

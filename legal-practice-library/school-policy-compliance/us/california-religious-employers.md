# School Policy Compliance in California: Discrimination Law and Religious Employers[^about]

How California and federal discrimination law applies to an independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school: which laws cover it, when a religious school falls outside the Fair Employment and Housing Act, the required written harassment policy, hiring co-religionists, the ministerial exception, off-duty conduct and student admissions.

## Which federal and California discrimination laws cover a private school, and at what employee count? {#discrimination-law-coverage}

**Short answer.** California's Fair Employment and Housing Act covers a private school that regularly employs five or more persons unless it is a religious association or corporation not organized for private profit, while Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act need fifteen employees and the ADEA twenty. [^ca-re-feha-employer] [^ca-re-usc-2000e-employer] [^ca-re-usc-12111-employer] [^ca-re-usc-2000gg-employer] [^ca-re-usc-630-employer]

For a covered employer, the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) bars discrimination in hiring, discharge, compensation and terms of employment because of race, religious creed, color, national origin, ancestry, physical or mental disability, reproductive health decisionmaking, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sex, gender, gender identity, gender expression, age, sexual orientation, or veteran or military status. [^ca-re-12940-a] The Civil Rights Council's regulation counts part-time employees the same as full-time employees and counts employees on paid or unpaid leave toward the five. [^ca-re-ccr-11008-counting] Each federal definition is limited to a person engaged in an industry affecting commerce and counts employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^ca-re-usc-2000e-employer] [^ca-re-usc-630-employer]

Several California statutes define the term employer for themselves, and those definitions set different thresholds; some repeat FEHA's religious exclusion and some do not:

| Law | Who is covered | Religious nonprofit school |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FEHA discrimination provisions, Gov. Code § 12926(d) | Any person regularly employing five or more persons [^ca-re-feha-employer] | Excluded, subject to the public benefit corporation rule in Gov. Code § 12926.2(f) [^ca-re-feha-employer] [^ca-re-12926-2-f] |
| FEHA harassment ban, Gov. Code § 12940(j) | Any person regularly employing one or more persons or receiving the services of one or more contractors [^ca-re-12940-j4] | Excluded by the subdivision's own text, except as provided in Gov. Code § 12926.2 [^ca-re-12940-j4] |
| Harassment training, Gov. Code § 12950.1 | Any person regularly employing five or more persons or receiving the services of five or more contractors [^ca-re-12950-1-h] | The section's own definition has no religious exclusion [^ca-re-12950-1-h] |
| California Family Rights Act, Gov. Code § 12945.2 | Any person who directly employs five or more persons [^ca-re-cfra-employer] | The section's own definition has no religious exclusion [^ca-re-cfra-employer] |
| Paid sick leave, Lab. Code § 245.5 | Any person employing another under any appointment or contract of hire [^ca-re-psl-employer] | The article's definition has no religious exclusion [^ca-re-psl-employer] |
| Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act | Fifteen or more employees [^ca-re-usc-2000e-employer] [^ca-re-usc-12111-employer] [^ca-re-usc-2000gg-employer] | Covered, with a religious-preference exemption in Title VII and the ADA [^ca-re-usc-2000e-1] [^ca-re-ada-12113-d] |
| ADEA | Twenty or more employees [^ca-re-usc-630-employer] | Covered [^ca-re-eeoc-other-bases-coverage] |

The harassment ban therefore reaches a secular school with a single employee. [^ca-re-12940-j4] Whether a religious school falls outside FEHA's discrimination and harassment provisions turns on how the school is organized, as explained in the [question on the religious-employer exemption](#religious-employer-feha-exemption). [^ca-re-feha-employer] [^ca-re-12926-2-f]

## Is a religious school in California exempt from the Fair Employment and Housing Act? {#religious-employer-feha-exemption}

**Short answer.** A California religious school run by a nonprofit religious association or corporation is not a FEHA employer, but a religiously affiliated nonprofit public benefit corporation that operates a school as its sole or primary activity is subject to FEHA apart from a right to hire co-religionists. [^ca-re-feha-employer-exemption] [^ca-re-12926-2-f-exemption]

The exclusion turns on the employer rather than the job. The Supreme Court of California held in *Kelly v. Methodist Hospital of Southern California* that the exemption renders qualified religious entities exempt from FEHA in its entirety and that the nature of the employee's work is not relevant. [^ca-re-kelly-entirety] In *Silo v. CHW Medical Foundation*, the same court restated its earlier holding that an organization need not be incorporated under the Nonprofit Religious Corporation Law and need only be religious and not organized for private profit to qualify. [^ca-re-silo-mckeon] The Civil Rights Council's regulation presumes that a nonprofit religious organization exempt from federal and state income tax as such is not an employer, but treats any portion of it that is taxed as an unrelated business and regularly employs five or more individuals as an employer. [^ca-re-ccr-11008-religious]

Section 12926.2(f) sets a narrower rule for schools organized as public benefit corporations. A nonprofit public benefit corporation formed by, or affiliated with, a particular religion that operates an educational institution as its sole or primary activity may restrict employment, including promotion, in any or all employment categories to individuals of a particular religion, but it is subject to FEHA in all other respects, including its prohibitions against discrimination. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-exemption] The same section defines a religious corporation as one formed under, or otherwise subject to, Part 4 or Part 6 of Division 2 of Title 1 of the Corporations Code, which are the provisions beginning at Corporations Code §§ 9110 and 10000. [^ca-re-12926-2-a]

Courts applying these provisions to schools have looked at the school's legal structure. In *Henry v. Red Hill Evangelical Lutheran Church of Tustin*, the school was part of a church incorporated in 1957 for nonprofit religious purposes and did not exist as a separate legal entity, [^ca-re-henry-facts] and the Court of Appeal held that the church was not a FEHA employer and was not subject to FEHA's prohibitions. [^ca-re-henry-not-employer] The court rejected the teacher's reliance on § 12926.2(f) because she pointed to no evidence that the school was a nonprofit public benefit corporation. [^ca-re-henry-not-pbc] In *Bohnert v. Roman Catholic Archbishop of San Francisco*, a federal district court described nonprofit religious corporations as enjoying broad immunity from FEHA and religious public benefit educational corporations as enjoying only the more limited right to restrict employment to members of a particular religion. [^ca-re-bohnert-categories] The court held that if the high school was a single employer with the other defendants, it shared their exemption as a nonprofit religious corporation. [^ca-re-bohnert-single-employer]

A school operated as a division of a church or other nonprofit religious corporation, or by another nonprofit organization of sufficiently religious character, therefore stands outside FEHA's discrimination provisions, while a separately incorporated public benefit corporation that runs a religious school is a FEHA employer except for religious hiring preferences. [^ca-re-henry-not-employer] [^ca-re-silo-mckeon] [^ca-re-12926-2-f-exemption] The exemption reaches FEHA's retaliation ban too: in *Mathews v. Happy Valley Conference Center, Inc.*, the Court of Appeal held that religious nonprofit employers were exempt from FEHA liability for retaliation and rejected the argument that the word person in § 12940(h) overrides the exemption. [^ca-re-mathews-retaliation] [^ca-re-mathews-person] The harassment subdivision reaches the same result by its own text: it defines employer for that subdivision as any person regularly employing one or more persons, but provides that the term does not include a religious association or corporation not organized for private profit, except as provided in § 12926.2. [^ca-re-12940-j4-exemption] The claims in *Bohnert* were for harassment and failure to prevent harassment, [^ca-re-bohnert-harassment-claims] and the court treated the nonprofit religious corporations as exempt from FEHA. [^ca-re-bohnert-categories]

## Does California law require a private school to have a written harassment and discrimination policy? {#harassment-policy-required}

**Short answer.** A California private school that is a FEHA employer must develop and distribute a written harassment, discrimination and retaliation prevention policy under the Civil Rights Council's regulation, while Title VII's posting provision requires a covered employer to post the EEOC's notice. [^ca-re-ccr-11023-b] [^ca-re-usc-2000e-10]

The policy must be in writing, list all current protected categories under FEHA, and state that coworkers and third parties, as well as supervisors and managers, are barred from prohibited conduct. [^ca-re-ccr-11023-b] It must also create a complaint process with confidentiality to the extent possible, timely response, impartial investigation by qualified personnel, documentation, remedial options and timely closure; offer a way to complain other than to the employee's immediate supervisor; instruct supervisors to report complaints; commit to a fair, timely and thorough investigation and to remedial measures where misconduct is found; avoid promising complete confidentiality; bar retaliation; and link to the Civil Rights Department's online training courses. [^ca-re-ccr-11023-b4] The regulation lists acceptable ways to distribute the policy, including a signed acknowledgment, email with an acknowledgment form, an intranet with a tracking system, or discussion at hiring, [^ca-re-ccr-11023-c] and it requires translation into every language spoken by at least 10 percent of the workforce at a facility. [^ca-re-ccr-11023-e]

The policy sits beside other FEHA obligations. Section 12950 requires every employer to post the Department's discrimination poster and to distribute its sexual harassment information sheet or equivalent information, [^ca-re-12950-poster] [^ca-re-12950-info-sheet] and the regulation requires the Department's transgender rights poster. [^ca-re-ccr-11023-d] Section 12950.1 requires an employer with five or more employees to give supervisors two hours and other employees one hour of interactive sexual harassment training, repeated every two years and given to new employees within six months. [^ca-re-12950-1-a] [^ca-re-12950-1-thereafter] Beginning January 1, 2028, AB 1803 (Stats. 2026, ch. 547) adds anti-hate speech training, on recognizing, reporting and confronting workplace speech that vilifies, humiliates or incites hatred based on the characteristics protected by § 12940(a), as a required component of that training. [^ca-re-ab-1803-anti-hate-speech] The regulation also states that there is no stand-alone private claim for failing to prevent discrimination under § 12940(k): a claimant must also prove the underlying discrimination, harassment or retaliation. [^ca-re-ccr-11023-a2]

The policy regulation applies to FEHA employers, and the Council's regulations state that a nonprofit religious association or corporation is not an employer under the Act. [^ca-re-ccr-11008-religious-policy] The training statute instead defines employer for itself, by reference to five or more employees or contractors and without a religious exclusion. [^ca-re-12950-1-h-policy] On the statutory text, a religious nonprofit school with five or more employees remains subject to the training requirement even though it is outside the policy regulation. [^ca-re-12950-1-h-policy] [^ca-re-ccr-11008-religious-policy] Federal contractor rules differ again: a covered contractor's affirmative action program under 41 C.F.R. § 60-741.44(a) must include an equal opportunity policy statement, which the contractor must post. [^ca-re-cfr-60-741-44]

## May a religious school in California prefer co-religionists or require employees to follow its religious tenets? {#religious-preference-and-mission}

**Short answer.** California lets a religiously affiliated nonprofit public benefit corporation that operates a school as its sole or primary activity restrict employment in any or all job categories to members of a particular religion, and a school run by a religious nonprofit association or corporation is outside FEHA's employer definition. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-preference] [^ca-re-feha-employer-preference]

For a school organized as a religiously affiliated public benefit corporation, the permission is limited to restricting employment to individuals of a particular religion, and the corporation remains subject to FEHA in all other respects, including its prohibitions on discrimination. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-preference] Those prohibitions include marital status, sex, gender identity and sexual orientation. [^ca-re-12940-a-preference] Section 12926.2(f) speaks of individuals of a particular religion and does not mention religious tenets or conduct standards, and in our review of California appellate decisions on CourtListener we found none deciding whether the provision lets such a school enforce a religious conduct rule against a claim of marital-status or sexual-orientation discrimination. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-preference] A school of that kind that applies a tenets-based conduct standard to staff therefore faces the ordinary FEHA prohibitions on any ground other than religion. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-preference] [^ca-re-12940-a-preference]

Federal law is broader. Section 702 of Title VII exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities, and § 703(e)(2) separately permits a school owned, supported, controlled or managed by a particular religion, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a particular religion, to hire and employ employees of that religion. [^ca-re-usc-2000e-1-preference] [^ca-re-usc-2000e-2-e-2] The ADA lets a religious organization require all applicants and employees to conform to its religious tenets. [^ca-re-ada-12113-d-preference] The Ninth Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in California, held in *Garcia v. Salvation Army* that because the exemption permits religious organizations to discriminate based on religion, retaliation claims based on religious discrimination fail against those organizations. [^ca-re-garcia-roe] The EEOC's Compliance Manual states that the exemption applies only to organizations whose purpose and character are primarily religious, judged on all the facts, [^ca-re-eeoc-org-test] and that religious organizations remain subject to Title VII's prohibitions on race, color, sex and national origin discrimination and to the ADEA and ADA. [^ca-re-eeoc-other-bases]

## Which religious school teachers and staff fall within the ministerial exception to discrimination claims? {#ministerial-exception-teachers}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception keeps courts from enforcing discrimination claims against a religious school by employees whose work includes important religious functions such as teaching the faith, and the Supreme Court applied it to teachers at Roman Catholic elementary schools in the Archdiocese of Los Angeles. [^ca-re-hosanna-recognized] [^ca-re-olg-what-employee-does] [^ca-re-olg-facts]

In *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, the Supreme Court recognized the exception, reasoning that requiring a church to accept or retain an unwanted minister intrudes on more than an employment decision. [^ca-re-hosanna-recognized] In *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru*, which arose from a Roman Catholic primary school in the Archdiocese of Los Angeles, the Court said that what matters is what an employee does and that educating young people in their faith lies at the core of a private religious school's mission. [^ca-re-olg-facts] [^ca-re-olg-what-employee-does] The EEOC's Compliance Manual describes the exception as reaching lay employees and even employees who are not co-religionists. [^ca-re-eeoc-ministerial-lay]

California courts apply the exception to state-law claims. In *Henry v. Red Hill Evangelical Lutheran Church of Tustin*, a preschool teacher taught religion as part of the regular curriculum and spoke to the children about Jesus daily, [^ca-re-henry-ministerial-facts] and the Court of Appeal held that leading the children in prayer and using Bible stories in discipline were ministerial functions. [^ca-re-henry-ministerial-functions] Because she taught religion, led daily prayers and led chapel services, the court held that the exception applied to her claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. [^ca-re-henry-ministerial-applies] [^ca-re-henry-holding-minister]

Because the test asks what the employee does, a teacher whose duties include teaching the faith is closer to the exception than an employee whose role has no religious function. [^ca-re-olg-what-employee-does] For a role without religious duties, the school's organization and religious character and the statutory exemptions described in the [question on the religious-employer exemption](#religious-employer-feha-exemption) govern whether FEHA applies. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-minister]

## Can a California religious school discipline or dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that conflicts with its religious teachings? {#off-duty-conduct-religious-school}

**Short answer.** A California religious school outside FEHA may dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that violates its religious teachings without FEHA liability, as the Court of Appeal held in *Henry v. Red Hill Evangelical Lutheran Church of Tustin* for a preschool teacher who lived with her boyfriend while unmarried. [^ca-re-henry-holding-conduct] [^ca-re-henry-title-vii]

The court in *Henry* found that the teacher was dismissed based upon a matter of religion, not her sex and not her having had a baby outside marriage, [^ca-re-henry-religious-reason] and held that the judgment did not violate any public policy rooted in Title VII. [^ca-re-henry-title-vii] It also held her public-policy wrongful-termination claim barred by the ministerial exception. [^ca-re-henry-holding-conduct] In *Silo v. CHW Medical Foundation*, the Supreme Court of California held that there is no tort liability for a FEHA-exempt religious organization's dismissal of an employee for workplace religious speech it considers inappropriate. [^ca-re-silo-holding]

A school organized as a religiously affiliated public benefit corporation stands differently, because § 12926.2(f) subjects it to FEHA's prohibitions apart from religious hiring preferences, [^ca-re-12926-2-f-conduct] and those prohibitions include marital status and sex. [^ca-re-12940-a-conduct] Federal law also limits the religious exemption. The Ninth Circuit held in *EEOC v. Fremont Christian School*, a case about a church-owned school in Fremont, California, that Title VII's religious exemption reaches religious institutions only to a narrow extent and that the school was liable for a sex-discriminatory health insurance benefit. [^ca-re-fremont-facts] [^ca-re-fremont-narrow] [^ca-re-fremont-liable]

California's lawful off-duty conduct statutes add a narrower layer. Labor Code § 96(k) lets the Labor Commissioner take claims for lost wages from demotion, suspension or discharge for lawful conduct during nonworking hours away from the employer's premises, [^ca-re-lab-96-k] and § 98.6 permits an employer to require a contract protecting it against conduct actually in direct conflict with its essential enterprise-related interests. [^ca-re-lab-98-6-c2] The new actions and remedies that § 98.6 made effective on January 1, 2002 do not apply to a religious association or corporation described in Gov. Code § 12926(d), except as § 12926.2 provides. [^ca-re-lab-98-6-d] In 2004, the Court of Appeal in *Grinzi v. San Diego Hospice Corp.* concluded that neither § 96(k) nor § 98.6 supports a public policy against a private employer's dismissal for lawful off-duty conduct that the Labor Code does not otherwise protect. [^ca-re-grinzi]

For a conduct rule applied to staff outside the ministerial exception, the school's organization and religious character decide whether FEHA's marital-status and sex protections apply at all, and a school organized as a religiously affiliated public benefit corporation faces FEHA on any ground other than religion. [^ca-re-12926-2-f-conduct] [^ca-re-12940-a-conduct]

## Does California's civil rights law reach a private school's admissions and discipline of students? {#student-admissions-unruh}

**Short answer.** The Court of Appeal held in *Doe v. California Lutheran High School Association* that a private religious high school was not a business establishment subject to the Unruh Civil Rights Act, so the Act did not reach its expulsion of two students under its Christian conduct rule. [^ca-re-doe-clhs-holding] [^ca-re-doe-clhs-facts]

The Unruh Civil Rights Act guarantees full and equal accommodations, advantages, facilities, privileges and services in all business establishments regardless of sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language or immigration status. [^ca-re-civ-51-b] The court in *Doe* reasoned that a school's nonprofit status is not controlling, but that a nonprofit school should not be deemed a business unless it significantly resembles an ordinary for-profit business. [^ca-re-doe-clhs-nonprofit] The decision concerned a religious school whose admissions were tied to its religious values, and a secular independent school relying on it would have to show the same lack of resemblance to an ordinary for-profit business. [^ca-re-doe-clhs-nonprofit]

The Education Code supplies a separate rule tied to state funding. Section 220 bars discrimination on the basis of disability, gender, gender identity, gender expression, nationality, race or ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, or other hate-crime characteristics, including immigration status, in any program or activity of an educational institution that receives or benefits from state financial assistance or enrolls pupils who receive state student financial aid. [^ca-re-ed-220] The article defines an educational institution to include a private preschool, elementary or secondary school, [^ca-re-ed-210-3] so a private school is within § 220 only through state financial assistance or state student aid. [^ca-re-ed-220] The article does not apply to an educational institution controlled by a religious organization if its application would be inconsistent with that organization's religious tenets. [^ca-re-ed-221]

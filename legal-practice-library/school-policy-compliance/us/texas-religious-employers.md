# School Policy Compliance in Texas: Discrimination Law and Religious Employers[^about]

How Texas and federal discrimination law applies to an independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school: which laws cover it, whether an EEO policy is required, sexual harassment liability for small schools, the Labor Code's religious-organization provision, the ministerial exception, off-duty conduct, student admissions and the education savings account program's autonomy protections.

## Which federal and Texas discrimination laws cover a private school, and at what employee count? {#discrimination-law-coverage}

**Short answer.** Chapter 21 of the Texas Labor Code, Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act reach a private employer with fifteen or more employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks of the current or preceding year, and the ADEA requires twenty. [^tx-re-tchra-employer-fifteen] [^tx-re-usc-2000e-employer-fifteen] [^tx-re-usc-12111-ada-employer] [^tx-re-usc-2000gg-pwfa-employer] [^tx-re-usc-630-adea-employer]

Chapter 21's definition reaches a private school as an employer only through paragraph (A), with paragraph (B) adding that employer's agents; paragraph (A) counts employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year; paragraph (D) separately covers a county, municipality, state agency or state instrumentality regardless of the number of individuals employed. [^tx-re-tchra-employer-fifteen] A covered employer commits an unlawful employment practice if, because of race, color, disability, religion, sex, national origin or age, it fails or refuses to hire, discharges, or otherwise discriminates against an individual in compensation or the terms, conditions or privileges of employment. [^tx-re-tchra-21-051-discrimination] Age protection applies only to individuals 40 years of age or older. [^tx-re-tchra-21-101-age] Sex discrimination includes discrimination because of pregnancy, childbirth or a related medical condition. [^tx-re-tchra-21-106-pregnancy]

Since September 1, 2023, race discrimination under Chapter 21 includes discrimination because of an employee's hair texture or protective hairstyle commonly or historically associated with race, and adopting or enforcing a dress or grooming policy that discriminates against such a hair texture or hairstyle is itself an unlawful employment practice. [^tx-re-tchra-21-1095-hair] The fifteen-employee threshold does not apply to sexual harassment: Subchapter C-1 defines employer, for that subchapter only, as a person who employs one or more employees. [^tx-re-tchra-21-141-harassment-employer] Each federal definition is limited to a person engaged in an industry affecting commerce and carries its own exclusions. [^tx-re-usc-2000e-employer-fifteen] [^tx-re-usc-12111-ada-employer] [^tx-re-usc-630-adea-employer] In *Texas v. Blanche*, decided November 20, 2026, the en banc Fifth Circuit held the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act's passage unconstitutional but said its decision extends no further than affirming an injunction against enforcing the Act against the State of Texas, as the [leave and benefits guide](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/texas-employee-leave-and-benefits#pregnancy-and-lactation-accommodation) explains. [^tx-re-texas-v-blanche-relief] [^tx-re-texas-v-blanche-texas-only]

## Does Texas or federal law require an independent school to adopt a written equal employment opportunity policy? {#eeo-policy-required}

**Short answer.** Neither Title VII nor Chapter 21 of the Texas Labor Code requires a private school to adopt a general written equal employment opportunity policy. Title VII instead requires a covered employer to post the EEOC's notice where notices to employees and applicants are customarily posted. [^tx-re-usc-2000e-10-posting-eeo]

Chapter 21's requirement to develop and implement personnel policies that comply with the chapter applies to each state agency, [^tx-re-tchra-21-452-state-agency-policies] which Chapter 21 defines as executive-branch bodies with statewide jurisdiction, statewide judicial agencies and public institutions of higher education, not a private school. [^tx-re-tchra-21-002-14-state-agency] A search of Chapter 21's full text for posting and written-policy provisions found no other provision that requires a private employer to adopt a written policy or to post a state notice. Chapter 21 does bar an employer from printing or publishing an employment notice or advertisement that indicates a preference, limitation, specification or discrimination based on race, color, disability, religion, sex, national origin or age, except where disability, religion, sex, national origin or age is a bona fide occupational qualification. [^tx-re-tchra-21-059-advertisement]

Under Title VII and Chapter 21, an equal employment opportunity statement in a school's handbook or job postings therefore describes obligations the school already has rather than a separately mandated document. A religious school's statement can also describe the religious preference that Texas Labor Code § 21.109(a) permits, as explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-eeo] Federal contractor rules differ: a covered contractor's affirmative action program, where the contractor must maintain one, under 41 C.F.R. § 60-741.44(a) must include an equal opportunity policy statement, which the contractor must also post. [^tx-re-cfr-60-741-44-policy-statement] For sexual harassment, Chapter 21 makes the employer liable when it or its agents or supervisors knew or should have known of the conduct and failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective action, so a complaint channel that reaches someone with authority to act bears directly on that standard. [^tx-re-tchra-21-142-eeo]

## Can a Texas private school with fewer than fifteen employees be liable under state law for sexual harassment of an employee? {#sexual-harassment-small-school}

**Short answer.** A Texas private school with as few as one employee commits an unlawful employment practice if sexual harassment of an employee occurs and the school or its agents or supervisors knew or should have known of it and failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective action. [^tx-re-tchra-21-141-small-employer] [^tx-re-tchra-21-142-small-school]

Subchapter C-1, added effective September 1, 2021, defines employer for that subchapter as a person who employs one or more employees or who acts directly in the interests of an employer in relation to an employee. [^tx-re-tchra-21-141-small-employer] It defines sexual harassment as an unwelcome sexual advance, a request for a sexual favor, or other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature where submission is made a term or condition of employment, submission or rejection is used as the basis for an employment decision, or the conduct has the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with work performance or creating an intimidating, hostile or offensive working environment. [^tx-re-tchra-21-141-definition] The employer is liable when it, its agents or its supervisors knew or should have known of the conduct and failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective action. [^tx-re-tchra-21-142-small-school]

A complaint alleging sexual harassment must be filed with the Texas Workforce Commission no later than the 300th day after the harassment occurred, while other Chapter 21 complaints must be filed within 180 days. [^tx-re-tchra-21-202-limitations] Chapter 21 also makes sexual harassment of an unpaid intern an unlawful employment practice on the same knew-or-should-have-known standard, but § 21.1065 falls outside Subchapter C-1, whose one-employee definition applies only within that subchapter, so its employer is the general fifteen-employee employer of § 21.002(8). [^tx-re-tchra-21-1065-interns] [^tx-re-tchra-21-141-small-employer] [^tx-re-tchra-employer-fifteen-harassment] Section 21.109's religious-organization provision speaks only to limiting employment or preferring members of the same religion and does not mention sexual harassment. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-harassment]

## May a religious school in Texas prefer co-religionists when it hires? {#religious-preference-and-mission}

**Short answer.** Under Texas Labor Code § 21.109(a), a religious school, or a school operated, supervised or controlled in whole or substantial part by a religious corporation, association or society, does not commit an unlawful employment practice by limiting employment or giving a preference to members of the same religion. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-preference]

Subsection (a) is not limited to religious roles: it reaches the school's employment generally, so long as the school is itself a religious educational institution or is operated, supervised or controlled in whole or in substantial part by a religious body. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-preference] Subsection (b) separately provides that Subchapter B does not apply to the employment of an individual of a particular religion by a religious corporation, association or society to perform work connected with its religious activities, and Subchapter B is where Chapter 21's core prohibitions on discrimination by an employer appear. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-preference] [^tx-re-tchra-21-051-preference] The text of § 21.109 addresses only religious membership and the employment of co-religionists for religious work, and a search of Chapter 21's full text for the word tenet found no provision that refers to an employer's religious tenets. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-preference] Separately, Chapter 21 permits hiring on the basis of religion where religion is a bona fide occupational qualification reasonably necessary to the normal operation of the particular business or enterprise. [^tx-re-tchra-21-119-bfoq]

Federal law runs parallel. Section 702 of Title VII exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities, and § 703(e)(2) separately permits a school that is, in whole or in substantial part, owned, supported, controlled or managed by a particular religion or religious body, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a particular religion, to hire and employ employees of that religion. [^tx-re-usc-2000e-1-religious-employer] [^tx-re-usc-2000e-2-e-2-religious-school] The ADA likewise does not prohibit a religious educational institution from preferring individuals of a particular religion, and it lets a religious organization require all applicants and employees to conform to its religious tenets. [^tx-re-ada-12113-d-religious-entities] The EEOC's Compliance Manual states that the § 702 exemption applies only to organizations whose purpose and character are primarily religious, judged on all the facts, [^tx-re-eeoc-religious-organization-test] and that religious organizations remain subject to Title VII's prohibitions on race, color, sex and national origin discrimination and to the ADEA and ADA. [^tx-re-eeoc-religious-employers-other-bases]

A school relying on § 21.109(a) must qualify under its terms, and a school that is independent of any religious body and not itself a religious educational institution has no basis in the section for a co-religionist requirement. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-preference] The conservative course is to document the school's religious character or religious control, to apply any co-religionist requirement uniformly, and to treat conduct standards for staff as a separate question from religious membership, as described in the [question on off-duty conduct](#off-duty-conduct-religious-school).

## Which religious school teachers and staff fall within the ministerial exception to discrimination claims? {#ministerial-exception-teachers}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception bars employment discrimination claims challenging a religious school's decisions about employees who perform important religious functions, such as educating students in the faith, and whether an employee qualifies turns on what the employee does. [^tx-re-olg-what-employee-does] [^tx-re-hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized]

In *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, the Supreme Court recognized the exception, reasoning that requiring a church to accept or retain an unwanted minister intrudes on more than an employment decision. [^tx-re-hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized] The Court balanced society's interest in enforcing discrimination statutes against a religious group's interest in choosing who will teach its faith. [^tx-re-hosanna-tabor-interest-balance] In *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru*, the Court said that educating young people in their faith lies at the core of a private religious school's mission. [^tx-re-olg-what-employee-does] The EEOC's Compliance Manual describes the exception as reaching lay employees and even employees who are not co-religionists. [^tx-re-eeoc-ministerial-lay-employees]

The Fifth Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in Texas, first applied the exception after *Hosanna-Tabor* in *Cannata v. Catholic Diocese of Austin*, where a former church employee sued under the ADEA and the ADA. [^tx-re-cannata-claims] [^tx-re-cannata-affirm] [^tx-re-hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized] The court held that it was enough that he played an integral role in the celebration of Mass and, by playing the piano during services, furthered the church's mission and helped convey its message, [^tx-re-cannata-integral-role] and it concluded that he fell within the exception, which barred his suit. [^tx-re-cannata-holding]

Because the test asks what the employee does, a teacher whose duties include teaching the faith is closer to the exception than an employee whose role has no religious function. [^tx-re-olg-what-employee-does] For a role without religious duties, Texas Labor Code § 21.109 is the school's state-law basis for a religious-membership preference, as described in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-minister]

## Can a Texas religious school discipline or dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that conflicts with its religious teachings? {#off-duty-conduct-religious-school}

**Short answer.** Texas Labor Code § 21.109 covers co-religionist preferences and religious work but does not mention conduct rules, so a religious school's discipline of a non-ministerial employee for off-duty conduct remains open to challenge under Chapter 21's bars on sex, pregnancy and other discrimination. [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-conduct] [^tx-re-tchra-21-051-conduct] [^tx-re-tchra-21-106-conduct]

Chapter 21 treats discrimination because of pregnancy, childbirth or a related medical condition as sex discrimination and requires that a woman so affected be treated the same as others similar in ability or inability to work, so a conduct rule enforced through a pregnancy is measured against that standard. [^tx-re-tchra-21-106-conduct] In *Tarrant County College District v. Sims*, the Dallas Court of Appeals followed *Bostock v. Clayton County* and read Chapter 21's prohibition on discrimination because of sex as prohibiting discrimination based on an individual's status as a homosexual or transgender person. [^tx-re-sims-follow-bostock] [^tx-re-bostock-holding] Effective September 1, 2025, H.B. 229 added to the Code Construction Act a definition of sex as an individual's biological sex, either male or female, which applies to codes enacted by the 60th or a later legislature unless the statute or context requires a different definition. [^tx-re-gov-311-005-sex] [^tx-re-gov-311-002-application] Authenticated CourtListener searches for this review found no Supreme Court of Texas decision on whether Chapter 21's sex provision reaches sexual orientation or gender identity, and no Texas appellate decision addressing the effect of the 2025 definition on *Sims*.

Federal law reaches the same conduct through Title VII. In *Bostock*, the Supreme Court held that an employer who fires an individual merely for being gay or transgender violates Title VII. [^tx-re-bostock-holding] In *Braidwood Management v. EEOC*, a suit against the EEOC, the Fifth Circuit held that the Religious Freedom Restoration Act requires that Braidwood be exempted on an individual level from Title VII because compliance after *Bostock* would substantially burden its ability to operate according to its religious beliefs about homosexual and transgender conduct. [^tx-re-braidwood-rfra] [^tx-re-bostock-holding] The Fourth Circuit held in October 2026 that Title VII's § 702 exemption does not cover a religiously motivated decision challenged as sex, race or national origin discrimination, a decision that does not bind courts in Texas. [^tx-re-doe-crs-702-not-sex] Texas's own religious-freedom statute bars a government agency, meaning the state, its political subdivisions and their agencies, from substantially burdening a person's free exercise of religion unless it shows a compelling interest pursued by the least restrictive means. [^tx-re-cprc-110-003] [^tx-re-cprc-110-001-agency]

Chapter 21 also lets an employer adopt a policy prohibiting the employment of an individual who currently uses or possesses a controlled substance listed in Schedule I or II of the federal Controlled Substances Act, except use or possession of a drug taken under the supervision of a licensed health care professional or otherwise authorized by federal or state law, and the provision does not protect a policy adopted or applied with intent to discriminate. [^tx-re-tchra-21-120-controlled-substance] For a conduct rule applied to staff outside the ministerial exception, the conservative course is to tie the rule to a stated religious tenet, make it a written condition of employment, apply it uniformly to men and women, and treat a decision that turns on pregnancy, sex or another protected characteristic as subject to Chapter 21 and Title VII.

## Which discrimination laws reach a Texas private school's admissions and treatment of students? {#student-admissions-discrimination}

**Short answer.** Federal law bars a nonsectarian private school from denying admission because of race under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, and the ADA's public-accommodations title covers private schools other than exempt private clubs, religious organizations and entities religious organizations control. [^tx-re-runyon-violation] [^tx-re-usc-1981-equal-rights] [^tx-re-usc-12181-private-school] [^tx-re-usc-12187-religious-exemption]

In *Runyon v. McCrary*, the Supreme Court held that the racial exclusion practiced by two commercially operated, nonsectarian private schools was a classic violation of § 1981, [^tx-re-runyon-violation] which gives all persons the same right to make and enforce contracts as is enjoyed by white citizens. [^tx-re-usc-1981-equal-rights] The cases, as the Court framed them, concerned the racial exclusion practiced by private schools, [^tx-re-runyon-question] and the Court said they did not present a private school's right to limit its student body to boys, to girls, or to adherents of a particular religious faith, because § 1981 is in no way addressed to those categories, and did not present the application of § 1981 to a sectarian school that practices racial exclusion on religious grounds. [^tx-re-runyon-not-religion] In *Domanic v. Christian Brothers Automotive Corp.*, a Jewish applicant sued under § 1981 after a faith-based business that franchises only to Christians refused him a franchise, and he argued that discrimination against Jews is racial discrimination even when motivated purely by religion. [^tx-re-domanic-facts] [^tx-re-domanic-argument] The Fifth Circuit declined to adopt that argument as in tension with longstanding Supreme Court precedent that § 1981 does not reach religious discrimination. [^tx-re-domanic-no-religion]

Under Texas law, persons with disabilities have the same right as others to the full use and enjoyment of any public facility, and no person with a disability may be denied admittance to a public facility because of the disability. [^tx-re-hrc-121-003-public-facility] The definition of public facility includes a college dormitory or other educational facility and any other place of public accommodation to which the general public or any classification of persons from the general public is regularly, normally or customarily invited. [^tx-re-hrc-121-002-definition] A violation by any person, including a private organization, is a misdemeanor and also supports a civil action for damages with a conclusive presumption of damages of at least $300. [^tx-re-hrc-121-004-penalties] A search of the full text of Chapter 121 of the Human Resources Code found no exception for religious organizations. The statute does not say whether a religious school that admits only students who meet its criteria is a public facility, so a religious school that relies on the ADA's religious exemption may still face a Chapter 121 claim over disability-based exclusion. [^tx-re-hrc-121-002-definition] [^tx-re-usc-12187-religious-exemption]

A school that participates in Texas's education savings account program keeps statutory protection from state action that limits its admissions and enrollment practices, policies and standards, as described in the [question on education savings accounts](#education-savings-account-autonomy). [^tx-re-ed-29-368-admissions]

## Does accepting Texas education savings account funds let the state regulate a religious school's hiring or admissions? {#education-savings-account-autonomy}

**Short answer.** Texas's 2025 education savings account statute bars state agencies, state officials and certified educational assistance organizations from program-related action that limits a participating school's religious practices, admissions standards, or employment practices based on its religious values. [^tx-re-ed-29-368-autonomy]

The protection, added by S.B. 2 effective September 1, 2025, reaches any rule or other governmental action related to the program that limits or imposes requirements contrary to the school's religious or institutional values or practices, or that limits the school from freely determining its curriculum, its admissions and enrollment practices, or whether to modify its values, policies or employment practices based on its religious values. [^tx-re-ed-29-368-autonomy] A school that receives program money is not, on the basis of receiving that money, a recipient of federal financial assistance or a state actor. [^tx-re-ed-29-368-a-not-state-actor] The section restrains state action related to the program; its text does not displace Chapter 21 of the Labor Code or federal discrimination law, so a participating school's employment decisions remain subject to the rules described in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^tx-re-ed-29-368-autonomy] [^tx-re-tchra-21-109-esa]

The program does impose entry conditions. Under the ordinary approval route for a private school, the comptroller must approve one that demonstrates accreditation by an organization recognized by the Texas Private School Accreditation Commission or the Texas Education Agency, annual administration of a nationally norm-referenced or state assessment to students in grades 3 through 12, and at least two school years of continuous campus operation. [^tx-re-ed-29-358-private-school] Each applicant must also submit documentation that every person it employs who will interact with a participating child is not identified, through the interagency reportable conduct search engine, as having engaged in misconduct described by former Education Code § 22.093(c)(1)(A) or (B), a section Senate Bill 571 (2025) transferred into chapter 22A without a conforming change to this cross-reference. [^tx-re-ed-29-358-e-screening] [^tx-re-ed-22a-052-transfer] A private school that enrolls a participating child with a disability must give the participating parent a copy of the program's notice that private schools are not subject to the same disability-services laws as public schools. [^tx-re-ed-29-367-notice]

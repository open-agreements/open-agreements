# School Policy Compliance in the District of Columbia: Discrimination Law and Religious Employers[^about]

How District of Columbia and federal discrimination law applies to an independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school: which laws cover it, whether an EEO policy is required, the Human Rights Act's religious-organization exemption, the ministerial exception, off-duty conduct, reproductive health decisions, cannabis testing and student admissions.

## Which federal and District of Columbia discrimination laws cover a private school, and at what employee count? {#discrimination-law-coverage}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia Human Rights Act covers any person who employs an individual for compensation, with no minimum headcount, while Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act reach employers with fifteen or more employees and the ADEA reaches those with twenty or more. [^dc-hra-employer-definition] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-dc] [^usc-12111-ada-employer-dc] [^usc-2000gg-pwfa-employer-dc] [^usc-630-adea-employer-dc]

The Act's only carve-out from the employer definition is for a person's parent, spouse or children working in and about the employer's household, so an independent or religious school of any size is an employer. [^dc-hra-employer-definition] Its definition of employee includes an unpaid intern and an individual working or seeking work as an independent contractor. [^dc-hra-employee-definition] D.C. Code § 2-1402.11(a) makes it an unlawful discriminatory practice for an employer to refuse to hire, discharge or otherwise discriminate in compensation, terms, conditions or privileges of employment, wholly or partially for a discriminatory reason based on actual or perceived race, color, religion, national origin, sex, age, marital status, personal appearance, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, family responsibilities, genetic information, disability, matriculation, political affiliation, status as a victim or family member of a victim of domestic violence, a sexual offense or stalking, credit information, or homeless status. [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-protected-traits]

The same section makes harassment based on a protected characteristic an unlawful discriminatory practice and provides that conduct need not be severe or pervasive to constitute harassment. [^dc-hra-harassment-standard] It requires an employer to accommodate an employee's religious observance by letting the employee make up lost work time unless the accommodation would cause undue hardship, which the section defines as more than de minimis cost, and it exempts an employer with 5 or fewer full-time employees from that duty. [^dc-hra-religious-observance] [^dc-hra-religious-observance-small-employer] If the requested accommodation would cause undue hardship, the employer must offer one that does not, including unpaid leave where that causes no undue hardship, and the employee must give at least 10 working days' notice unless the need cannot reasonably be foreseen. [^dc-hra-religious-observance-alternatives] A person aggrieved by an unlawful discriminatory practice may sue in court within 2 years after the act or its discovery. [^dc-hra-private-action-two-years]

Each federal definition is limited to a person engaged in an industry affecting commerce, and the federal headcount counts employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-dc] [^usc-12111-ada-employer-dc] [^usc-630-adea-employer-dc] The Human Rights Act's provision on hiring co-religionists is worded differently from Title VII's, as explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission).

## Does District of Columbia or federal law require an independent school to adopt a written equal employment opportunity policy? {#eeo-policy-required}

**Short answer.** Neither Title VII nor the District of Columbia Human Rights Act, whose general requirements in D.C. Code §§ 2-1402.51 to 2-1402.53 cover posting, records and an affirmative action plan for banks, requires a private school to adopt a general written equal employment opportunity policy. [^usc-2000e-10-posting-eeo-dc] [^dc-hra-posting-notice] [^dc-hra-records-one-year] [^dc-hra-affirmative-action-banks] Title VII instead requires a covered employer to post the EEOC's notice, [^usc-2000e-10-posting-eeo-dc] and the Human Rights Act requires every person subject to it to post the Office of Human Rights' notice in a conspicuous location. [^dc-hra-posting-notice]

An employer that displays the Mayor's universal workplace-rights poster and the accompanying printed compilation, as D.C. Code § 32-161 requires, need not separately meet the Human Rights Act posting requirement. [^dc-universal-poster-substitution-eeo] The Human Rights Act notice must be one whose language and form the Office of Human Rights has prepared, setting out excerpts or summaries of the Act and information on filing a complaint. [^dc-hra-posting-notice] The general requirements in Part F of the Act's discrimination subchapter (D.C. Code §§ 2-1402.51 to 2-1402.53) consist of that posting duty, a records duty and an affirmative action provision. [^dc-hra-posting-notice] [^dc-hra-records-one-year] [^dc-hra-affirmative-action-banks] Every person subject to the Act must preserve regularly kept business records, including application forms and personnel records, for one year from the record's making or the action it concerns, whichever is longer, and must keep records relevant to a filed charge until final disposition. [^dc-hra-records-one-year] The only mandatory written plan in that Part is the annual affirmative action plan required of banks and savings and loan associations, so it does not reach a school. [^dc-hra-affirmative-action-banks]

The Act separately makes it unlawful for an employer to print or publish an employment notice or advertisement, or use a publication form, unlawfully indicating a preference, limitation, specification or distinction based on a listed characteristic. [^dc-hra-advertisements] D.C. Code § 2-1411.05a(f) requires a filed and distributed sexual-harassment policy, but only from an employer that employs an employee paid in accordance with § 32-1003(f), the tipped minimum wage subsection, so it does not apply to a school without tipped employees. [^dc-2-1411-05a-tipped-policy] [^dc-32-1003-f-tipped-wage]

Federal contractor rules differ: a covered contractor's affirmative action program under 41 C.F.R. § 60-741.44(a) must include an equal opportunity policy statement, which the contractor must also post. [^cfr-60-741-44-policy-statement-dc] A religious school's equal employment opportunity statement can also describe the same-religion preference that D.C. Code § 2-1401.03(b) permits, as explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-eeo]

## May a religious school in the District of Columbia prefer co-religionists or require employees to support its religious mission? {#religious-preference-and-mission}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia Human Rights Act lets a religious organization, or an educational organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with one, limit employment or give preference to persons of the same religion as it calculates will promote its religious principles. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption]

Two conditions in D.C. Code § 2-1401.03(b) shape the exemption. A school that is not itself a religious organization must be "operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with a religious or political organization"[^dc-hra-religious-exemption], and the preference must be "calculated by the organization to promote the religious or political principles for which it is established or maintained"[^dc-hra-religious-exemption]. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption] The exemption speaks of limiting employment or giving preference to "persons of the same religion"[^dc-hra-religious-exemption]; it contains no separate clause for employment criteria drawn from religious tenets, and it does not by its terms permit decisions based on the Act's other protected characteristics, such as sexual orientation or marital status. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption] [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-protected-traits-preference] In *Gay Rights Coalition of Georgetown University Law Center v. Georgetown University*, five judges of the en banc D.C. Court of Appeals concluded that the District's compelling interest in eradicating sexual orientation discrimination required a Catholic university, despite its religious objection, to provide equal tangible benefits to gay student groups, although the Act did not require the university to grant its form of recognition. [^dc-georgetown-vote-alignment]

Federal law is broader on co-religionist hiring. Section 702 of Title VII exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities, with no limit to religious roles, and § 703(e)(2) separately permits a school that is, in whole or in substantial part, owned, supported, controlled or managed by a particular religion or by a particular religious corporation, association or society, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a particular religion, to hire and employ employees of that religion. [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer-dc] [^usc-2000e-2-e-2-religious-school-dc] The ADA likewise does not prohibit a religious educational institution from preferring individuals of a particular religion, and it lets a religious organization require all applicants and employees to conform to its religious tenets. [^ada-12113-d-religious-entities-dc]

Because a District school answers to both statutes, a same-religion requirement fits both the federal exemptions and § 2-1401.03(b) when the school meets the D.C. provision's control and purpose conditions, while a conduct or belief standard that turns on another protected characteristic finds no express shelter in § 2-1401.03(b). [^dc-hra-religious-exemption] [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer-dc] For employees who perform important religious functions, the ministerial exception described in the [question on the ministerial exception](#ministerial-exception-teachers) applies to Human Rights Act claims as well. [^dc-pardue-dchra-holding-preference]

## Which religious school teachers and staff fall within the ministerial exception to discrimination claims? {#ministerial-exception-teachers}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception bars employment discrimination claims, including claims under the District of Columbia Human Rights Act, that challenge a religious school's decisions about employees who perform important religious functions, and whether an employee qualifies turns on what the employee does. [^dc-pardue-dchra-holding] [^olg-what-employee-does-dc]

In *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, the Supreme Court recognized the exception, reasoning that requiring a church to accept or retain an unwanted minister intrudes on more than an employment decision. [^hosanna-tabor-exception-recognized-dc] In *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru*, the Court said that educating young people in their faith lies at the core of a private religious school's mission. [^olg-what-employee-does-dc] In *EEOC v. Catholic University of America*, the D.C. Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in the District, considered a Title VII sex-discrimination and retaliation claim by a canon law professor denied tenure, and held that the exception encompasses all employees of a religious institution, ordained or not, whose primary functions serve its spiritual and pastoral mission. [^dc-cua-claims] [^dc-cua-primary-functions]

In *Pardue v. Center City Consortium Schools of the Archdiocese of Washington*, the D.C. Court of Appeals applied the exception to a race-discrimination and retaliation claim under the Human Rights Act brought by the principal of a Catholic elementary school in the District. [^dc-pardue-claims] The court held that her Human Rights Act claims were barred for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. [^dc-pardue-barred] The Supreme Court later held in *Hosanna-Tabor* that the exception operates as an affirmative defense to an otherwise cognizable claim, not a jurisdictional bar, so a school now raises it as a defense. [^hosanna-tabor-affirmative-defense-dc] It reasoned that the principal's predominantly secular and predominantly religious responsibilities were inextricably intertwined in the school's mission and her role in fulfilling it, [^dc-pardue-intertwined] and it held that her claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith was barred by the exception as well. [^dc-pardue-contract-barred]

Because the test asks what the employee does, a teacher or administrator whose duties include teaching the faith or leading the school's religious life is closer to the exception than an employee whose role has no religious function. [^olg-what-employee-does-dc] [^dc-cua-primary-functions] For a role without religious duties, D.C. Code § 2-1401.03(b), described in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission), is the school's statutory basis for a same-religion preference. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-minister]

## Can a District of Columbia religious school discipline or dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that conflicts with its religious teachings? {#off-duty-conduct-religious-school}

**Short answer.** A District of Columbia religious school that disciplines a non-ministerial employee for off-duty conduct tied to marital status or sexual orientation acts on characteristics the District of Columbia Human Rights Act protects, and the Act's religious-organization provision speaks only of preferring persons of the same religion. [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-protected-traits-conduct] [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-conduct]

The Act's definitions reach conduct as well as status. Marital status means being married, in a domestic partnership, single, divorced, separated or widowed "and the usual conditions associated therewith, including pregnancy or parenthood"[^dc-hra-marital-status-definition]. [^dc-hra-marital-status-definition] Sexual orientation means homosexuality, heterosexuality and bisexuality "by preference or practice"[^dc-hra-sexual-orientation-definition]. [^dc-hra-sexual-orientation-definition] Personal appearance, also protected, covers manner or style of dress and grooming, but it does not reach prescribed standards uniformly applied to a class of employees for a reasonable business purpose. [^dc-hra-personal-appearance-definition]

For an employee who performs important religious functions, the ministerial exception can be raised to bar a Human Rights Act claim, as the D.C. Court of Appeals held in *Pardue v. Center City Consortium Schools of the Archdiocese of Washington*. [^dc-pardue-dchra-holding-conduct] For other staff, the closest District authority on a religious institution's free exercise defense under the Act is *Gay Rights Coalition of Georgetown University Law Center v. Georgetown University*, where five judges of the en banc court concluded that the District's compelling interest in eradicating sexual orientation discrimination required a Catholic university, despite its religious objection, to provide equal tangible benefits to student groups. [^dc-georgetown-vote-alignment-conduct] That case concerned student groups rather than employees. In our review, which included case-law and web searches for decisions applying D.C. Code § 2-1401.03(b) to employment, we found no D.C. Court of Appeals decision deciding whether a religious school may dismiss a non-ministerial employee for conduct that violates its religious teachings but falls within a protected characteristic. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-conduct]

Federal law presents a parallel question under Title VII, whose § 702 exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion. [^usc-2000e-1-religious-employer-dc-conduct] In *Doe v. Catholic Relief Services*, the Fourth Circuit held in October 2026 that § 702 does not cover a religiously motivated decision challenged as sex, race or national origin discrimination, a decision that does not bind courts in the District. [^doe-crs-702-not-sex-dc] Separately, D.C. Code § 7-1703.03 bars refusing to hire, discharging or otherwise discriminating against an applicant or employee on the basis of tobacco use, while permitting workplace smoking restrictions and tobacco-use restrictions that are bona fide occupational qualifications. [^dc-7-1703-03-tobacco] Off-duty cannabis use is addressed in the [question on cannabis](#cannabis-off-duty-and-testing). A conduct rule applied to non-ministerial staff that turns on marital status, pregnancy or parenthood, or sexual orientation therefore rests on constitutional or other religious-liberty defenses rather than on an express Human Rights Act exemption. [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-protected-traits-conduct] [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-conduct]

## May a District of Columbia religious school treat an employee differently because of a reproductive health decision, and must its health plan cover that care? {#reproductive-health-decisions}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia Human Rights Act requires an employer to treat an employee affected by a reproductive health decision the same for all employment-related purposes as a similarly able employee, but it does not require an employer to provide insurance coverage related to a reproductive health decision. [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-a-1-b]

D.C. Code § 2-1401.05(a) provides that, for interpreting the Human Rights Act, discrimination on the basis of sex includes discrimination on the basis of pregnancy, childbirth, related medical conditions, breastfeeding, or reproductive health decisions, so the Act's bar on sex discrimination in employment reaches discrimination based on an employee's reproductive health decision. [^dc-hra-2-1401-05-a] That rule binds an independent or religious school of any size, because the Act's employer definition has no headcount threshold, as explained in the [question on which laws cover a private school](#discrimination-law-coverage). The Act defines a reproductive health decision to include a decision by an individual, the individual's dependent or the individual's spouse about the use or intended use of a drug, device or medical service, including contraception or fertility control, or about the planned initiation or termination of a pregnancy. [^dc-hra-reproductive-health-definition] Under D.C. Code § 2-1402.11(a)(1)(B), an employer must treat an employee affected by pregnancy, childbirth, a related medical condition, breastfeeding or a reproductive health decision the same for all employment-related purposes, including fringe benefits, as an employee not so affected but similar in ability or inability to work. [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-a-1-b] The Strengthening Reproductive Health Protections Amendment Act of 2020, D.C. Law 23-90, added that subparagraph and the definition, and it struck the earlier insurance-coverage sentence from D.C. Code § 2-1401.05(a) while carrying the same limitation into the new subparagraph. [^dc-law-23-90-amendments] [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-a-1-b]

The religious-organization provision in D.C. Code § 2-1401.03(b) addresses a preference for persons of the same religion and does not mention reproductive health decisions. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-reproductive] For an employee who performs important religious functions, the ministerial exception can bar a Human Rights Act claim. [^dc-pardue-dchra-holding-reproductive] The insurance proviso means the Act itself does not require a school's health plan to cover contraception, fertility treatment or abortion; the equal-treatment rule still applies to how the school treats the employee in employment decisions and fringe benefits. [^dc-hra-2-1402-11-a-1-b]

## Does District of Columbia law protect a school employee's off-duty cannabis use or limit cannabis drug testing? {#cannabis-off-duty-and-testing}

**Short answer.** District of Columbia law in force bars an employer from testing a prospective employee for marijuana use until after a conditional offer of employment, unless otherwise required by law, while the broader 2022 cannabis employment protections apply only once funded and the official D.C. Code still marks them not funded. [^dc-32-931-preoffer-testing] [^dc-law-24-190-applicability] [^dc-32-951-02-not-funded]

D.C. Code § 32-931 protects a "prospective employee"[^dc-32-931-preoffer-testing], meaning an individual applying for employment, and it reaches private employers through its cross-referenced definition of employer, which covers any corporation or other person having control or custody of a place of employment or of any employee and excludes only the United States government and its agencies. [^dc-32-931-preoffer-testing] [^dc-32-1101-employer-definition] The section bars only marijuana testing before a conditional offer. It does not affect employee compliance with workplace drug policies, does not require an employer to permit or accommodate marijuana use in the workplace or at any time during employment, does not interfere with federal employment contracts, and does not prevent an employer from denying a position based on a positive test for marijuana. [^dc-32-931-preoffer-testing]

The Cannabis Employment Protections Amendment Act of 2022, D.C. Law 24-190, would bar an employer from refusing to hire, terminating, suspending, failing to promote, demoting or penalizing an individual based on the individual's use of cannabis, status as a medical cannabis program patient, or the presence of cannabinoid metabolites in an employer-required or requested drug test without additional factors indicating impairment. [^dc-law-24-190-102-protections] The act defines employer as any person who, for compensation, employs an individual, and its only exclusions are household family members, the District of Columbia court system and the federal government, so its words contain no exemption for a religious or nonpublic school. [^dc-law-24-190-employer-definition]

Section 102(b) provides that "an employer shall not be in violation of this section when the employer takes action related to the use of cannabis based on any of the following"[^dc-law-24-190-safety-sensitive-exemption] four grounds: the employee "is in a position designated as safety sensitive"[^dc-law-24-190-safety-sensitive-exemption]; [^dc-law-24-190-safety-sensitive-exemption] the employer's actions are "required by federal statute, federal regulations, or a federal contract or funding agreement"[^dc-law-24-190-federal-exemption]; [^dc-law-24-190-federal-exemption] the employee "used, consumed, possessed, stored, delivered, transferred, displayed, transported, sold, purchased, or grew cannabis at the employee's place of employment, while performing work for the employer, or during the employee's hours of work"[^dc-law-24-190-workplace-use-exception], unless otherwise permitted under section 211(b-1) of the Human Rights Act or section 2062 of the Comprehensive Merit Personnel Act; [^dc-law-24-190-workplace-use-exception] or, notwithstanding those two provisions, the employee "is impaired by the use of cannabis"[^dc-law-24-190-impairment], meaning the employee "manifests specific articulable symptoms while working, or during the employee's hours of work, that substantially decrease or lessen the employee's performance of the duties or tasks of the employee's job position"[^dc-law-24-190-impairment], or symptoms that interfere with the employer's obligation to provide a safe and healthy workplace under District or federal occupational safety and health law. [^dc-law-24-190-impairment] Section 211(b-1), which Title II of the act would add to the Human Rights Act, would require an employer to treat a qualifying patient's use of medical marijuana to treat a disability like the legal use of a prescribed controlled substance, unless that would require a federal violation, use while the employee is in or assigned to a safety sensitive position, or smokable use at a location the employer owns, uses or controls. [^dc-law-24-190-hra-medical-cannabis]

Under § 103, Title I would not require an employer to "permit or accommodate"[^dc-law-24-190-no-accommodation] cannabis use, possession or other handling at the place of employment, while performing work or during hours of work, unless section 211(b-1) or section 2062 requires it. [^dc-law-24-190-no-accommodation] Nor would it prohibit a "reasonable drug-free workplace or employment policy"[^dc-law-24-190-drug-free-workplace-policy] that requires "post-accident or reasonable suspicion drug testing of employees for cannabis or other drugs or drug testing of employees in safety sensitive positions"[^dc-law-24-190-drug-free-workplace-policy], that is necessary to comply with federal law, including Chapter 81 of Title 41 of the United States Code, or a federal contract or funding agreement if applicable to the employer, that prohibits workplace or work-hours cannabis use unless section 211(b-1) or section 2062 permits it, or that prohibits impairment at work as described in § 102(b)(4). [^dc-law-24-190-drug-free-workplace-policy] Section 103 also states that Title I would not create or eliminate a cause of action against an employer for injury, loss or liability to a third party, would not eliminate any other cause of action available under District law, and would not create a safe harbor or immunity from suit for an employer. [^dc-law-24-190-construction-causes-of-action] Sections 102 and 103 and Title II all carry the § 401 funding condition described below. [^dc-law-24-190-applicability]

The codified definition of safety sensitive, which is in the Code without a funding condition, describes a position, designated by the employer, in which performing routine duties under the influence would likely cause actual, immediate and serious bodily injury or loss of life, and its illustrative list does not name teaching or the supervision of students. [^dc-32-951-01-safety-sensitive] The Act's operative protections apply only upon inclusion of their fiscal effect in an approved budget and financial plan or 365 days after the Mayor's approval, whichever is later, and in June 2025 the Office of Human Rights testified that the Act remained unfunded and could not be implemented. [^dc-law-24-190-applicability] [^dc-ohr-cepa-unfunded] The D.C. Code, current through November 9, 2026, still shows the employment-protections section as not funded. [^dc-32-951-02-not-funded] A school drug and alcohol policy written to the 2022 Act's framework therefore anticipates protections that are not yet enforceable, while § 32-931's pre-offer testing limit applies now. [^dc-law-24-190-applicability] [^dc-32-931-preoffer-testing]

## Does the D.C. Human Rights Act reach a private school's admissions and treatment of students? {#student-admissions-educational-institution}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia Human Rights Act's educational-institution provisions cover private elementary and secondary schools and bar discrimination in access to their facilities, services, programs and benefits on listed grounds, subject to the religious-organization exemption in D.C. Code § 2-1401.03(b). [^dc-hra-educational-institution-definition] [^dc-hra-2-1402-41-education]

D.C. Code § 2-1402.41 makes it unlawful for an educational institution to deny, restrict, abridge or condition access to any of its facilities, services, programs or benefits to any person otherwise qualified, wholly or partially for a discriminatory reason based on actual or perceived race, color, religion, national origin, sex, age, marital status, personal appearance, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, familial status, family responsibilities, political affiliation, source of income, disability or homeless status. [^dc-hra-2-1402-41-education] The Act defines age as 18 years of age or older, so its age ground does not protect applicants younger than 18. [^dc-hra-age-definition-admissions] The same section bars an educational institution from using a written or oral inquiry or application form that elicits, or keeping a record of, an applicant's race, color, religion or national origin, except as permitted by the Office of Human Rights' regulations. [^dc-hra-2-1402-41-education] The exemption in § 2-1401.03(b) lets an educational organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with a religious organization limit admission to, or give preference to, persons of the same religion as calculated to promote its religious principles. [^dc-hra-religious-exemption-admissions]

The Act's rules on sex discrimination in admission policy do not apply to any private preschool, elementary or secondary school, so a private single-sex school may limit admission by sex. [^dc-hra-2-1402-42-single-sex] That carve-out reaches admission policy only; the other prohibitions in § 2-1402.41, including those on sexual orientation and gender identity or expression, still govern access to facilities, services, programs and benefits. [^dc-hra-2-1402-41-education] In *Gay Rights Coalition of Georgetown University Law Center v. Georgetown University*, five judges of the en banc D.C. Court of Appeals concluded that a Catholic university had to provide gay student groups equal tangible benefits despite its religious objection, though the Act did not require it to grant its form of recognition. [^dc-georgetown-vote-alignment-admissions]

# School Policy Compliance in Virginia: Discrimination Law and Religious Employers[^about]

How Virginia and federal discrimination law applies to an independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school: which laws cover it, whether an EEO policy is required, the Virginia Human Rights Act's religious-employer provisions, the ministerial exception, off-duty conduct rules, accommodation notices and student admissions.

## Which federal and Virginia discrimination laws cover a private school, and at what employee count? {#discrimination-law-coverage}

**Short answer.** The Virginia Human Rights Act covers a private school employing five or more employees in each of 20 or more calendar weeks, while Title VII, the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act require fifteen employees and the ADEA requires twenty. [^va-re-vhra-employer] [^va-re-usc-2000e-employer] [^va-re-usc-12111-employer] [^va-re-usc-2000gg-employer] [^va-re-usc-630-employer] Each count is of employees for each working day in those weeks of the current or preceding calendar year. [^va-re-vhra-employer] [^va-re-usc-2000e-employer] [^va-re-usc-12111-employer] [^va-re-usc-2000gg-employer] [^va-re-usc-630-employer]

The five-employee threshold comes from 2026 Va. Acts chapter 950, which replaced the former threshold of 15 employees with five and struck the separate definitions that had reached employers of more than five persons only for unlawful discharge claims. [^va-re-ch950-employer] Chapter 950 has no enactment clause setting its own effective date, so under the Constitution of Virginia it took effect July 1, 2026. [^va-re-ch950-employer] [^va-re-const-art-4-13] The same act reenacted, without change, subsection E's exemption for a religious corporation, association, educational institution or society employing individuals of a particular religion. [^va-re-ch950-religious-exemption] The Act's employment provision makes it an unlawful discriminatory practice for a covered employer to refuse to hire, discharge or otherwise discriminate against an individual because of race, color, religion, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions including lactation, age, military status, disability, or ethnic or national origin. [^va-re-vhra-protected-classes] The Act defines sexual orientation as actual or perceived heterosexuality, bisexuality or homosexuality, defines gender identity with or without regard to designated sex at birth, and provides that religion includes any outward expression of religious faith, including religious dress and grooming practices. [^va-re-vhra-definitions] [^va-re-vhra-religion-definition]

Section 2.2-3902 provides that conduct violating any Virginia or federal statute or regulation governing discrimination on the listed bases is also an unlawful discriminatory practice under the Act. [^va-re-vhra-federal-conduct] A court or jury that finds unlawful discrimination in a private action may award compensatory and punitive damages, and the court may award attorney fees and injunctive relief. [^va-re-vhra-remedies] Chapters 950 and 1119 of the 2026 Acts also lengthened the time to file a complaint with the Office of Civil Rights of the Department of Law from 300 days to two years after the alleged discriminatory practice. [^va-re-ch1119-complaint-deadline] [^va-re-ch950-complaint-deadline] Each federal definition is limited to a person engaged in an industry affecting commerce, and the federal headcount counts employees for each working day in twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. [^va-re-usc-2000e-employer] [^va-re-usc-12111-employer] [^va-re-usc-630-employer]

The Act's provisions on hiring co-religionists are explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission).

## Does Virginia or federal law require an independent school to adopt a written equal employment opportunity policy? {#eeo-policy-required}

**Short answer.** Neither Title VII nor the Virginia Human Rights Act requires a private school to adopt a general written equal employment opportunity policy. Title VII instead requires a covered employer to post the EEOC's notice, [^va-re-usc-2000e-10-posting] and the Virginia Human Rights Act requires a covered employer to post, and to include in any employee handbook, information on employees' rights to reasonable accommodation for disabilities and for pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions. [^va-re-vhra-disability-notice] [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-notice-eeo] A licensed private school for students with disabilities must, however, have a written nondiscrimination policy under the Board of Education's licensing regulations. [^va-re-8vac20-671-210-nondiscrimination] [^va-re-22-1-319-school-definition]

We read the text of Va. Code §§ 2.2-3900 through 2.2-3909 for posting, policy and handbook requirements; the only ones are the accommodation notices in § 2.2-3905.1(C) and § 2.2-3909(D). [^va-re-vhra-disability-notice] [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-notice-eeo] Each of those notices must also be given directly to new employees when their employment begins and to an employee within 10 days after the employee notifies the employer of a disability or a pregnancy. [^va-re-vhra-disability-notice] [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-notice-eeo] The Act separately bars an employer from publishing an employment notice or advertisement that indicates a preference, limitation, specification or discrimination based on a protected characteristic, except that a notice may state a preference based on religion, sex, age, or ethnic or national origin when that characteristic is a bona fide occupational qualification. [^va-re-vhra-advertising]

Under Title VII and the Virginia Human Rights Act, an equal employment opportunity statement in a school's handbook or job postings therefore describes obligations the school already has rather than a separately mandated document. Federal contractor rules differ: a covered contractor's affirmative action program under 41 C.F.R. § 60-741.44(a) must include an equal opportunity policy statement, which the contractor must also post. [^va-re-cfr-60-741-44-policy] One Virginia rule within this audience does require a written policy: the Board of Education's licensing regulations require a private school for students with disabilities licensed under Chapter 16 of Title 22.1 to have a written policy and procedure that it does not discriminate according to state and federal law. [^va-re-8vac20-671-210-nondiscrimination] [^va-re-22-1-319-school-definition] A religious school's statement can also describe the religious-employment provisions of Va. Code § 2.2-3905, explained in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission). [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-eeo]

## May a religious school in Virginia prefer co-religionists or require employees to follow its religious tenets? {#religious-preference-and-mission}

**Short answer.** Virginia's employment-discrimination section does not apply to a religious educational institution's employment of individuals of a particular religion to perform work associated with its activities, and a separate clause lets a religiously owned, controlled or directed elementary or secondary school hire employees of a particular religion. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption] [^va-re-vhra-religious-school]

Subsection E's operative words are "employment of individuals of a particular religion"[^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption] by a religious corporation, association, educational institution or society "to perform work associated with its activities"[^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption]; the text does not limit the exemption to religious roles. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption] Subdivision C 2 applies to an elementary or secondary school that is, in whole or in substantial part, owned, supported, controlled or managed by a particular religion or by a particular religious corporation, association or society, or whose curriculum is directed toward the propagation of a particular religion. [^va-re-vhra-religious-school] Both provisions speak of a particular religion, and the text of § 2.2-3905 contains no clause allowing an employer to require employees to conform to its religious tenets, unlike the ADA's religious-tenets provision. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption] [^va-re-ada-12113-d] A religious preference stated in a job posting falls under subdivision B 8, which allows a notice or advertisement to state a religious preference when religion is a bona fide occupational qualification, and subsection E's exemption extends to the whole section. [^va-re-vhra-advertising-preference] [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption]

Federal law is parallel on co-religionist hiring. Section 702 of Title VII exempts a religious educational institution with respect to employing individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities, and § 703(e)(2) separately permits a school owned, supported, controlled or managed by a particular religion, or whose curriculum is directed toward propagating a particular religion, to hire and employ employees of that religion. [^va-re-usc-2000e-1] [^va-re-usc-2000e-2-e-2] The ADA likewise does not prohibit a religious educational institution from preferring individuals of a particular religion, and it lets a religious organization require all applicants and employees to conform to its religious tenets. [^va-re-ada-12113-d] The EEOC's Compliance Manual states that the § 702 exemption applies only to organizations whose purpose and character are primarily religious, judged on all the facts, [^va-re-eeoc-religious-organization] and that religious organizations remain subject to Title VII's prohibitions on race, color, sex and national origin discrimination and to the ADEA and ADA. [^va-re-eeoc-other-bases]

A co-religionist requirement therefore rests on the same footing under the Virginia Human Rights Act and Title VII. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption] [^va-re-usc-2000e-1] A conduct or belief standard that is challenged as discrimination on another protected basis, such as sex, sexual orientation, marital status or pregnancy, does not fit as easily within an exemption phrased in terms of a particular religion, as explained in the [question on off-duty conduct](#off-duty-conduct-religious-school). [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption] The conservative course is to document the school's religious ownership, control or curriculum, to state any religious-affiliation requirement in writing, and to apply it uniformly.

## Which Virginia religious school teachers and staff fall within the ministerial exception to discrimination claims? {#ministerial-exception-teachers}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception bars employment discrimination claims challenging a religious school's decisions about employees who perform important religious functions, such as educating students in the faith, and whether an employee qualifies turns on what the employee does. [^va-re-olg-what-employee-does] [^va-re-hosanna-tabor-recognized]

In *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, the Supreme Court recognized the exception, reasoning that requiring a church to accept or retain an unwanted minister intrudes on more than an employment decision. [^va-re-hosanna-tabor-recognized] In *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru*, the Court said that educating young people in their faith lies at the core of a private religious school's mission. [^va-re-olg-what-employee-does] The EEOC's Compliance Manual describes the exception as reaching lay employees and even employees who are not co-religionists. [^va-re-eeoc-ministerial-lay]

The Fourth Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in Virginia, applied the exception to a teacher of secular subjects in *Billard v. Charlotte Catholic High School*, holding that a longtime English and drama teacher who played a vital role as a messenger of the school's faith fell within it. [^va-re-billard-holding] The court reasoned that his duties included conforming his instruction to Christian thought and providing a classroom environment consistent with Catholicism, and it said that seemingly secular teaching may be so imbued with religious significance that it implicates the exception. [^va-re-billard-secular-duties] [^va-re-billard-secular-subjects] The court treated employment contracts and evaluations as relevant because they articulate the school's religious expectations for the role. [^va-re-billard-contracts] Virginia's appellate courts apply these religious-liberty limits under the state constitution as well: in *Episcopal Diocese of Southern Virginia v. Marshall*, the Court of Appeals of Virginia held a deposed priest's defamation claim against his bishop barred by the First Amendment and by Article I, Section 16 of the Virginia Constitution, the state's free-exercise provision. [^va-re-marshall-holding] [^va-re-va-const-art-i-16]

Because the test asks what the employee does, a teacher whose duties include teaching the faith or integrating it into a secular subject is closer to the exception than an employee whose role has no religious function. [^va-re-olg-what-employee-does] [^va-re-billard-secular-subjects] For a role without religious duties, the statutory provisions on hiring individuals of a particular religion, described in the [question on preferring co-religionists](#religious-preference-and-mission), are the school's basis for religious employment criteria. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-minister]

## Can a Virginia religious school discipline or dismiss an employee for off-duty conduct that conflicts with its religious teachings? {#off-duty-conduct-religious-school}

**Short answer.** The ministerial exception bars a discrimination claim by an employee who performs important religious functions, including a claim over discipline for off-duty conduct. [^va-re-billard-facts] [^va-re-doe-702-not-sex] For other staff, the Fourth Circuit held in 2026 that Title VII's religious-employer exemption does not cover a religiously motivated decision challenged as sex discrimination. [^va-re-billard-facts] [^va-re-doe-702-not-sex]

In *Billard v. Charlotte Catholic High School*, a teacher sued for sex discrimination under Title VII after the school ended his employment over his plans to marry his same-sex partner, and the Fourth Circuit resolved the case on the ministerial exception without deciding the school's statutory defenses. [^va-re-billard-facts] [^va-re-billard-ground] In *Doe v. Catholic Relief Services*, decided October 16, 2026 by a divided panel, the Fourth Circuit held that § 702 does not apply to claims of sex, race or national origin discrimination even when the decision is motivated by religious belief. [^va-re-doe-702-not-sex] [^va-re-doe-panel] The court also held that, for lay employees like the plaintiff, who did not perform a spiritual function, was not involved in carrying out the organization's mission and was not a member of the church, the church autonomy doctrine does not require courts to give less protection than employees of secular organizations receive, and it said that religious organizations may require employees to adhere to religious doctrine so long as the requirements do not discriminate on the basis of race, sex or national origin. [^va-re-doe-church-autonomy] [^va-re-doe-summary]

The Virginia Human Rights Act protects sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status and pregnancy as well as sex. [^va-re-vhra-protected-classes-conduct] Its religious-employer exemption covers "the employment of individuals of a particular religion"[^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-conduct] and contains no religious-tenets clause. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-conduct] Our review found no Virginia appellate or federal decision construing § 2.2-3905(E) or (C)(2) (we searched CourtListener for opinions of the Supreme Court of Virginia, the Court of Appeals of Virginia, the Fourth Circuit and the federal district courts in Virginia citing § 2.2-3905 with religious terms), so whether the exemption covers a religiously motivated decision challenged as sexual orientation, marital status or pregnancy discrimination is undecided. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-conduct] Subsection E closely tracks § 702, so *Doe*'s reading of § 702 is the nearest guide, though it does not bind Virginia's state courts construing the state statute. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-conduct] [^va-re-usc-2000e-1-conduct] [^va-re-doe-702-not-sex] The conservative course is to treat a conduct rule applied to staff outside the ministerial exception as subject to the Act's sex, sexual orientation, marital status and pregnancy protections.

Virginia's religious-freedom statute bars a government entity from substantially burdening a person's free exercise of religion unless it demonstrates that the burden is essential to a compelling governmental interest and the least restrictive means, and it lets a person assert a violation as a claim or defense in any judicial or administrative proceeding. [^va-re-va-rfra-burden] [^va-re-va-rfra-defense] The statute speaks of a burden imposed by government, and the Fourth Circuit observed in *Billard* that only one federal court of appeals has applied the federal Religious Freedom Restoration Act to a lawsuit between private parties; the conservative course is not to rely on the Virginia statute as a defense to a private employee's suit. [^va-re-va-rfra-defense] [^va-re-billard-rfra]

Separate statutes protect particular off-duty conduct. Va. Code § 40.1-27.4 bars an employer from discharging, disciplining or discriminating against an employee for lawful use of cannabis oil under a practitioner's valid written certification, and it uses the Title 40.1 definition of employer, which reaches a private or religious school. [^va-re-cannabis-oil-protection] [^va-re-title-40-1-employer] The section preserves adverse action for work impairment caused by cannabis oil and bans on possession during work hours, does not require an act that would violate federal law or cost the employer a federal contract or federal funding, and exempts defense industrial base sector employers as to applicants and employees who test above stated THC levels; it does not otherwise regulate drug testing. [^va-re-cannabis-oil-exceptions] Va. Code §§ 2.2-2902 and 15.2-1504, which bar requiring abstention from off-duty tobacco use as a condition of employment, reach only employees of and applicants to the Commonwealth and its localities and political subdivisions, not a private school. [^va-re-tobacco-state] [^va-re-tobacco-local]

## Does Virginia's religious-employer exemption excuse a religious school from the pregnancy and disability accommodation rules and handbook notices? {#accommodation-notices-religious-school}

**Short answer.** The Virginia Human Rights Act's religious-employer exemption is limited to § 2.2-3905, so it does not excuse a religious school meeting the employee thresholds from the Act's pregnancy and disability accommodation duties or from describing those rights in any employee handbook. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-notice] [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-accommodation] [^va-re-vhra-disability-refuse]

Subsection E begins "The provisions of this section shall not apply"[^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-notice], and the pregnancy and disability accommodation duties sit in separate sections, § 2.2-3909 and § 2.2-3905.1. [^va-re-vhra-religious-exemption-notice] [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-accommodation] [^va-re-vhra-disability-refuse] The pregnancy section applies to an employer with five or more employees for each working day in each of 20 or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year, while the disability section applies to an employer with more than five employees measured the same way. [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-employer] [^va-re-vhra-disability-accommodation] Under § 2.2-3909, an employer may not refuse a reasonable accommodation to known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions unless it demonstrates undue hardship, and it must engage in a timely, good faith interactive process with an employee who requests one. [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-accommodation] [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-interactive]

Each section requires the employer to post the rights information in a conspicuous location, include it in any employee handbook, and give it directly to new employees and to an employee within 10 days after notice of a pregnancy or disability. [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-notice] [^va-re-vhra-disability-notice-religious] An employee or applicant denied a pregnancy accommodation right may sue in a general district or circuit court within two years, or within 90 days after a final disposition of a timely complaint to the Office of Civil Rights or a local human rights agency, and the court or jury may award compensatory damages, back pay and other equitable relief, with attorney fees. [^va-re-vhra-pregnancy-remedy] Federal law points the same way for larger schools: the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act applies to employers with fifteen or more employees and is made subject to Title VII's § 702 rule on employing individuals of a particular religion. [^va-re-usc-2000gg-employer-notice] [^va-re-usc-2000gg-5-religious]

## Does the Virginia Human Rights Act or federal law bar discrimination in a private school's admissions and treatment of students? {#student-admissions-public-accommodation}

**Short answer.** The Virginia Human Rights Act treats educational institutions offering or holding out their services to the general public as places of public accommodation, but its public accommodation section does not apply to a religious body's place that is not in fact open to the public. [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation] [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation-exemption] The same exemption covers any other establishment not in fact open to the public. [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation] [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation-exemption]

Educational institutions were added to the definition of a place of public accommodation by 2026 Va. Acts chapter 950, which sets no effective date of its own and so took effect July 1, 2026, and the same act reenacted in the same words subsection C's exemption for a religious body's place that is not in fact open to the public. [^va-re-ch950-public-accommodation] [^va-re-ch950-public-accommodation-exemption] [^va-re-const-art-4-13-admissions]

For a covered school, the Act makes it unlawful to refuse, withhold or deny any of its accommodations, advantages, facilities, services or privileges, or to segregate or discriminate in their use, on the basis of race, color, religion, ethnic or national origin, sex, pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, disability, or military status. [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation] Age in this section means 18 or older, and the section does not prohibit discrimination against individuals younger than 18 solely on the basis of age. [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation-age] The Act does not define "not in fact open to the public"[^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation-exemption], and our CourtListener search of Virginia appellate and federal decisions citing § 2.2-3904 found no decision applying the phrase to a religious school, so whether a religious school that admits students from the general community qualifies is undecided; the conservative course is to assume the section applies unless the school's enrollment is in fact restricted. [^va-re-vhra-public-accommodation-exemption]

Federal law separately bars racial exclusion. Under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, all persons have the same right to make and enforce contracts as white citizens, and that right is protected against nongovernmental discrimination. [^va-re-usc-1981] In *Runyon v. McCrary*, the Supreme Court held that two Virginia private schools' racial exclusion of applicants was a classic violation of § 1981. [^va-re-runyon-violation] The Court noted that the case did not present a private school's right to limit its student body to boys, to girls or to adherents of a particular religious faith, or the application of § 1981 to sectarian schools that practice racial exclusion on religious grounds. [^va-re-runyon-reserved]

A school operated by a § 501(c)(3) organization, including one operated as an activity of a church, must certify annually to the IRS that it meets Rev. Proc. 75-50, which requires a statement of a racially nondiscriminatory policy as to students in its governing instrument or a governing-body resolution and in its brochures and catalogues dealing with admissions, programs and scholarships. [^va-re-irs-5578-who-files] [^va-re-irs-5578-school-definition] [^va-re-irs-5578-policy-statement] Title III of the ADA lists private elementary and secondary schools as public accommodations but does not apply to religious organizations or entities controlled by religious organizations. [^va-re-ada-12181-schools] [^va-re-ada-12187-religious]

# School Policy Compliance in the District of Columbia: Employee Handbook Notices and Workplace Rules[^about]

What a District of Columbia independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's employee handbook and workplace policies must reflect: required notices, the Human Rights Act harassment standard, confidentiality clauses, outside-work rules under the non-compete ban, cannabis testing, pay transparency, criminal-history and credit checks, and NLRB work-rule standards.

## What written notices and postings must a District of Columbia independent school give its employees, and can the handbook carry them? {#handbook-required-notices}

**Short answer.** A District of Columbia school must display the Mayor's universal workplace-rights poster, with a printed compilation of the District's workplace-rights website at each poster location, and must give each employee a written notice of pay terms at hiring and whenever that information changes. [^dc-hb-universal-poster] [^dc-hb-universal-binder] [^dc-hb-wage-notice]

| Requirement | What the law requires | When or where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Universal workplace-rights poster, D.C. Code § 32-161 | The Mayor's poster summarizing employee rights under listed District labor and anti-discrimination laws, with a printed compilation of the District's workplace-rights website kept beside it and checked at least monthly | Posted in a conspicuous place and at each breakroom or time clock [^dc-hb-universal-poster] [^dc-hb-universal-binder] |
| Human Rights Act, D.C. Code § 2-1402.51 | A notice prepared by the Office of Human Rights summarizing the Act and how to file a complaint | Posted in a conspicuous location where business is customarily conducted [^dc-hb-dchra-poster] |
| Accrued sick and safe leave, D.C. Code § 32-531.09 | The Mayor's notice summarizing the sick and safe leave law and complaint information, in English and in the languages of employees with limited or no English proficiency | Posted and maintained in a conspicuous place [^dc-hb-assl-poster] |
| Minimum wage, D.C. Code § 32-1009 | A copy or summary of the Minimum Wage Revision Act and applicable regulations, in a form the Mayor prescribes or approves | Posted in a conspicuous and accessible place on the premises [^dc-hb-minwage-poster] |
| Pay notice, D.C. Code § 32-1008(c) | A written notice of the employer's name, address and telephone number, the employee's rate and basis of pay, overtime and other exemptions, and the regular payday, in English and, where a template exists, the employee's primary language | At hiring and whenever the information changes [^dc-hb-wage-notice] [^dc-hb-wage-notice-contents] |
| D.C. Family and Medical Leave Act, D.C. Code § 32-511 | The Mayor's notice summarizing the Act and complaint information, for employers with twenty or more persons in the District | Posted and maintained in a conspicuous place [^dc-hb-fmla-poster] [^dc-hb-fmla-twenty] |
| Universal Paid Leave, D.C. Code § 32-541.06(i)(3) | The Mayor's notice of paid-leave benefits, the bar on retaliation and complaint procedures, for covered employers | Given at hiring, annually and when the employer learns leave is needed, and posted [^dc-hb-upl-notice] [^dc-hb-upl-covered-employer] |
| Wage transparency, D.C. Code § 32-1453.02 | A notice of employees' rights under the wage transparency law | Posted where employees congregate [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-poster] |
| Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-10, for employers with fifteen or more employees | A posted notice prepared or approved by the EEOC | Posted where notices are customarily posted [^usc-2000e-10-posting-dc-hb] [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-dc-hb] |

An employer that displays the universal poster and compilation as required need not meet the separate posting requirements of the Human Rights Act, the sick and safe leave law, the minimum wage law, the D.C. Family and Medical Leave Act, the Universal Paid Leave notice provision, the pregnancy accommodation law and several other District laws, but the universal poster itself is not optional, [^dc-hb-universal-substitution] and the Mayor assesses a $100 fine for each day an employer fails to meet those requirements. [^dc-hb-universal-fine] The substitution covers posting requirements only, so notices the law requires an employer to give each employee, such as the pay notice and the Universal Paid Leave notice at hiring and annually, must still be delivered. [^dc-hb-universal-substitution] [^dc-hb-wage-notice] [^dc-hb-upl-notice]

Most of these duties reach an independent or religious school without regard to size. The Human Rights Act defines an employer as any person who, for compensation, employs an individual, with an exception only for a household employer's parent, spouse or children. [^dc-hb-dchra-employer] The sick and safe leave law defines an employer as a legal entity, including a nonprofit association or corporation, [^dc-hb-assl-employer] and the minimum wage law reaches any association or corporation, while excluding from the definition of employee an unpaid volunteer of an educational, charitable, religious or nonprofit organization and a lay member elected or appointed to office within a religious organization and engaged in religious functions. [^dc-hb-minwage-definitions] The D.C. Family and Medical Leave Act applies to an employer that employs twenty or more persons in the District. [^dc-hb-fmla-twenty]

The Universal Paid Leave notice binds only a covered employer, defined as one required to pay District unemployment insurance on behalf of its employees. [^dc-hb-upl-covered-employer] The unemployment law excludes from covered employment service for a church and service for an organization operated primarily for religious purposes and operated, supervised, controlled or principally supported by a church, [^dc-hb-ui-church-exclusion] so a church-operated school within that exclusion falls outside the Universal Paid Leave notice duty on the statute's terms. [^dc-hb-upl-covered-employer] [^dc-hb-ui-church-exclusion] The employer proves the pay notice by keeping a copy signed and dated by both the employer and the employee acknowledging receipt, or an electronic record showing that the employee received and acknowledged it, so a handbook acknowledgment page can serve that purpose only if it carries the notice's required contents. [^dc-hb-wage-notice-proof] [^dc-hb-wage-notice-contents]

## Does a District of Columbia school's anti-harassment policy affect its liability when an employee claims harassment by a supervisor? {#anti-harassment-policy}

**Short answer.** Under Title VII, *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton* holds an employer vicariously liable for a supervisor's actionable harassment, subject, when no tangible employment action is taken, to an affirmative defense that looks to the reasonableness of the employer's conduct and the employee's. [^faragher-holding-dc-hb] [^faragher-defense-elements-dc-hb]

When no tangible employment action is taken, *Faragher* requires the employer to prove that it exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct promptly any harassing behavior and that the employee unreasonably failed to use the preventive or corrective opportunities it provided or to avoid harm otherwise; a stated anti-harassment policy with a complaint procedure is not required in every case but may be addressed in litigating the first element. [^faragher-defense-elements-dc-hb] Title VII reaches an employer with fifteen or more employees in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding year. [^usc-2000e-employer-fifteen-dc-hb-harassment]

The D.C. Human Rights Act sets its own harassment standard for every employer it covers, which includes any person who employs an individual for compensation. [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-definition] [^dc-hb-dchra-employer-harassment] Subsection (c-2) of D.C. Code § 2-1402.11 makes harassment based on a protected characteristic an unlawful discriminatory practice and defines it as conduct that unreasonably alters terms, conditions or privileges of employment or has the purpose or effect of creating an intimidating, hostile or offensive work environment. [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-definition] Conduct need not be severe or pervasive, and the fact finder weighs factors that include whether a party held formal authority or informal power over another. [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-factors] Harassment may be found even when the conduct was a single incident, was directed at someone else or occurred outside the workplace, [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-circumstances] so a policy that reaches only on-premises conduct or only severe or pervasive conduct is narrower than the District standard. [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-factors] [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-circumstances] Subsection (c-2) defines harassment and lists the fact finder's factors; it does not say whether the *Faragher* defense applies to a Human Rights Act claim. [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-definition] [^dc-hb-dchra-harassment-factors]

The Human Rights Act protects applicants and independent contractors as employees. [^dc-hb-dchra-employee] Subsection (c-2) was added by the Human Rights Enhancement Amendment Act of 2022, applicable October 1, 2022. [^dc-hb-law-24-172-applicability] The Fairness in Human Rights Administration Amendment Act of 2024 extended the definition of sexual harassment to conduct based on sex, gender, sexual orientation or gender identity or expression, [^dc-hb-law-25-300-sexual-harassment] and the Fiscal Year 2026 Budget Support Act of 2025 repealed the funding condition on that act. [^dc-hb-law-26-55-repeal] The Act's religious-organization exception permits a religious organization, or an educational organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with one, to limit employment or give preference to persons of the same religion; its text speaks to that limiting and preference only. [^dc-hb-dchra-religious-exception] The religious-employer provisions and the ministerial exception are addressed in the [guide on discrimination law and religious employers](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/district-of-columbia-religious-employers).

## Can a District of Columbia school's employment agreement or handbook require employees to keep a harassment claim or their pay confidential? {#confidentiality-clauses}

**Short answer.** Under the federal Speak Out Act, a nondisclosure or nondisparagement clause agreed to before a sexual assault or sexual harassment dispute arises is not judicially enforceable where the conduct is alleged to have violated federal, tribal or state law. [^speak-out-19403-dc-hb]

The Act defines a nondisclosure clause as a provision requiring the parties not to disclose or discuss conduct, a settlement involving conduct, or information covered by the agreement, and a sexual harassment dispute as one about conduct alleged to be sexual harassment under applicable federal, tribal or state law. [^speak-out-19402-dc-hb] The Act applies to claims filed under federal, state or tribal law on or after December 7, 2022. [^speak-out-19404-dc-hb] The rule reaches clauses agreed to before the dispute arises, so it does not by its terms address a confidentiality clause in a settlement reached after the dispute; it also preserves an employer's protection of trade secrets and proprietary information and state or local laws that are at least as protective of speech. [^speak-out-19403-dc-hb] A separate federal statute makes a predispute arbitration agreement or joint-action waiver unenforceable, at the election of the person alleging a sexual harassment or sexual assault dispute, for a case relating to that dispute. [^efaa-402-dc-hb]

District law separately limits confidentiality rules about pay. An employer may not require, as a condition of employment, that an employee refrain from inquiring about, disclosing, comparing or discussing compensation, and may not retaliate against an employee who does so. [^dc-hb-pay-discussion] An employer may bar an employee with regular access to other employees' compensation information, such as a human resources employee, from sharing it, unless the disclosure responds to an investigation, action or hearing or the employer has a legal obligation to furnish it. [^dc-hb-pay-discussion-hr-exception] The wage transparency law reaches an individual, firm, association or corporation that employs at least one employee in the District, excluding only the District and federal governments. [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-employer]

Where the National Labor Relations Board has jurisdiction, its 2023 *McLaren Macomb* decision held that simply offering a severance agreement requiring employees to broadly give up their labor-law rights is unlawful, in a case involving non-disparagement and confidentiality terms. [^nlrb-mclaren-macomb-dc-hb] The Board's General Counsel stated on August 26, 2026 that the General Counsel is arguing to overrule *McLaren Macomb* in a pending case, while directing regional offices to continue prosecuting cases under extant Board law. [^nlrb-gc-26-04-positions-dc-hb] [^nlrb-gc-26-04-extant-law-dc-hb]

## Can a District of Columbia school's handbook bar teachers and staff from tutoring or other outside paid work? {#outside-work-policies}

**Short answer.** Since October 1, 2022, a District of Columbia employer may not require or request that a covered employee comply with a workplace policy that prohibits the employee from performing work for another for pay, subject to exceptions the statute lists. [^dc-hb-noncompete-ban] [^dc-hb-noncompete-provision] The exceptions permit a rule against accepting outside pay that the employer reasonably believes would lead to disclosure of confidential or proprietary information, conflict with established conflict-of-interest rules, or impair its ability to comply with law, a contract or a grant agreement. [^dc-hb-noncompete-provision]

A workplace policy includes rules and restrictions implemented by an employer to govern employee conduct, whether written or as a matter of practice, so an unwritten moonlighting rule is within the ban. [^dc-hb-workplace-policy] An employer whose workplace policy relies on one of the exceptions must give each employee a written copy of those provisions within 30 days after the employee accepts employment and any time the policy changes. [^dc-hb-exception-disclosure] An employer may not retaliate against a covered employee for refusing a prohibited provision, for not complying with one, or for asking about or complaining about a provision the employee reasonably believes is prohibited. [^dc-hb-noncompete-ban]

The ban protects a covered employee, meaning an employee who is not highly compensated and who either spends more than half of his or her work time for the employer in the District or is based in the District and regularly spends a substantial amount of work time there and not more than half in another jurisdiction, [^dc-hb-covered-employee] and the Department of Employment Services states that, as of January 1, 2026, the ban applies to employees earning less than $162,164. [^dc-hb-noncompete-threshold-2026] The statute defines employer as an association, corporation or other entity operating in the District, excluding only the District and federal governments, so it reaches an independent or religious school. [^dc-hb-noncompete-employer]

## Can a District of Columbia school discipline an employee for off-duty cannabis use or a positive cannabis test? {#off-duty-cannabis-use}

**Short answer.** The District's 2022 cannabis employment protections, which would bar most adverse action for off-duty cannabis use or a positive test, do not yet apply, because their operative sections take effect only when funded and the Council's October 2026 report lists the act as not funded. [^dc-hb-cepaa-long-title] [^dc-hb-cepaa-applicability] [^dc-hb-council-unfunded-report]

The Cannabis Employment Protections Amendment Act of 2022 makes its employment protections, rules of construction, notice duty and enforcement sections apply on the later of inclusion of their cost in an approved budget and financial plan or 365 days after the Mayor's approval. [^dc-hb-cepaa-applicability] The official D.C. Code marks the employment-protections and notice sections as not funded, [^dc-hb-cepaa-not-funded-protections] [^dc-hb-cepaa-not-funded-notice] and the Office of Human Rights told the Council on June 10, 2025 that the act remained unfunded and could not be implemented. [^dc-hb-ohr-unfunded-testimony] As of the November 23, 2026 review, the most recent record cited here is the Council's report of currently unfunded laws dated October 16, 2026, which lists the act as not funded. [^dc-hb-council-unfunded-report]

If funded, the act would bar refusing to hire, terminating, suspending, failing to promote, demoting or penalizing an individual based on cannabis use, medical cannabis patient status, or cannabinoid metabolites in a drug test without additional factors indicating impairment. [^dc-hb-cepaa-protections-text] It would reach a nonpublic school, because it defines employer as any person who, for compensation, employs an individual, and its only exclusions are household family members, the District of Columbia court system and the federal government. [^dc-hb-cepaa-employer-definition]

Section 102(b) provides that "an employer shall not be in violation of this section when the employer takes action related to the use of cannabis based on any of the following"[^dc-hb-cepaa-exceptions-text] four grounds. [^dc-hb-cepaa-exceptions-text] The first is that the employee "is in a position designated as safety sensitive"[^dc-hb-cepaa-exceptions-text]. [^dc-hb-cepaa-exceptions-text] The second is that the employer's actions are "required by federal statute, federal regulations, or a federal contract or funding agreement"[^dc-hb-cepaa-federal-exception]. [^dc-hb-cepaa-federal-exception] The third is that the employee "used, consumed, possessed, stored, delivered, transferred, displayed, transported, sold, purchased, or grew cannabis at the employee's place of employment, while performing work for the employer, or during the employee's hours of work"[^dc-hb-cepaa-workplace-use-exception], unless otherwise permitted under section 211(b-1) of the Human Rights Act (D.C. Code § 2-1402.11(b-1)) or section 2062 of the Comprehensive Merit Personnel Act (D.C. Code § 1-620.62). [^dc-hb-cepaa-workplace-use-exception] Section 211(b-1), which Title II of the act would add and which carries the same funding condition, would require an employer to treat a qualifying patient's use of medical marijuana to treat a disability in the same manner as the legal use of a prescribed controlled substance, unless that would require a federal violation, use while the employee is in or assigned to a safety sensitive position, or use in a smokable form at a location the employer owns, uses or controls. [^dc-hb-cepaa-hra-medical-cannabis] [^dc-hb-cepaa-applicability] The fourth applies notwithstanding those two provisions: the employee "is impaired by the use of cannabis"[^dc-hb-cepaa-impairment], meaning the employee "manifests specific articulable symptoms while working, or during the employee's hours of work, that substantially decrease or lessen the employee's performance of the duties or tasks of the employee's job position"[^dc-hb-cepaa-impairment], or symptoms that interfere with the employer's obligation to provide a safe and healthy workplace under District or federal occupational safety and health law. [^dc-hb-cepaa-impairment]

Section 103, which also carries the funding condition, sets rules of construction. [^dc-hb-cepaa-applicability] Title I would not require an employer to "permit or accommodate"[^dc-hb-cepaa-no-accommodation] the use, possession or other handling of cannabis at the place of employment, while performing work or during hours of work, unless section 211(b-1) or section 2062 requires it. [^dc-hb-cepaa-no-accommodation] Nor would it prohibit an employer from adopting a "reasonable drug-free workplace or employment policy"[^dc-hb-cepaa-drug-free-workplace-policy] that requires "post-accident or reasonable suspicion drug testing of employees for cannabis or other drugs or drug testing of employees in safety sensitive positions"[^dc-hb-cepaa-drug-free-workplace-policy], that is necessary to comply with federal law, including Chapter 81 of Title 41 of the United States Code, or a federal contract or funding agreement if applicable to the employer, that prohibits cannabis use, possession or other handling at the place of employment, while performing work or during hours of work unless section 211(b-1) or section 2062 permits it, or that prohibits employees from being impaired at work as described in § 102(b)(4). [^dc-hb-cepaa-drug-free-workplace-policy] Section 103 also states that Title I would not create or eliminate a cause of action against an employer for injury, loss or liability to a third party, would not eliminate any other cause of action available under District law, and would not create a safe harbor or immunity from suit for an employer. [^dc-hb-cepaa-construction-causes-of-action]

The act's definitions, which are in force, make an applicant or unpaid intern an employee, [^dc-hb-cepaa-employee-definition] limit use of cannabis to legal consumption of marijuana under District law, [^dc-hb-cepaa-use-definition] and treat as a medical cannabis program patient an individual actively registered in the District's program or in the program of the employee's jurisdiction of residence. [^dc-hb-cepaa-patient-definition] Its definition of safety sensitive, which is also in force, is a position designated by the employer in which impaired performance of routine duties would likely cause actual, immediate and serious bodily injury or loss of life, and its examples include regular or frequent operation of a motor vehicle and the administration of medications. [^dc-hb-safety-sensitive-definition]

The rule now in force is narrower: an employer may test a prospective employee for marijuana only after extending a conditional offer, unless otherwise required by law, and the statute preserves workplace drug policies, does not require accommodation of marijuana in the workplace or during employment, does not interfere with federal employment contracts, and does not prevent denying a position based on a positive test. [^dc-hb-preemployment-testing] That statute borrows the occupational safety law's definition of employer, which covers any corporation or other person having control or custody of a place of employment or of any employee. [^dc-hb-osh-employer]

## Must a District of Columbia school's job postings include a pay range, and may it ask applicants about salary history? {#pay-range-job-postings}

**Short answer.** Since June 30, 2024, a District of Columbia employer with at least one employee in the District must state the minimum and maximum projected salary or hourly pay in every job listing and position description it advertises. [^dc-hb-pay-range] [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-employer-postings] [^dc-hb-law-25-138-applicability]

The range must run from the lowest to the highest pay the employer in good faith believes, at the time of posting, it would pay for the advertised job, promotion or transfer, and the employer must disclose the existence of healthcare benefits to prospective employees before the first interview. [^dc-hb-pay-range] The employer may not screen applicants based on wage history, require disclosure of wage history as a condition of an interview or continued consideration, or seek an applicant's wage history from a former employer. [^dc-hb-wage-history] It must also post a notice of employees' rights under the law where employees congregate. [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-poster-postings]

The law defines employer to exclude only the District and federal governments, so it reaches an independent or religious school with one or more employees in the District. [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-employer-postings] These requirements were added by the Wage Transparency Omnibus Amendment Act of 2023, which applies as of June 30, 2024. [^dc-hb-law-25-138-applicability] The Mayor assesses fines of $1,000 for a first violation, $5,000 for a second and $20,000 for each later violation, and the statute creates no private right of action. [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-enforcement] [^dc-hb-wage-transparency-no-private-action]

## When may a District of Columbia school ask job applicants about arrests and criminal convictions? {#criminal-history-inquiries}

**Short answer.** The District's Fair Criminal Record Screening rules, which bar employers with more than ten District employees from asking about convictions before a conditional offer, do not apply to any facility or employer that provides programs, services or direct care to minors. [^dc-hb-fcrs-employer] [^dc-hb-fcrs-rules-and-exemptions]

Where the screening law applies, an employer may not ask about an arrest or about a criminal accusation that is not pending or did not result in a conviction, and may ask about a conviction only after a conditional offer. [^dc-hb-fcrs-rules-and-exemptions] The prohibitions also do not apply where federal or District law requires consideration of criminal history, or to a position in a government program designed to encourage employing people with criminal histories. [^dc-hb-fcrs-rules-and-exemptions] A K–12 school provides programs and services to minors and so falls within the minors exemption on the statute's terms. [^dc-hb-fcrs-rules-and-exemptions]

The District's criminal background check law for child and youth services providers reaches a private entity only if it is licensed by or contracts with the District to provide direct services to children or youth, or services for their benefit, that affect their health, safety and welfare. [^dc-hb-cbc-covered-provider] Where it applies, applicants for paid employment, employees and unsupervised volunteers must apply for criminal background checks, except those whose positions will not bring them into direct contact with children and youth, volunteers with only supervised contact, and holders of an active federal security clearance. [^dc-hb-cbc-required] [^dc-hb-cbc-exceptions] A covered provider may make an offer contingent on a satisfactory check and let the applicant work in a supervised setting before the results arrive, a volunteer with direct contact may not begin volunteering unsupervised until satisfactory results arrive, and employees and unsupervised volunteers working in an unsupervised setting must submit to periodic checks. [^dc-hb-cbc-exceptions]

## Can a District of Columbia school run credit checks on job applicants or employees? {#credit-history-checks}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia Human Rights Act makes it an unlawful discriminatory practice for an employer to require, request or cause an employee to submit credit information, or to use or inquire into an employee's credit information, subject to exceptions the statute lists. [^dc-hb-credit-prohibition] The exceptions cover credit inquiries required by District law, law enforcement positions, specified District agencies, positions requiring a security clearance under District law, disclosures by District government employees to District ethics and inspector general offices, financial institutions where the position involves access to personal financial information, and requests under a subpoena, court order or law enforcement investigation. [^dc-hb-credit-exceptions]

Credit information means any communication bearing on creditworthiness, credit standing, credit capacity or credit history, and an inquiry includes application forms, interviews and credit history checks. [^dc-hb-credit-definitions] The Act's definition of employee includes an individual seeking employment, so the rule reaches applicants as well as staff. [^dc-hb-dchra-employee-credit] The Act's definition of employer covers any person who employs an individual for compensation, so the rule reaches an independent or religious school of any size. [^dc-hb-dchra-employer-credit]

## Do NLRB standards for handbook work rules apply to a nonprofit independent school in the District of Columbia? {#nlrb-handbook-rules}

**Short answer.** The National Labor Relations Board applies a $1 million annual minimum to private and nonprofit schools, and where it has jurisdiction its 2023 *Stericycle* standard presumes unlawful a work rule that reasonably tends to chill employees' exercise of labor-law rights. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools-dc-hb] [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-dc-hb]

The Board states that it will not assert jurisdiction over employees of a religious organization who are involved in effectuating its religious purpose, such as teachers in church-operated schools. [^nlrb-jurisdiction-religious-dc-hb] Under *Stericycle*, an employer can rebut the presumption by proving that the rule advances a legitimate and substantial business interest that a more narrowly tailored rule could not advance. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-dc-hb] In *NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago*, the Court declined to construe the Act to reach teachers in church-operated schools absent a clear expression of congressional intent, because doing so would raise questions under the Religion Clauses. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools-dc-hb]

The Board's General Counsel stated on August 26, 2026 that the General Counsel is arguing to overturn *Stericycle* in a pending case, while directing regional offices to continue prosecuting cases under extant Board law. [^nlrb-gc-26-04-positions-dc-hb-work-rules] [^nlrb-gc-26-04-extant-law-dc-hb-work-rules] As of the November 23, 2026 review, the most recent Board record cited here on the standard itself is the Board's August 2, 2023 announcement of *Stericycle*, so the General Counsel's challenges to work rules at a secular nonprofit school that meets the $1 million minimum, including rules on confidentiality, social media and workplace conduct, proceed under that standard while the Board considers whether to overturn it. [^nlrb-stericycle-standard-dc-hb] [^nlrb-gc-26-04-extant-law-dc-hb-work-rules] [^nlrb-jurisdiction-schools-dc-hb] *Catholic Bishop* addressed teachers in church-operated schools, so it does not by itself remove a religious school's non-teaching staff from the Act. [^catholic-bishop-church-schools-dc-hb]

# School Policy Compliance in the District of Columbia: Employee Leave, Benefits and Tuition Remission[^about]

The leave and benefit rules a District of Columbia independent or religiously affiliated K–12 school's policies must reflect: accrued sick and safe leave, pregnancy and lactation accommodation, Universal Paid Leave coverage for school staff, the D.C. Family and Medical Leave Act and FMLA, leave and accommodation after domestic violence, school-event and Emancipation Day leave, jury service, voting leave, tuition remission and deductions from final pay.

## What paid sick and safe leave must a private school in the District of Columbia provide, and which employees does it cover? {#accrued-sick-and-safe-leave}

**Short answer.** The District's Accrued Sick and Safe Leave Act requires every employer to provide paid leave accruing at one hour for every 87, 43 or 37 hours worked, capped at three, five or seven days a year, for employers with up to 24, 25 to 99, or 100 or more employees. [^dc-assla-employer-defined] [^dc-assla-accrual-tiers]

The Act's definition of employer expressly includes a nonprofit corporation or association and sets no minimum number of employees, and employer size for the accrual tiers is the average monthly number of full-time equivalent employees in the prior calendar year. [^dc-assla-employer-defined] [^dc-assla-accrual-tiers] The employee definition excludes unpaid volunteers of an educational, charitable, religious or nonprofit organization, any "lay member elected or appointed to office within the discipline of any religious organization and engaged in religious functions"[^dc-assla-employee-defined], independent contractors and students as the Act defines them. [^dc-assla-employee-defined] The Act's substitute-teacher exclusion reaches only a substitute teacher or aide employed by District of Columbia Public Schools for thirty or fewer consecutive work days, so it does not reach a substitute at a private school. [^dc-assla-employee-defined] Leave accrues from the start of employment, but an employee may begin to use it after ninety days of service. [^dc-assla-accrual-start] An employee exempt from federal overtime pay as an executive, administrative or professional employee does not accrue leave for hours worked beyond a 40-hour week, [^dc-assla-exempt-accrual] and an employer need not provide the Act's paid leave to an employee who suffers no loss of income when absent, for up to the number of days the Act would otherwise provide. [^dc-assla-no-income-loss]

Accrued leave may be used for the employee's own illness, injury or medical condition, medical diagnosis or preventive care, the care of a family member with such a condition, and absences related to stalking, domestic violence or sexual abuse that are directly related to the listed social or legal services. [^dc-assla-uses] An employer may require reasonable certification for leave of three or more consecutive days, and information provided to the employer may not be disclosed except as the employee requests or consents, as a court or agency orders, or as other law requires. [^dc-assla-certification] An existing paid time off policy need not be changed if it lets employees accrue and use leave on terms at least equivalent to the statute, which is presumed when it accrues at least at the statutory rate and covers the same purposes, including unscheduled leave. [^dc-assla-pto-equivalence]

An employer's absence control policy may not count leave taken under the Act as an absence that may lead to discipline, and an adverse action within ninety days of a complaint or other listed protected activity raises a rebuttable presumption of a violation. [^dc-assla-retaliation] An employer that fails to allow an employee to use paid leave as the Act requires owes $500 in additional damages for each accrued day denied. [^dc-assla-500-per-day]

## What pregnancy and lactation accommodations must a District of Columbia school provide to its employees? {#pregnancy-and-lactation-accommodation}

**Short answer.** A District employer may not refuse to reasonably accommodate an employee's known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth, related medical conditions or breastfeeding unless it demonstrates that the accommodation would impose an undue hardship. [^dc-ppwfa-prohibitions]

The statute's examples of reasonable accommodation include more frequent or longer breaks, time off to recover from childbirth, light duty or a modified schedule, refraining from heavy lifting, private non-bathroom space for expressing breast milk and time off for pre-birth complications. [^dc-ppwfa-accommodation-defined] The employer may not take adverse action against an employee who requests or uses an accommodation, including by failing to reinstate the employee to the original or an equivalent job when the need ends. [^dc-ppwfa-prohibitions] The chapter's definitions section defines reasonable accommodation and undue hardship but does not define employer or set a minimum number of employees. [^dc-ppwfa-accommodation-defined] The employer must post a notice of rights in English and Spanish and give written notice to new employees and to an employee within ten days after she notifies the employer of her pregnancy or another covered condition. [^dc-ppwfa-notice]

The D.C. Human Rights Act separately requires an employer to treat an employee affected by pregnancy, childbirth, a related medical condition or breastfeeding the same for all employment-related purposes, including fringe benefits, as other employees similar in their ability or inability to work. [^dc-hra-pregnancy-equal-treatment] The Act requires reasonable daily unpaid break periods to express breast milk, running concurrently with any paid or unpaid break the employer already provides, unless the breaks would create an undue hardship, and reasonable efforts to provide a sanitary, private location near the work area other than a bathroom. [^dc-hra-lactation-breaks] The Human Rights Act's employer definition reaches any person who employs an individual for compensation, with no minimum number of employees. [^dc-hra-employer-defined] Its religious-organization provision states that "Nothing in this chapter shall be construed to bar"[^dc-hra-religious-organization-exception] a religious organization, or an educational organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in connection with one, from limiting employment to or preferring persons of the same religion. [^dc-hra-religious-organization-exception] That provision speaks to religion-based hiring preferences within the Human Rights Act; the pregnancy accommodation statute is a separate chapter of Title 32 of the D.C. Code. [^dc-hra-religious-organization-exception] [^dc-ppwfa-prohibitions] How the religious-organization provision bears on a religious school's other employment decisions is addressed in the [District of Columbia guide on discrimination law and religious employers](/practice-guides/school-policy-compliance/us/district-of-columbia-religious-employers).

Federal law adds two overlapping duties. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act requires an employer with fifteen or more employees to reasonably accommodate the known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions of a qualified employee unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship. [^dc-fed-usc-2000gg-1-pwfa] [^dc-fed-usc-2000gg-covered-entity] That Act is subject to Title VII's religious-employer provision in 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-1(a). [^dc-fed-usc-2000gg-5-religious] The federal PUMP Act provision requires reasonable break time each time the employee needs to express breast milk, for one year after the child's birth, and a private place other than a bathroom. [^dc-fed-usc-218d-lactation] An employer with fewer than fifty employees is exempt from the federal provision if compliance would impose an undue hardship by causing significant difficulty or expense in relation to the size, financial resources, nature or structure of its business; the District duties apply their own undue-hardship tests. [^dc-fed-usc-218d-small-employer] [^dc-hra-lactation-breaks]

## Do District of Columbia Universal Paid Leave benefits cover staff at a nonprofit or church-run school? {#universal-paid-leave}

**Short answer.** District Universal Paid Leave benefits cover employees of an employer that must pay District unemployment insurance, and the unemployment law reaches service for a nonprofit educational organization but excludes service for a church or for a primarily religious organization that a church operates, supervises, controls or principally supports. [^dc-upl-covered-employer] [^dc-ui-educational-and-nonprofit] [^dc-ui-church-exclusion]

The Universal Paid Leave law defines a covered employer as an employer that "is required to pay unemployment insurance on behalf of its employees by § 51-103"[^dc-upl-covered-employer], and a covered employee as one who spends more than half of his or her work time for that employer in the District, or whose employment is based in the District and who regularly spends a substantial amount of work time there and no more than half of that time in any other jurisdiction. [^dc-upl-covered-employer] The unemployment law treats as employment service for an educational organization and for a religious, charitable or other § 501(c)(3) organization, subject to the exclusions in paragraph (2)(A)(iv). [^dc-ui-educational-and-nonprofit] The Department of Employment Services states that a nonprofit required to pay District unemployment insurance tax is a covered employer, including one that reimburses benefits instead of paying quarterly tax. [^dc-does-nonprofit-covered] A covered employer contributes 0.75 percent of each covered employee's wages. [^dc-upl-contribution]

The exclusion in paragraph (2)(A)(iv) applies to service in the employ of a church or convention or association of churches, or of an organization "operated primarily for religious purposes and which is operated, supervised, controlled, or principally supported by a church or convention or association of churches"[^dc-ui-church-exclusion]. [^dc-ui-church-exclusion] The test has two parts, so a school with a religious affiliation that is not operated, supervised, controlled or principally supported by a church does not fall within its words. [^dc-ui-church-exclusion] The provision also excludes service by an ordained, commissioned or licensed minister in the exercise of the ministry and by a member of a religious order performing duties the order requires. [^dc-ui-church-exclusion] Because Universal Paid Leave coverage follows the obligation to pay unemployment insurance, a school whose employees' service falls within that exclusion is not, on the statute's words, a covered employer for those employees. [^dc-upl-covered-employer] [^dc-ui-church-exclusion]

For claims filed on or after October 1, 2026 and before October 1, 2030, the maximum benefit durations within a fifty-two-workweek period are twelve workweeks of parental leave, six of family leave, ten of medical leave and two of prenatal leave, and the twelve-week family and medical durations return for claims filed on or after October 1, 2030. [^dc-upl-durations] Benefits for any combination of leave events within fifty-two workweeks may not exceed the maximum parental-leave duration, except that prenatal leave may be added to parental leave, and combined prenatal and medical leave may not exceed the maximum medical-leave duration. [^dc-upl-combined-cap] The notice the Mayor prescribes must tell employees that an employee of a covered employer with fewer than twenty employees is not entitled to job protection when taking paid leave. [^dc-upl-notice-job-protection] Each covered employer must give that notice at hiring, annually and when it learns leave is needed, and must post it. [^dc-upl-notice-delivery] Retaliation against a person for requesting, applying for or using paid-leave benefits is unlawful. [^dc-upl-retaliation] Job-protected leave for an employer with twenty or more employees in the District comes from the D.C. Family and Medical Leave Act, as described in the [question on job-protected family and medical leave](#dc-fmla-and-fmla).

## Which job-protected family and medical leave laws apply to a private school in the District of Columbia? {#dc-fmla-and-fmla}

**Short answer.** The District of Columbia Family and Medical Leave Act applies to an employer with twenty or more employees in the District and entitles an eligible employee to sixteen workweeks of family leave and sixteen workweeks of medical leave, each within a twenty-four-month period. [^dc-fmla-applicability] [^dc-fmla-family-leave] [^dc-fmla-medical-leave]

An employee is eligible after twelve consecutive or non-consecutive months with the employer within the preceding seven years and at least 1,000 hours of work for the employer during that twelve-month period of employment. [^dc-fmla-definitions] The employer definition reaches any firm, association or corporation that uses another individual's services for pay in the District, with no exception for nonprofit or religious employers. [^dc-fmla-definitions] Family leave covers the birth or placement of a child and the care of a family member with a serious health condition, and leave for a birth or placement expires twelve months afterward. [^dc-fmla-family-leave] Medical leave covers the employee's own serious health condition that prevents the employee from performing the job, and either kind of leave may be unpaid, with elected paid leave counting against the sixteen weeks. [^dc-fmla-medical-leave] [^dc-fmla-unpaid]

During leave the employer must maintain group health coverage at the same level and on the same conditions as if the employee had continued working. [^dc-fmla-health-coverage] On return the employee must be restored to the same or an equivalent position. [^dc-fmla-restoration] An employer may deny restoration to a salaried employee among its five highest-paid employees, if it has fewer than fifty employees, or its highest-paid ten percent, if it has fifty or more, if it shows that denial is necessary to prevent substantial economic injury not directly related to the leave and gives notice of its intent and basis when it decides denial is necessary. [^dc-fmla-highest-paid] The economic-injury showing is not required where the employee's absence prevents the employer from completing a contract for work or services, failing to complete it will cause substantial economic injury, and reasonable attempts to find a temporary replacement failed. [^dc-fmla-highest-paid-contract]

The D.C. Act has its own rules for a private elementary or secondary school's employees who work principally in an instructional capacity. When such an employee elects foreseeable leave based on planned medical treatment or supervision that would cover more than 20 percent of the working days in the leave period, the school may require the employee to take the leave for periods of particular duration or to transfer temporarily to an available alternative position with equivalent pay and benefits. [^dc-fmla-school-employees] Near the end of an academic term, the school may require an instructional employee whose leave begins more than five weeks before the term ends, lasts at least three weeks and would end within the term's last three weeks to stay on leave through the end of the term, with shorter thresholds for leave that begins closer to the end of the term. [^dc-fmla-end-of-term]

The federal Family and Medical Leave Act covers a private elementary or secondary school regardless of its size, but an employee is eligible only if the school employs at least fifty employees within seventy-five miles of the employee's worksite. [^dc-fed-cfr-825-600-schools] [^dc-fed-usc-2611-75-mile] A federally eligible employee must also have worked for the employer for at least twelve months and 1,250 hours in the preceding twelve months, [^dc-fed-cfr-825-110-eligibility] and the federal regulations set special rules for instructional employees, such as teachers and coaches, on intermittent leave, reduced schedules and leave near the end of an academic term. [^dc-fed-cfr-825-600-instructional] Wage replacement during leave comes from Universal Paid Leave where the school's staff are covered, as explained in the [question on Universal Paid Leave](#universal-paid-leave).

## What leave and accommodation must a District of Columbia school give an employee who is a victim of domestic violence, sexual abuse or stalking? {#domestic-violence-leave-and-accommodation}

**Short answer.** A District employer must let an employee use accrued paid sick and safe leave for an absence related to stalking, domestic violence or sexual abuse of the employee or a family member, when the absence is directly related to the social or legal services the statute lists. [^dc-assla-safe-leave-uses]

The listed purposes are seeking medical attention, obtaining services from a victim services organization, counseling, relocating, taking legal action and taking other steps to enhance the safety of the employee, the family member or those who work with the employee. [^dc-assla-safe-leave-uses] Acceptable certification for safe leave of three or more consecutive days includes a police report, a court order or a signed statement from a victim and witness advocate or domestic violence counselor. [^dc-assla-safe-leave-certification]

The D.C. Human Rights Act separately makes it an unlawful discriminatory practice for an employer to refuse a reasonable accommodation for an employee who is a victim, or a family member of a victim, of domestic violence, a sexual offense or stalking when the accommodation is necessary to ensure the person's security and safety, unless it would cause undue hardship. [^dc-hra-dv-accommodation] The Act's examples of reasonable accommodation include a transfer, a modified schedule, leave, a changed work station or work telephone or email address, an installed lock and other safety procedures. [^dc-hra-dv-accommodation-defined] The Act also bars adverse treatment because an employee attended, prepared for or requested leave for a related legal proceeding, or sought related health treatment or counseling. [^dc-hra-dv-protected-activity] The employer may not disclose information the employee provides about that status except in listed cases, such as written authorization, a court order or legal requirement, a report to law enforcement, imminent harm to other employees or the extent needed to accommodate the employee, and it must notify the employee of any disclosure. [^dc-hra-dv-confidentiality] The Human Rights Act's religious-organization provision addresses limiting employment to, or preferring, persons of the same religion and does not mention this accommodation duty. [^dc-hra-religious-exception-dv]

## Must a District of Columbia school give employees time off for their own children's school events or for Emancipation Day? {#school-event-and-emancipation-day-leave}

**Short answer.** A District employer must give an employee who is a parent up to 24 hours of leave in any twelve-month period to attend or participate in a school-related event for the employee's child, and the leave may be unpaid unless the employee elects available paid leave. [^dc-parental-leave-entitlement]

The definition of employer reaches any individual, firm, association or corporation that uses an individual's services for pay in the District, with no minimum number of employees, and parent includes a legal custodian, a guardian, an aunt, uncle or grandparent and the spouse or domestic partner of any of them. [^dc-parental-leave-definitions] A school-related event is an activity sponsored by a school or an associated organization such as a parent-teacher association, including a performance, a school team's game or practice or a meeting with a teacher or counselor, and it must involve the child directly as participant or subject rather than as a spectator. [^dc-parental-leave-definitions]

The same section entitles every employee, parent or not, to a day of leave each year on April 16, District of Columbia Emancipation Day. [^dc-parental-leave-entitlement] The employer may deny either kind of leave only if granting it would disrupt the employer's business and make achieving production or service delivery unusually difficult, and the employee must give ten calendar days' notice unless the need to attend a school event cannot reasonably be foreseen. [^dc-parental-leave-entitlement] An employee who takes the leave may not lose any employment benefit or seniority accrued before or during it. [^dc-parental-leave-benefits] An employee may bring a civil action within one year after the violation occurs or is discovered. [^dc-parental-leave-civil-action]

## Must a District of Columbia school pay an employee who is serving on a jury, and can it penalize the absence? {#jury-service-leave}

**Short answer.** A District employer with more than ten employees must pay a full-time employee's usual compensation, less the jury fee, for jury service of five days or less, and no District employer may deprive an employee of employment because of jury service. [^dc-juror-pay] [^dc-juror-employment-protection]

The protection against penalties bars depriving an employee of employment, threatening or coercing the employee because the employee receives or responds to a jury summons, serves as a juror or attends court for prospective jury service. [^dc-juror-employment-protection] A violation is criminal contempt, punishable by a fine of up to $300, up to thirty days' imprisonment or both for a first offense, and an employee discharged in violation may sue within nine months for lost wages, reinstatement and damages, with reasonable attorney fees if the employee prevails. [^dc-juror-employment-protection] The pay entitlement applies to a juror employed full-time in the District, and an employer with ten or fewer employees is not required to pay it. [^dc-juror-pay] A person is not a full-time employed juror on a day for which the person would not have accrued regular wages from the employer or would not have worked more than half of a shift extending into another day, and an employee who is not paid as required may sue for the lost wages, with reasonable attorney fees if the employee prevails. [^dc-juror-pay]

## Must a District of Columbia school give employees paid time off to vote, and must it excuse students who vote? {#voting-leave}

**Short answer.** On an employee's request, a District employer must provide at least two hours of paid leave to vote in person, provided the employee would otherwise have been scheduled to work during that time. [^dc-voting-leave-employees]

The section defines employer as any person who employs an individual for compensation and employee as an employee who is eligible to vote, and, for an employee who is not eligible to vote in the District, the leave covers an election run by the jurisdiction where the employee is eligible to vote. [^dc-voting-leave-employees] The employer may require a reasonable advance request and may specify the hours, including during early voting instead of on election day or at the beginning or end of working hours. [^dc-voting-leave-employees] The employer may not deduct the leave from salary, wages or accrued leave, interfere with it or retaliate for taking it, and must post the notice developed by the Board of Elections. [^dc-voting-leave-employees]

The same section defines an educational institution to include an independent school, a private school and a parochial school in the District. [^dc-voting-leave-educational-institution] On the request of a student who is eligible to vote, the institution must provide an excused absence of at least two hours to vote in person in a District election or, for a student not eligible to vote in the District, in an election run by the jurisdiction where the student is eligible, at hours it may specify, including during early voting instead of on election day. [^dc-voting-leave-students]

## Is tuition remission for employees' children at a District of Columbia K–12 school excluded from federal income tax? {#employee-tuition-remission}

**Short answer.** Internal Revenue Code § 117(d) excludes from gross income an educational organization's tuition reduction for below-graduate education of an employee or the employee's dependent child, but a highly compensated employee's reduction qualifies only if offered on nondiscriminatory terms. [^dc-fed-irc-117-d-reduction] [^dc-fed-irc-132-h-dependent] [^dc-fed-irc-117-d-3-nondiscrimination]

The exclusion is limited to tuition reductions given by an organization described in § 170(b)(1)(A)(ii), which is an educational organization that normally maintains a regular faculty and curriculum and an enrolled student body at the place where it teaches. [^dc-fed-irc-117-d-reduction] [^dc-fed-irc-170-b-educational] A K–12 independent school that meets that description falls within the definition, and the education may be at the employer's own school or at another such organization. [^dc-fed-irc-117-d-reduction]

A reduction that does not qualify, such as one offered only to highly compensated employees, is not excluded by § 117(d) and falls within gross income as compensation for services, including fringe benefits. [^dc-fed-irc-117-d-3-nondiscrimination] [^dc-fed-irc-61-compensation] The highly compensated employee test uses the definition in Code § 414(q), [^dc-fed-irc-117-d-3-nondiscrimination] which covers a 5-percent owner in the current or preceding year and an employee whose preceding-year compensation exceeded an inflation-adjusted threshold, limited, if the employer so elects, to the top-paid group of employees. [^dc-fed-irc-414-q-hce] IRS Notice 2025-67 states that the threshold remains $160,000. [^dc-fed-irs-notice-2025-67] IRS Publication 970 describes a qualified tuition reduction as one for education below the graduate level, including primary and secondary school. [^dc-fed-irs-pub-970] The Treasury regulation describes a § 170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational organization as one whose primary function is the presentation of formal instruction, with a regular faculty, curriculum and enrolled student body, so a standalone pre-kindergarten or child care provider falls within it only if formal instruction, rather than custodial care, is its primary function. [^dc-fed-cfr-1-170a-9-instruction] This answer addresses federal income tax only; the treatment of tuition remission under the District's income tax is outside its scope.

## Can a District of Columbia school deduct a signing bonus or training repayment from a teacher's final paycheck? {#repayment-from-final-pay}

**Short answer.** The District's Attorney General takes the position that, absent statutory authorization, a District employer may require an employee to repay training costs only for training that primarily benefits the worker, such as a portable certification or credential. [^dc-oag-trap-position]

The Office stated that position in a November 2025 release announcing settlements, in which it also alleged that training repayment provisions violated the District's antitrust laws and its ban on non-compete agreements because they deterred workers from seeking other jobs. [^dc-oag-trap-position] [^dc-oag-noncompete-allegation] These are the Office's position and allegations, not a court's ruling.

The Wage Payment and Collection Law defines wages as all monetary compensation "after lawful deductions"[^dc-wpcl-wages-defined] and expressly includes a bonus. [^dc-wpcl-wages-defined] That law, D.C. Code §§ 32-1301 to 32-1312, contains no list of permitted deductions, so including a bonus in wages does not itself authorize deducting a disputed bonus or training repayment, and no provision of the law may be contravened or set aside by private agreement. [^dc-wpcl-no-waiver] A discharged employee must be paid wages earned by the next working day, and an employee who resigns and does not have a written contract of employment for a period of more than thirty days must be paid by the next regular payday or within seven days, whichever is earlier, and an employer may take up to four days after a discharge or resignation to check the accounts of an employee responsible for the employer's money. [^dc-wpcl-final-pay] A teacher on a written contract of more than thirty days is outside the resignation deadline's terms. [^dc-wpcl-final-pay] Late final wages carry liquidated damages of ten percent of the unpaid wages per working day or treble the unpaid wages, whichever is smaller. [^dc-wpcl-final-pay] In a civil action an aggrieved employee who prevails recovers back wages unlawfully withheld, liquidated damages equal to treble the unpaid wages, statutory penalties and reasonable attorneys' fees and costs. [^dc-wpcl-civil-action] Whether a separate repayment promise is enforceable as a contract claim against the former employee is a different question that this guide does not cover.

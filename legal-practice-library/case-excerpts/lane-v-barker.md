## Quoted passages

### lane-v-barker-here-the-record-reveals-that-the-afcd70df {#lane-v-barker-here-the-record-reveals-that-the-afcd70df}

> Here, the record reveals that the parents were present at their home when the altercation took place and there is evidence from which it could be inferred that they knew or should have known that as many as one half of the 75 to 100 guests were minors, many of whom were drinking. Under the circumstances, a question of fact exists as to whether it was foreseeable “that someone would get drunk at the party, engage in a fight, and cause injury to a third party” (Comeau v Lucas, 90 AD2d 674, 675).

### lane-v-barker-inasmuch-as-general-obligations-law-52dfd30e {#lane-v-barker-inasmuch-as-general-obligations-law-52dfd30e}

> Inasmuch as General Obligations Law § 11-100 mandates that a person “furnish” or “procure” alcoholic beverages as a predicate for liability, Supreme Court erred in denying summary judgment as to the parents with regard to the third cause of action of plaintiffs complaint (see, e.g., Rust v Reyer, 235 AD2d 413; Pelinsky v Rockensies, 209 AD2d 392).

### lane-v-barker-it-is-clear-from-the-record-57ae1ed6 {#lane-v-barker-it-is-clear-from-the-record-57ae1ed6}

> It is clear from the record that the parents authorized their son, an adult, to host a party at their residence and that they knew alcohol would be consumed at the affair. The record further reveals that they knew or should have known that minors would be in attendance and that they instructed their son to be sure that there was no underage drinking.

### lane-v-barker-the-record-makes-plain-that-he-4a01c51d {#lane-v-barker-the-record-makes-plain-that-he-4a01c51d}

> The record makes plain that he procured and furnished alcoholic beverages for the party, and there exists a question of fact as to whether Barker was intoxicated at the time of the underlying altercation. Thus, Supreme Court quite properly denied the motion for summary judgment as to Todd Keehfus.

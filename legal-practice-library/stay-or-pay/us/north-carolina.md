# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in North Carolina[^about]

North Carolina has no statute aimed at private-employer stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment clause is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while the Wage and Hour Act's withholding rules and minimum-wage floor limit taking the money out of pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in North Carolina? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** No North Carolina statute specifically governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or other employee repayment agreements in private employment. The Wage and Hour Act preserves an employer's civil action to collect amounts the employee owes [^nc-sop-employer-civil-action], so whether a repayment term can be enforced turns on ordinary contract rules. Where the term fixes the amount owed on a breach, the North Carolina Supreme Court enforces liquidated damages but not a penalty [^nc-sop-knutton-liquidated-not-penalty].

"[A] stipulated sum is for liquidated damages only (1) where the damages which the parties might reasonably anticipate are difficult to ascertain because of their indefiniteness or uncertainty and (2) where the amount stipulated is either a reasonable estimate of the damages which would probably be caused by a breach or is reasonably proportionate to the damages which have actually been caused by the breach."[^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test]

In *Knutton v. Cofield*, the court applied that two-part test to decide whether a stipulated sum is liquidated damages or a penalty [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test]. Both requirements must be met: the damages the parties could anticipate from the breach must be difficult to ascertain, and the stipulated amount must be a reasonable estimate of the probable damages or reasonably proportionate to the actual damages [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test]. Evidence of what the employer spent on training or recruiting does not by itself establish both requirements [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test].

A payment triggered by competitive work is not always treated as a non-compete. In *Eastern Carolina Internal Medicine, P.A. v. Faidas*, a physician's employment contract required a cost-sharing payment if she practiced within a three-county area after leaving, and the Court of Appeals held that the provision did not prohibit her from practicing but only required a payment for making that choice [^nc-sop-faidas-payment-not-prohibition]. The court therefore held that the provision was not a covenant not to compete and did not apply the strict reasonableness scrutiny required for one [^nc-sop-faidas-not-a-covenant]. The court then rejected the argument that the payment was an unenforceable penalty [^nc-sop-faidas-penalty-rejected], stating that a liquidated-damages clause that is reasonable in amount is enforceable and is not a penalty [^nc-sop-faidas-reasonable-clause-enforceable].

Where a repayment term does operate as a restraint, the covenant tests apply. The North Carolina Supreme Court enforces a covenant not to compete only if it is in writing, part of an employment contract, based on valuable consideration, reasonable as to time and territory, and not against public policy [^nc-sop-whittaker-covenant-elements], and the Court of Appeals will not enforce a restraint that is too broad to be a reasonable protection of the employer's business [^nc-sop-hartman-too-broad]. No North Carolina appellate decision located for this guide applies those tests, or the *Faidas* reasoning, to a training-repayment or bonus-repayment clause. The conservative course is to draft the clause to meet both sets of requirements: for a stipulated amount, the two *Knutton* requirements [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test], and for a clause that operates as a restraint, a writing in the employment contract, valuable consideration, reasonable time and territory, and consistency with public policy [^nc-sop-whittaker-covenant-elements].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment schedule that fixes an amount without relating it to the anticipated or actual loss from an early departure risks being struck as a penalty [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test][^nc-sop-knutton-liquidated-not-penalty].

## Can a North Carolina employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No North Carolina appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a bonus or other payment already made can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. The conservative course is to collect a disputed repayment through a civil action, which the Wage and Hour Act preserves [^nc-sop-employer-civil-action-clawback], rather than by withholding wages. Taking the money out of wages is limited separately, because for the wage-payment sections a promised bonus is a wage [^nc-sop-wage-includes-bonuses] and an employer may withhold wages only on a ground G.S. 95-25.8 allows, such as the employee's written authorization [^nc-sop-withholding-authorization-clawback].

For the wage-payment sections, G.S. 95-25.6 through 95-25.13, a wage includes bonuses and other amounts promised when the employer has a policy or practice of making such payments [^nc-sop-wage-includes-bonuses]. The Department of Labor's rule lists moving expenses and educational expenses among the other amounts promised [^nc-sop-promised-moving-educational]. Once a promised amount is due, it must be paid on the regular payday [^nc-sop-regular-payday], and keeping it back to offset a repayment debt is a withholding that needs a G.S. 95-25.8 ground [^nc-sop-withholding-authorization-clawback].

## Can a North Carolina employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A North Carolina employer may deduct a repayment balance from the final paycheck if the employee's G.S. 95-25.8(a) written authorization provides for deducting the unpaid balance at separation, subject to the withholding limits of G.S. 95-25.8(b) [^nc-sop-final-paycheck-balance-rule]. Those limits allow a withholding for the employer's benefit to reduce wages only to the minimum wage level and bar any reduction of overtime wages owed [^nc-sop-minimum-wage-limit].

"If the employer obtains such an authorization, the employer may deduct as much of the balance possible from the final paycheck without having to give the employee notice of the amount and a reasonable opportunity to withdraw his or her authorization as required by G.S. 95-25.8(a), subject to the withholding limitations of G.S. 95-25.8(b)."[^nc-sop-final-paycheck-balance-rule]

The authorization itself must meet G.S. 95-25.8(a). For a deduction whose amount or rate is known and agreed in advance, the employee's written authorization must be signed on or before the payday, state the reason, and state the dollar amount or percentage to be deducted [^nc-sop-withholding-authorization]. Ordinarily, for a deduction whose amount is not known in advance, the employee must also receive advance written notice of the actual amount, written notice of the right to withdraw the authorization, and a reasonable opportunity to withdraw it in writing [^nc-sop-unknown-amount-notice], except that an authorization providing for the balance at separation permits the final-paycheck deduction without those notice and withdrawal steps [^nc-sop-final-paycheck-balance-rule]. The monetary limits and time requirements of G.S. 95-25.8 cannot be waived [^nc-sop-limits-not-waivable].

The statute treats some sums differently. A bona fide overpayment of wages, a wage advance made at the employee's request and the principal of an employer loan are prepayment of wages that may be deducted notwithstanding subsections (a) and (b) [^nc-sop-prepayment-of-wages]. No North Carolina decision located for this guide decides whether a training-repayment obligation is the principal of a loan for that purpose, so the conservative course is to obtain a written authorization that meets G.S. 95-25.8(a) and to respect the subsection (b) floor.

Timing still runs while the deduction question is open: a separated employee must be paid all wages due on or before the next regular payday [^nc-sop-final-pay-timing]. An employer that withholds for a purpose not permitted by law violates G.S. 95-25.6 or 95-25.7 even if the employee signed an authorization, because that authorization is invalid [^nc-sop-invalid-authorization].

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> An ordinary repayment deduction for the employer's benefit that does not meet the applicable G.S. 95-25.8 authorization or notice requirements, or that takes a workweek's pay below the minimum wage level, leaves the employer liable for the wages unlawfully withheld plus interest and an equal amount of liquidated damages based on the wages found due, which the court may reduce or deny only if the employer shows both good faith and reasonable grounds for believing it was not violating the Act [^nc-sop-unpaid-wages-liability][^nc-sop-wage-act-liquidated-damages].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in North Carolina? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** North Carolina has no statute that gives private-employer training, tuition or relocation repayment a separate rule. Those terms are tested under the same contract and wage-withholding rules as a bonus repayment [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test-training][^nc-sop-withholding-authorization-training]. The one training-repayment statute located for this guide is limited to State Troopers: the State may require a trainee to agree in writing to reimburse part of the training cost if the trainee becomes a State Trooper but does not remain one for 36 months [^nc-sop-trooper-reimbursement].

The trooper statute fixes the reimbursable amount at $36,000 less $1,000 for each month served [^nc-sop-trooper-reimbursement], and it bars enforcement where the Commander finds it impossible for the individual to serve because of death, health-related reasons or other hardship [^nc-sop-trooper-hardship]. The statute does not establish a general rule for private-employer repayment agreements, although subsection (d) makes a municipal law enforcement agency, a sheriff's office or a certified company police agency that hires, within six months, a trooper who separates before completing 36 months of post-training service liable to the State for $36,000 [^nc-sop-trooper-hiring-entity]. A pending bill would add one: H.B. 973, filed in the 2025-2026 session, provides that a training-repayment agreement is prohibited and unenforceable unless it requires repayment only of the cost of special training, lasts no longer than two years after that training and prorates the repayment [^nc-sop-hb973-training-repayment]. The bill's last recorded action is referral to the House Rules committee on April 14, 2025 [^nc-sop-hb973-status], so it is not current law.

Where the federal minimum-wage rules apply, a required repayment is also measured against them. Under the federal rule, wages count as paid only when they are paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^nc-sop-flsa-free-and-clear], and the wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back the whole or part of the wage to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^nc-sop-flsa-kickback]. The rule illustrates the point with employer-required tools: there is a violation in any workweek when their cost cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^nc-sop-flsa-tools-workweek]. No federal rule quoted here addresses training repayment specifically, so the conservative course is to keep any repayment taken from wages from cutting into a workweek's minimum or overtime wages. North Carolina's own minimum-wage section is the higher of $6.15 an hour or the federal minimum wage [^nc-sop-state-minimum-wage], but it does not apply to a person employed in an enterprise covered by the Fair Labor Standards Act [^nc-sop-flsa-enterprise-exclusion]. That exclusion names the minimum-wage, overtime and youth-employment sections, not the withholding limits of G.S. 95-25.8, which still cap a withholding for the employer's benefit at the minimum wage level [^nc-sop-minimum-wage-limit-training].

## How are North Carolina repayment and wage-withholding rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employer collects a repayment debt through a civil action, which the Wage and Hour Act expressly preserves [^nc-sop-employer-civil-action-enforcement]. On the employee's side, wages withheld in violation of the Act are recoverable with interest [^nc-sop-unpaid-wages-liability-enforcement], plus an equal amount as liquidated damages unless the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds [^nc-sop-wage-act-liquidated-damages-enforcement].

The court may also order the employer to pay costs and reasonable attorneys' fees [^nc-sop-attorneys-fees]. The Commissioner of Labor may bring the action at the request of the employees affected [^nc-sop-commissioner-action], and an action under the section must be brought within two years [^nc-sop-two-year-limit].

## What repayment structure is safest under North Carolina law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A stipulated repayment amount is enforceable as liquidated damages only where the damages from an early departure are difficult to ascertain and the amount is a reasonable estimate of the probable damages or reasonably proportionate to the actual damages [^nc-sop-knutton-penalty-test-safest]. For a deduction whose amount or rate is known and agreed in advance, G.S. 95-25.8(a) requires the employee's written authorization, signed on or before the payday for the pay period of the deduction, stating the reason and the dollar amount or percentage to be deducted [^nc-sop-withholding-authorization-safest], and a provision in it for deducting the balance at separation allows a final-paycheck deduction [^nc-sop-final-paycheck-balance-rule-safest]. An ordinary repayment deduction for the employer's benefit may not take a workweek's wages below the minimum wage level or reduce overtime wages owed [^nc-sop-minimum-wage-limit-safest]; the statute's separate exception for wage overpayments, wage advances and employer-loan principal applies notwithstanding those limits [^nc-sop-prepayment-of-wages-safest]. Where those conditions cannot be met, paying final wages in full and suing on the repayment term avoids liquidated-damages exposure under the Wage and Hour Act [^nc-sop-employer-civil-action-safest][^nc-sop-wage-act-liquidated-damages-safest].

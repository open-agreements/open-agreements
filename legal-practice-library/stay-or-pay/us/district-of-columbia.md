# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in the District of Columbia[^about]

The District of Columbia has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is a contract question tested against the common-law rule that a liquidated damages clause disproportionate to foreseeable damages is void as a penalty; the District's non-compete ban governs only provisions that prohibit working for another or running a business, and the Wage Payment and Collection Law requires final wages on a short deadline and bars private agreements that set its protections aside.

## Can a District of Columbia employer make an employee repay a bonus or training cost for leaving early? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Under District of Columbia common law, a liquidated damages clause is void as a penalty if its amount is disproportionate to the damages reasonably foreseeable when the contract was made [^dc-sop-cablevision-penalty-rule], and applying that rule to a fixed repayment due when an employee leaves early is an inference. No District statute governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment terms in private employment, and no D.C. Court of Appeals decision located for this guide applies the penalty rule to an employee repayment term.

"For a liquidated damages clause to be valid and enforceable, and not void as a penalty, the common law insists that ‘the liquidated damages must not be disproportionate to the level of damages reasonably foreseeable at the time of the making of the contract.’"[^dc-sop-cablevision-penalty-rule]

In *District Cablevision Ltd. Partnership v. Bassin*, the D.C. Court of Appeals stated that rule [^dc-sop-cablevision-penalty-rule] and added that a clause designed to make the other party's default more profitable to the drafter than performance would be is void as a penalty [^dc-sop-cablevision-profitable-default]. The court explained that its skepticism of liquidated damages is at its height where one party with greater bargaining power unilaterally imposes the clause in an adhesive contract [^dc-sop-cablevision-adhesion]. In *Falconi-Sachs v. LPF Senate Square, LLC*, the court treated whether a fixed charge was disproportionate to the drafter's reasonably anticipated damages, and thus an improper penalty, as a factual question [^dc-sop-falconi-factual-question]. Both decisions involved consumer charges, not employment terms, so applying them to a stay-or-pay amount is an inference. An employer's form repayment agreement presented to a new hire resembles the unilaterally imposed term the court scrutinized most closely, and the conservative course is to set any fixed amount by reference to a documented estimate of the employer's loss from an early departure.

The District's Attorney General takes a narrower view of training repayment. In announcing 2025 settlements with a hospital and a preschool, the Office of the Attorney General stated that, absent statutory authorization, District employers can require repayment of training costs only for training that primarily benefits the worker, such as a portable certification or credential [^dc-sop-oag-training-position], and it alleged that repayment terms triggered by resignation violated the District's antitrust laws and non-compete ban because they deterred workers from seeking other jobs [^dc-sop-oag-noncompete-allegation]. Those are the Attorney General's enforcement positions and allegations in settled matters, not court holdings, but they show how the District's enforcer reads a stay-or-pay term.

A void clause does not leave the employer without any remedy: the court held that invalidating a liquidated damages provision does not deprive the non-breaching party of a remedy for the breach [^dc-sop-cablevision-actual-damages], so an employer may still try to prove its actual damages.

> **Drafting note.**
>
> A single repayment figure that does not decline with service and is disproportionate to the employer's reasonably foreseeable loss from an early departure risks being void as a penalty under the disproportion test [^dc-sop-cablevision-penalty-rule].

## Can a District of Columbia employer take back a bonus it already paid? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No District statute or D.C. Court of Appeals decision located for this guide decides whether an employer may recover a bonus it has already paid. The Wage Payment and Collection Law defines wages to include a bonus [^dc-sop-1301-wages-bonus], and its protections cannot be contravened or set aside by private agreement [^dc-sop-1305-no-private-waiver].

"‘Wages’ means all monetary compensation after lawful deductions, owed by an employer, whether the amount owed is determined on a time, task, piece, commission, or other basis of calculation. The term ‘wages’ includes a: (A) Bonus; (B) Commission; (C) Fringe benefits paid in cash; (D) Overtime premium; and (E) Other remuneration promised or owed: (i) Pursuant to a contract for employment, whether written or oral; (ii) Pursuant to a contract between an employer and another person or entity; or (iii) Pursuant to District or federal law."[^dc-sop-1301-wages-bonus]

Once a bonus is owed, it is a wage [^dc-sop-1301-wages-bonus], and an employer must pay all wages earned on regular paydays at least twice each calendar month, or monthly for bona fide administrative, executive and professional employees, and an employer that has paid monthly by contract or custom may continue to do so [^dc-sop-1302-paydays]. A repayment amount that functions as liquidated damages for leaving early is void as a penalty if it is disproportionate to the damages reasonably foreseeable when the contract was made [^dc-sop-cablevision-clawback-penalty]. The Attorney General has also challenged a preschool's contracts that required teachers who resigned early to repay bonuses they had earned, as well as training costs [^dc-sop-oag-bonus-repayment].

Equity and other long-term awards are treated differently under the non-compete ban. The Ban on Non-Compete Agreements Amendment Act excludes from the definition of a non-compete provision an otherwise lawful provision that provides a long-term incentive [^dc-sop-581-01-long-term-incentive], and defines a long-term incentive to include bonuses, equity compensation and other performance-driven incentives typically earned over more than one year [^dc-sop-581-01-lti-definition]. That exclusion removes such provisions from the non-compete ban; it does not decide whether a forfeiture or clawback is enforceable as a contract.

## Can a District of Columbia employer keep a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Withholding a repayment from final wages in the District of Columbia risks a wage claim, because the Attorney General has alleged that final-paycheck repayment deductions violated the District's wage and hour laws [^dc-sop-oag-deduction-allegation]. The Wage Payment and Collection Law defines wages as compensation after lawful deductions without listing which deductions are lawful [^dc-sop-1301-wages-lawful-deductions]. No D.C. Code section or D.C. Court of Appeals decision located for this guide authorizes deducting an employee's repayment debt on the strength of a signed authorization, and the statute provides that its protections cannot be contravened or set aside by private agreement [^dc-sop-1305-final-pay-no-waiver].

"Whenever an employer discharges an employee, the employer shall pay the employee’s wages earned not later than the working day following such discharge;"[^dc-sop-1303-discharge-deadline]

The deadlines are short unless a collective agreement with a bona fide union specifies otherwise. A discharged employee's earned wages are due by the next working day [^dc-sop-1303-discharge-deadline], with a 4-day period to check the accounts of an employee responsible for the employer's money [^dc-sop-1303-accounts-period], and an employee without a written contract for more than 30 days who quits must be paid by the next regular payday or within 7 days, whichever is earlier [^dc-sop-1303-resignation-deadline]. If the employer disputes the amount in good faith, it must give written notice of the amount it concedes and pay that amount without condition within those deadlines [^dc-sop-1304-conceded-wages].

A deduction also cannot take pay below the District minimum wage, which the employer must pay for all hours a covered employee works [^dc-sop-1003-minimum-wage-all-hours]. Under federal law, wages kicked back to the employer for its benefit do not count toward the Fair Labor Standards Act's wage requirements [^dc-sop-fed-531-35-kickback], so an employer-required cost borne by the employee violates the Act in any workweek in which it cuts into the required minimum or overtime wages [^dc-sop-fed-531-35-overtime]. The conservative course is to pay final wages in full on time and collect any repayment by separate demand.

> **Drafting note.**
>
> A final-pay deduction clause that treats the employee's signature as sufficient authorization risks a wage claim when the employer withholds the repayment balance [^dc-sop-oag-deduction-allegation].

## Does the District of Columbia treat training or tuition repayment differently from a bonus repayment? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No District statute treats training, tuition or relocation repayment differently from a bonus repayment. Where a repayment amount functions as liquidated damages for leaving early, the common-law rule voids it as a penalty if it is disproportionate to the damages reasonably foreseeable when the contract was made [^dc-sop-cablevision-training-rule], though applying that rule to employee repayment terms is an inference. The District's non-compete ban reaches a different kind of term: it defines a non-compete provision as one that prohibits an employee from performing work for another for pay or from operating the employee's own business [^dc-sop-581-01-noncompete-definition], and it does not mention repayment.

"Beginning October 1, 2022, no employer may require or request that a covered employee sign an agreement or comply with a workplace policy that includes a non-compete provision."[^dc-sop-581-02-ban]

The ban covers employees who are not highly compensated [^dc-sop-581-01-covered-employee], and a non-compete provision in an agreement entered on or after October 1, 2022 with a covered employee is void and unenforceable [^dc-sop-581-02-void]. The Department of Employment Services' 2026 notice states that the ban applies to employees earning less than $162,164 and to medical specialists earning less than $270,274 [^dc-sop-does-2026-thresholds]. For a highly compensated employee, a non-compete agreement must specify its functional scope and geography, run no more than 365 days after separation (730 for a medical specialist), and be given at least 14 days before the employee starts or must sign [^dc-sop-581-03-hce-limits].

No District court decision located for this guide decides whether a repayment obligation is a non-compete provision under the Act. The Attorney General has taken the position that it can be: it alleged that training repayment terms triggered by resignation violated the non-compete ban because they deterred workers from seeking other jobs [^dc-sop-oag-training-allegation], and it stated that, absent statutory authorization, an employer can require repayment of training costs only for training that primarily benefits the worker, such as a portable certification or credential [^dc-sop-oag-training-portable]. For a covered employee, the conservative course is to limit any training repayment to training of that kind.

## What can a District of Columbia employee recover if an employer withholds final pay for a repayment? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A District of Columbia employee whose wages are withheld unlawfully may sue and, on prevailing, recover back wages, liquidated damages equal to treble the unpaid wages, statutory penalties and reasonable attorneys' fees and costs [^dc-sop-1308-civil-action]. An employer that misses the final-pay deadlines owes additional liquidated damages of 10 percent of the unpaid wages per working day, or treble the unpaid wages, whichever is smaller [^dc-sop-1303-late-pay-damages].

The action must be brought within 3 years after the cause of action accrued, or after the last occurrence if the violation is continuous [^dc-sop-1308-limitations]. The Attorney General may also sue for restitution and, on behalf of an aggrieved employee, back wages and treble liquidated damages [^dc-sop-1306-attorney-general].

Separate remedies apply to the non-compete ban. An employer that attempts to enforce a void non-compete provision is liable to each employee against whom it tried to enforce the provision for not less than $1,500 [^dc-sop-581-04-enforcement-relief], and an aggrieved person may file an administrative complaint with the Mayor or a civil action [^dc-sop-581-04-complaint].

## What repayment structure is safest under District of Columbia law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** The safest District of Columbia repayment term covers only training that primarily benefits the employee, such as a portable certification or credential, following the Attorney General's stated position [^dc-sop-oag-structure-portable]. Its amount should not be disproportionate to the employer's reasonably foreseeable loss, because a clause disproportionate to foreseeable damages is void as a penalty [^dc-sop-cablevision-structure-rule]. A schedule that reduces the amount as the employee serves the commitment keeps the charge closer to the loss that remains, though no District decision located for this guide requires proration.

A departure trigger does not by itself avoid the non-compete ban. The statute defines a non-compete provision as one that prohibits working for another for pay or operating one's own business [^dc-sop-581-01-structure-definition], and the Attorney General has alleged that repayment terms triggered by resignation violated the ban because they deterred workers from seeking other jobs [^dc-sop-oag-structure-allegation]. Collecting the balance by separate demand, rather than withholding it from wages, avoids the payroll-deduction question: the Wage Payment and Collection Law permits only lawful deductions from wages [^dc-sop-1301-structure-lawful-deductions], the parties cannot set the statute aside by agreement [^dc-sop-1305-structure-no-waiver], and the Attorney General has alleged that final-paycheck repayment deductions violated the District's wage and hour laws [^dc-sop-oag-structure-deduction]. Separate collection does not settle whether the repayment obligation itself is enforceable.

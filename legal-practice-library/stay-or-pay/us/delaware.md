# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Delaware[^about]

Delaware has no generally applicable statute aimed at stay-or-pay terms in private employment, so repayment agreements are tested as contracts under the penalty rule, while the Wage Payment and Collection Act separately limits collecting the debt from pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Delaware? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Delaware has no generally applicable statute that bans or limits stay-or-pay, training-repayment or sign-on bonus repayment terms in private employment, so a Delaware court tests the repayment amount under the contract rule that separates enforceable liquidated damages from an unenforceable penalty [^de-swier-penalty-distinction]. For agreements among physicians, 6 Del. C. § 2707 voids a covenant restricting practice in a locale or for a period after termination, but keeps enforceable a provision requiring damages reasonably related to the injury suffered by reason of termination, which may include damages related to competition [^de-2707-physician-termination-damages]. Under that rule a penalty is void as against public policy and recovery is limited to actual damages, while a true liquidated damages provision is enforced according to its terms [^de-swier-penalty-distinction].
The Delaware test comes from *Lee Builders v. Wells*: where the damages are uncertain and the amount agreed upon is reasonable, the agreement will not be disturbed [^de-brazen-lee-builders-test]. The Delaware Supreme Court applied that test to an employment agreement in *Delaware Bay Surgical Services, P.A. v. Swier*, which required a physician who left early to pay $25,000 in recognition of the expenses the employer incurred in hiring him and introducing him to the medical community [^de-swier-early-termination-clause]. The court upheld the payment because the evidence showed that $25,000 was a reasonable forecast, rationally related to those expenses and not unconscionable [^de-swier-reasonable-forecast].
In *Troy Ventures, LLC v. Kosloski*, the Superior Court granted summary judgment to employers on an employee repayment agreement whose repayment obligation was triggered when the employee gave notice of his intention to resign, holding the agreement valid and binding [^de-kosloski-resignation-trigger][^de-kosloski-agreement-binding]. The same opinion held that a $5,000 liquidated damages provision in the employment agreement was a reasonable forecast and not a penalty [^de-kosloski-reasonable-forecast].
A repayment term that is triggered by going to work for a competitor raises a separate question. In *Cantor Fitzgerald, L.P. v. Ainslie*, the Delaware Supreme Court distinguished a penalty enforced against an employee for breaching a restrictive covenant from a condition that excuses the employer from paying a deferred benefit [^de-cantor-condition-not-penalty]. A repayment framed as a penalty for breaching a restrictive covenant falls outside that condition rationale [^de-cantor-condition-not-penalty], and an employer enforcing an overbroad covenant should not expect a court to narrow it: in *Sunder Energy, LLC v. Jackson*, the Delaware Supreme Court held that the Court of Chancery did not abuse its discretion by refusing to blue pencil the restrictive covenants [^de-sunder-no-blue-pencil].

"The distinction between a penalty and a liquidated damages clause is significant — if a provision is considered a penalty, it is void as against public policy and recovery is limited to actual damages; if the provision is a true liquidated damages provision, it will be enforced according to its terms."[^de-swier-penalty-distinction]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A flat repayment amount that the employer cannot tie to any estimate of its actual training or hiring costs risks being treated as a penalty, which limits recovery to the actual damages the employer can prove [^de-swier-penalty-distinction][^de-swier-reasonable-forecast].

## Can a Delaware employer claw back pay already received? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** Delaware enforces a forfeiture-for-competition provision requiring an employee who voluntarily resigns to return equity benefits already received as an ordinary contract term, subject to ordinary contract defenses [^de-lkq-voluntary-termination][^de-lkq-employee-choice][^de-lkq-already-received]. Separately, an employer may not recover wages by withholding them from pay outside the deductions the Wage Payment and Collection Act allows [^de-1107-withholding-clawback].
In *LKQ Corp. v. Rutledge*, the Delaware Supreme Court held that the rule of *Cantor Fitzgerald*, under which a forfeiture-for-competition provision is enforced as an ordinary contract term rather than reviewed for reasonableness, is not limited to limited partnerships [^de-lkq-employee-choice][^de-lkq-not-limited-partnership]. The court added that the fact that the restricted stock unit agreements required the return of benefits already received did not alter its analysis [^de-lkq-already-received]. The court left open whether a clawback so extreme in duration and financial hardship that it precludes an unsophisticated employee's choice should be reviewed for reasonableness [^de-lkq-extreme-clawback-reserved].
Those decisions concern equity awards and partnership distributions, and in our review we found no Delaware appellate decision applying them to a cash sign-on bonus, a retention bonus or a training-cost repayment, so the conservative course for those terms is to assume the *Swier* penalty test applies [^de-lkq-extreme-clawback-reserved][^de-swier-penalty-clawback]. Under that test an agreed amount stands where the damages are uncertain and the amount is reasonable, and *Swier* upheld an amount the evidence showed was a reasonable forecast of the employer's hiring expenses, so the repayment amount should rest on a documented estimate of the employer's costs [^de-brazen-test-clawback][^de-swier-forecast-clawback]. Whatever the contract says, the Act provides that no provision of the wage-payment chapter may be contravened or set aside by private agreement [^de-1110-no-private-waiver].

"B. The fact that the LKQ RSU Agreements require the return of benefits already received does not alter our analysis."[^de-lkq-already-received]

## Can repayment come out of a Delaware final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Delaware employer may take a repayment out of wages, including final wages, only within the deductions section 1107 allows, which for a repayment means a signed authorization by the employee for a lawful purpose that benefits the employee [^de-1103-final-pay-compliance][^de-1107-withholding-limits]. Final wages must be paid in compliance with sections 1104 and 1107 [^de-1103-final-pay-compliance].
The Department of Labor's deduction regulation, which the Department amended by an order published in the June 2026 Delaware Register and effective 10 days after publication, narrows that authorization [^de-1328-amendment-effective-date]. An employee-authorized deduction must be expressly authorized in writing, for the employee's benefit, and given voluntarily only after the employee receives written notice of the payment's terms and the manner of deduction [^de-1328-voluntary-written-authorization]. A cash advance or charges for goods or services may be repaid through payroll only under a written agreement signed by both parties that specifies the amount, the repayment schedule and the method, and no such agreement may provide for repayment of more than 15% of the employee's gross wages per pay period [^de-1328-advance-signed-agreement][^de-1328-advance-repayment-cap]. A balance above 15% of gross wages may be withheld from final compensation only if the original agreement included that arrangement [^de-1328-final-pay-advance-balance]. Cash or inventory shortages, and losses from damage to the employer's or a customer's property, may not be deducted in any case [^de-1328-shortage-bar][^de-1328-damage-bar]. The regulation does not prevent an employee from voluntarily reimbursing an employer [^de-1328-voluntary-reimbursement]. Separately, the minimum wage is $15.00 per hour from January 1, 2025, and an agreement to work for less than the minimum wage is no defense to the employee's action for the shortfall [^de-902-minimum-wage-floor][^de-911-agreement-no-defense].
In *Swier*, decided in 2006, the Delaware Supreme Court held that when an employee leaves owing an employment-related debt, the employer may deduct the debt from final wages where reasonable grounds for a dispute arising out of the employment contract exist [^de-swier-reasonable-grounds][^de-swier-final-wage-offset]. The current version of section 1103 ties the reasonable-grounds defense to disputes under sections 1104 and 1107 and requires final wages to be paid in compliance with those sections [^de-1103-final-pay-compliance][^de-1103-reasonable-grounds-current]; in our review we found no Delaware appellate decision applying *Swier* to that text, so the conservative course is to pay final wages in full unless a signed authorization covers the deduction and to collect any remaining balance separately.

"No such agreement shall provide for a repayment schedule of more than 15% of an employee’s gross wages per pay period."[^de-1328-advance-repayment-cap]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a training or bonus repayment against the last paycheck without a signed deduction authorization risks the liquidated damages that section 1103 imposes for unpaid wages withheld without reasonable grounds for dispute under section 1104 or section 1107 [^de-1103-reasonable-grounds-current][^de-1107-withholding-limits].

## Are Delaware training or tuition repayments treated differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** For private employers, Delaware law does not treat training, tuition or relocation repayment differently from other repayment terms: no Delaware statute addresses them in private employment, and the same penalty rule and wage-deduction limits apply [^de-brazen-test-training][^de-1107-withholding-training]. The closest Delaware Supreme Court decision is *Swier*, which upheld a $25,000 early-departure payment as liquidated damages because the evidence tied it to the expenses the employer incurred in hiring the employee [^de-swier-forecast-training]. In our review we found no Delaware appellate decision on a training-repayment agreement as such, so the conservative course is to treat a training repayment as liquidated damages that must reflect a reasonable estimate of uncertain training costs.
Payroll collection of a training balance faces the same limits as any other debt. Federal law adds a separate floor: under the Fair Labor Standards Act regulations, the Act's wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back to the employer, directly or indirectly, the whole or part of the wage delivered, and the regulation's example treats the cost of employer-required tools as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the required minimum or overtime wages [^fed-531-35-kickback-rule-training][^fed-531-35-kickback-training].

## What happens to a Delaware employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee whose wages are withheld without authorization may sue to recover the unpaid wages and liquidated damages, and a judgment for the employee must include the costs of the action and reasonable attorney's fees [^de-1113-civil-action-enforcement]. Where the employer had no reasonable grounds for dispute under section 1104 or section 1107, the liquidated damages are the lower of ten percent of the unpaid wages for each day, excluding Sundays and legal holidays, that the failure continues after payment is due, or an amount equal to the unpaid wages [^de-1103-liquidated-enforcement].
The Department of Labor may also bring an action to collect unpaid wages, and an employer that violates the chapter or its regulations is subject to a civil penalty of $1,000 to $5,000 for each violation [^de-1113-civil-action-enforcement][^de-1112-civil-penalty-enforcement]. A claim for wages, or for damages or penalties from failing to pay them, must be brought within two years after it accrues [^de-8111-two-year-limit].

## What repayment structure is safest under Delaware law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** The safest Delaware repayment term sets an amount the employer can show was a reasonable forecast of uncertain costs, such as documented training or hiring expenses, because Delaware enforces an agreed amount where the damages are uncertain and the amount is reasonable [^de-brazen-test-structure][^de-swier-forecast-structure].
If the employer plans to collect through payroll, the deduction needs the employee's signed authorization for a lawful purpose accruing to the employee's benefit [^de-1107-authorization-structure], given voluntarily after written notice of its terms [^de-1328-authorization-structure]. If the repayment is structured as a cash advance, the regulation's advance rules also apply: both parties sign a written agreement stating the amount, the repayment schedule and the method [^de-1328-signed-agreement-structure], no scheduled deduction exceeds 15% of gross wages per pay period [^de-1328-cap-structure], and an advance balance above 15% can come out of final compensation only if the original agreement provided for it [^de-1328-final-balance-structure]. Each deduction should also leave pay at or above the $15.00 hourly minimum wage [^de-902-floor-structure]. Because final wages must be paid in compliance with section 1107, any balance a signed authorization does not cover is safer collected by a separate demand or action than by withholding the last paycheck [^de-1103-compliance-structure].

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in New Jersey[^about]

New Jersey has no statute in force that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract and a sum stipulated as damages for breaching a promise to stay must be a reasonable forecast of the employer's loss, while the Wage Payment Law allows a deduction from wages only for a listed purpose, such as an employer loan repaid on the schedule in the original loan agreement. A pending 2026 bill would make training repayment agreements void.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in New Jersey? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** New Jersey has no statute in force that limits stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment terms in private employment. Where a contract fixes a sum owed as damages for its breach, the New Jersey Supreme Court tests that stipulated sum for reasonableness [^nj-sop-wass-reason-enf]: the sum must be a reasonable forecast of the provable injury from the breach, or it is unenforceable as a penalty and the employer is limited to ordinary contract damages [^nj-sop-wass-forecast-enf].

"As the law has evolved, a stipulated damage clause ‘must constitute a reasonable forecast of the provable injury resulting from breach; otherwise, the clause will be unenforceable as a penalty and the non-breaching party will be limited to conventional damage measures."[^nj-sop-wass-forecast-enf]

*Wasserman's Inc. v. Township of Middletown* arose from a commercial lease of township property [^nj-sop-wass-lease-enf]. No New Jersey appellate decision located for this guide applies it to a private employer's training or bonus repayment term. The one appellate decision located that involves a training repayment agreement arose in public employment: in *New Jersey Transit Authority v. New Jersey Transit PBA*, the transit agency required new police applicants to agree to repay part of their academy training costs if they left within two years of completing the academy [^nj-sop-njt-terms-enf]. The Appellate Division held that the agreement was subject to collective negotiation [^nj-sop-njt-negotiate-enf], reasoning that a cost-shifting requirement affects employees' freedom to leave their jobs and their ability to keep the compensation they received [^nj-sop-njt-freedom-enf]. The court did not decide whether such an agreement is enforceable against a private-sector employee.

The New Jersey Supreme Court has distinguished a forfeiture from a penalty. In *Rosen v. Smith Barney, Inc.*, employees who had signed no agreement preventing them from leaving did not breach anything by leaving, so no liquidated damages clause could arise [^nj-sop-rosen-nobreach-enf], and the court held that a forfeiture clause unrelated to an underlying breach of contract is not a penalty [^nj-sop-rosen-penalty-enf]. Read together, these decisions suggest that a repayment obligation framed as a promise to stay, with a sum owed for breaking it, is exposed to the *Wasserman's* reasonableness test [^nj-sop-wass-reason-enf] in a way that a condition on keeping an unvested benefit may not be [^nj-sop-rosen-penalty-enf]; no New Jersey decision located for this guide draws that line for an employee repayment term. The conservative course is to set any fixed early-departure sum by reference to a documented estimate of the employer's loss, such as the cost of the training or the hiring incentive, and to frame a retention bonus as earned over the commitment period rather than as a promise to stay backed by a sum owed on departure.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment sum set to discourage departure, rather than as a reasonable forecast of the employer's loss from an early departure, risks being struck as a penalty [^nj-sop-wass-forecast-enf].

## Can a New Jersey employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No New Jersey appellate decision located for this guide decides whether an employer may recover a bonus or other payment that had already vested. In *Rosen v. Smith Barney, Inc.*, the incentive plan was entirely voluntary and its terms were disclosed to each participant [^nj-sop-rosen-voluntary-claw]. The New Jersey Supreme Court held that neither the vesting period attached to full ownership of the plan interest nor the plan's forfeiture provision violated the wage statute [^nj-sop-rosen-wpl-claw], and that a forfeiture clause unrelated to an underlying breach of contract is not a penalty [^nj-sop-rosen-penalty-claw]. The decision addressed forfeiture of an interest subject to a vesting period, not recovery of pay already made unconditional.

"That being the case, the operation of the forfeiture clause, because it is unrelated to an underlying breach of contract, cannot be understood to be such a penalty."[^nj-sop-rosen-penalty-claw]

Whether a bonus counts as wages matters for the Wage Payment Law. The Act defines wages as direct monetary compensation determined on a time, task, piece or commission basis, and excludes supplementary incentives and bonuses calculated independently of regular wages and paid in addition to them [^nj-sop-wpl-wages-claw]. A signing or retention bonus calculated apart from regular pay may therefore fall outside that definition, but the employee's regular wages do not, and the Act bars withholding any part of them except as law requires or empowers or for a listed purpose [^nj-sop-wpl-general-claw]. The conservative course is to state in the bonus agreement, before payment, that the bonus is earned only over the commitment period, rather than to try to recover pay the agreement had already made unconditional, and to recover any balance by demand rather than from wages.

## Can a New Jersey employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A New Jersey employer may withhold part of an employee's wages only when state or federal law requires or empowers the deduction or the deduction falls within a category the Wage Payment Law lists [^nj-sop-wpl-general-final]. The list includes payments for employer loans to employees made in accordance with a periodic payment schedule contained in the original loan agreement, and payments to correct payroll errors, if the employer approves the deduction [^nj-sop-wpl-loans-final]. Training costs, relocation costs and bonus repayments are not listed by name.

"(4) Payments for company products purchased in accordance with a periodic payment schedule contained in the original purchase agreement; payments for employer loans to employees, in accordance with a periodic payment schedule contained in the original loan agreement; payments for safety equipment; payments for the purchase of United States Government bonds; payments to correct payroll errors; and payments of costs and related fees for the replacement of employee identification, which is used to allow employees access to sterile or secured areas of airports, in accordance with a fee schedule described in any airline media plan approved by the federal Transportation Security Administration; provided all such deductions are approved by the employer."[^nj-sop-wpl-loans-final]

The loan category is tied to a schedule fixed when the loan is made. No New Jersey decision located for this guide decides whether a training, relocation or sign-on advance is an employer loan for this purpose, or whether a lump-sum deduction of an unpaid balance from the last paycheck fits a periodic payment schedule. An employer and employee also cannot agree around the Act: it is unlawful to make any agreement for paying wages other than as the Act provides, except to pay at shorter intervals or in advance [^nj-sop-wpl-void-final]. The conservative course is to pay final wages in full and pursue any repayment separately; where the advance was documented from the start as an employer loan, a deduction should be limited to the installment due under the original loan agreement's periodic schedule, with any remaining balance pursued separately.

Timing runs while any dispute is open. When employment ends for any reason, the employer must ordinarily pay all wages due no later than the regular payday for the pay period in which employment ended; incentive compensation may be paid as a reasonable approximation until the exact amount can be computed, and an employer gets an additional 10 days when a labor dispute involving payroll employees causes a suspension [^nj-sop-wpl-final-final]. Where federal law applies, wages kicked back to the employer, directly or indirectly, do not satisfy the minimum wage and overtime requirements [^nj-sop-cfr-kickback-final], although the Department of Labor allows recovery of the principal of a genuine loan or wage advance even when the deduction cuts into those amounts [^nj-sop-dol-loan-final].

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-paycheck deduction for a training or bonus repayment that does not fit a listed category leaves the withheld amount owed as unpaid wages, with liquidated damages of up to 200 percent of the wages due available to the employee [^nj-sop-wpl-general-final][^nj-sop-wpl-remedy-final].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in New Jersey? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No New Jersey statute in force gives training, tuition or relocation repayment terms a rule separate from bonus repayment terms. A sum stipulated as damages for leaving early is tested as a stipulated damage clause [^nj-sop-wass-forecast-train], and recovery from wages is limited to the deductions the Wage Payment Law permits [^nj-sop-wpl-general-train]. A pending bill would change that: S. 2105 would bar an employer from requiring a training repayment agreement as a condition of employment and would make such an agreement void [^nj-sop-s2105-void-train].

"No employer shall require, as a condition of employment, any employee or prospective employee to enter into a training repayment agreement. Any agreement which is a training repayment agreement is void and no employee shall be obligated to make payments under that agreement."[^nj-sop-s2105-void-train]

The bill's definition is broad. It would reach any agreement requiring an employee to pay the employer, a training provider or another third party a sum if the employee leaves, including reimbursement for training already provided, but would exclude a cash advance, a payment for equipment sold or leased to the employee, an educational sabbatical leave contract and an agreement made as part of a collective bargaining agreement [^nj-sop-s2105-def-train]. On October 1, 2026, the Senate Labor Committee reported the bill favorably [^nj-sop-s2105-reported-train]. No New Jersey statute in force enacts the bill's training-repayment rule. The version that may pass could differ from the introduced text.

For unionized public employees, a training repayment requirement is a subject of collective negotiation. In *New Jersey Transit Authority v. New Jersey Transit PBA*, the Public Employment Relations Commission ruled that a requirement to repay training costs on leaving is a mandatorily negotiable term and condition of employment [^nj-sop-njt-perc-train], and the Appellate Division affirmed that the repayment agreement was subject to negotiation [^nj-sop-njt-negotiate-train]. Under federal wage law, the Department of Labor has stated that an employer may recoup tuition advanced on condition of continued employment if the employee clearly understood the policy before accepting the payment [^nj-sop-dol-tuition-train].

## How are New Jersey repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A New Jersey employee whose wages were unlawfully withheld for a repayment can sue under the Wage Payment Law for the full amount of wages due, plus liquidated damages of not more than 200 percent of the wages due, with costs and reasonable attorney's fees [^nj-sop-wpl-remedy-enforce]. The employee may instead file a wage claim with the Department of Labor and Workforce Development, which can reach wages owed for work performed up to six years before the claim is filed [^nj-sop-wage-claim-enforce]. Where the employer's claim rests on a clause stipulating damages for breach of a promise to remain employed, rather than on repayment of an amount actually advanced, the stipulated sum is unenforceable as a penalty unless it is a reasonable forecast of the provable injury [^nj-sop-wass-forecast-enforce].

"c. If any employer fails to pay the full amount of wages to an employee agreed to or required by, or in the manner required by, the provisions of article 1 of chapter 11 of Title 34 of the Revised Statutes and all acts supplementing that article (R.S.34:11-2 et al.), the employee may recover in a civil action the full amount of any wages due, or any wages lost because of any retaliatory action taken in violation of subsection of this section, plus an amount of liquidated damages equal to not more than 200 percent of the wages lost or of the wages due, together with costs and reasonable attorney's fees as are allowed by the court, except that if there is an agreement of the employee to accept payment of the unpaid wages supervised by the commissioner pursuant to section 9 of P.L.1965, c.173 (C.34:11-4.9) or R.S.34:11-58, the liquidated damages shall be equal to not more than 200 percent of wages that were due prior to the supervised payment."[^nj-sop-wpl-remedy-enforce]

The statute excuses liquidated damages for a first violation only in narrow circumstances, including that the error was inadvertent and made in good faith and that the employer pays the amount owed within 30 days of notice [^nj-sop-wpl-goodfaith-enforce]. An agreement with the employee does not defeat these remedies: an agreement to pay wages other than as the Act provides is unlawful [^nj-sop-wpl-void-enforce]. If S. 2105 is enacted as introduced, a training repayment agreement would be void and no employee would owe payments under it [^nj-sop-s2105-void-enforce].

## What repayment structure is safest under New Jersey law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A New Jersey clause stipulating damages for breach of a promise to remain employed must set a sum that reasonably forecasts the provable injury from the breach [^nj-sop-wass-forecast-safe]. Tying the sum to the documented cost of the training or the amount of the bonus, and reducing it as the employee serves the commitment, is a drafting choice aimed at that test; no New Jersey decision located for this guide approves a particular schedule. For tuition advances, the Department of Labor's opinion conditions recoupment under the FLSA on the employee clearly understanding the tuition-payment policy before accepting payment [^nj-sop-dol-tuition-safe]. Final wages should be paid in full by the regular payday for the pay period in which employment ended, subject to the statute's incentive-compensation and labor-dispute exceptions [^nj-sop-wpl-final-safe], and the balance collected by demand or suit; for a genuine employer loan, the deduction the statute permits is the installment due under the periodic payment schedule in the original loan agreement [^nj-sop-wpl-loans-safe], with any remaining balance pursued separately.

A bonus that is earned only over the commitment period, so that leaving forfeits an unvested amount rather than breaching a promise to stay, draws on the reasoning in *Rosen v. Smith Barney, Inc.* that a forfeiture unrelated to an underlying breach is not a penalty [^nj-sop-rosen-penalty-safe]. Because S. 2105 would make training repayment agreements void if enacted [^nj-sop-s2105-void-safe], an employer relying on one should check the bill's status before each new hire.

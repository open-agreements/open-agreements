# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Alaska[^about]

Alaska has no statute aimed at stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while a Department of Labor regulation separately limits collecting the debt from wages.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Alaska? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Alaska decides case by case whether an agreed damages amount is a valid liquidated damages clause or an unenforceable penalty [^ak-carr-gottstein-case-by-case]. Alaska has no statute that bans or limits stay-or-pay, training-repayment or sign-on bonus repayment terms in private employment, so the conservative inference is that a court would test a fixed repayment amount under that penalty rule [^ak-carr-gottstein-case-by-case].
The Alaska Supreme Court has held that liquidated damages clauses are proper where it would be difficult to ascertain actual damages and where the agreed amount is a reasonable forecast of the damages likely to occur in the event of breach [^ak-zerbetz-two-part-test]. In *Carr-Gottstein Properties v. Benedict*, the court explained that contractual penalties serve no positive purpose, which is why courts do not enforce them [^ak-carr-gottstein-no-penalties]. In *Kalenka v. Taylor*, the court held that stipulated damages are illegitimate where there is no attempt to calculate the actual damages a breach might cause, and that the same amount applying to a total or partial breach, or to minor or major breaches, indicates that lack of calculation [^ak-kalenka-no-calculation].
*Dominic Wenzell, DMD PC v. Ingrim* involved a liquidated damages provision attached to a covenant restricting competition and solicitation [^ak-wenzell-clause]. The court noted that the buyer had chosen the $250,000 figure because it was half the purchase price, essentially conceding that it was not a forecast of actual damages [^ak-wenzell-no-forecast]. For an overbroad covenant not to compete, Alaska requires the court to alter it reasonably to make it enforceable where possible, unless the covenant was not drafted in good faith, and the employer bears the burden of proving good faith [^ak-data-management-reasonable-alteration].

"Contractual penalties serve no positive purpose, which is why courts do not enforce them."[^ak-carr-gottstein-no-penalties]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A flat repayment amount that stays the same however much of the commitment the employee served, and that the employer picked without trying to calculate the loss an early departure causes, risks being refused as a penalty [^ak-kalenka-caution][^ak-wenzell-caution].

## Can an Alaska employer claw back pay already received? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** An Alaska employer may not require or induce an employee, by threat of dismissal or by any other manner, to return or give up any part of the compensation to which the employee is entitled [^ak-8aac-15-160-no-return-clawback]. A written agreement for deductions payable to the employer is not valid if it would reduce the employee's wage rate below the statutory minimum wage or overtime rates [^ak-8aac-15-160-floor-clawback].
A bonus that has not yet been earned is a different matter. In *Klondike Industries Corp. v. Gibson*, the Alaska Supreme Court held that a hotel manager whose contract paid a bonus only if he was still employed when the hotel sold, and who left before the sale, was entitled to no part of the bonus, and it observed that bonus agreements conditioned on continued employment have been widely upheld [^ak-klondike-partial-performance][^ak-klondike-widely-upheld].
In our review we found no Alaska appellate decision on recovering a cash sign-on bonus, retention bonus or other conditional advance the employee has not become entitled to keep. Our inference is that a court would test the repayment amount under the penalty rule, which allows an agreed amount only where actual damages would be difficult to ascertain and the amount is a reasonable forecast of the likely damages [^ak-zerbetz-test-clawback], so the conservative course is to collect such a repayment separately rather than by withholding wages. Separate collection does not make it lawful to recover compensation the employee has already earned, because the regulation prohibits inducing an employee to give up compensation to which the employee is entitled by any manner [^ak-8aac-15-160-no-return-clawback].

"As we noted earlier, bonus agreements conditioned on continued employment have been widely upheld."[^ak-klondike-widely-upheld]

## Can repayment come out of an Alaska final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Alaska's deduction regulation recognizes written agreements between employer and employee for deductions of the employee's monetary obligations, but a written deduction agreement payable to the employer is invalid if it would reduce the employee's wage rate below the statutory minimum wage or overtime rates [^ak-8aac-15-160-written-deductions][^ak-8aac-15-160-floor-final-pay]. The Department of Labor and Workforce Development's regulation provides that the final-pay statute does not limit the right of an employer and employee to enter into a written agreement for deductions of the employee's monetary obligations [^ak-8aac-15-160-written-deductions].
When employment ends, all wages become due immediately. If the employer ends the employment, for any cause, payment is due within three working days; if the employee quits, payment is due at the next regular payday that is at least three days after the employer received notice [^ak-23-05-140-final-pay-timing][^ak-23-05-140-final-pay-deadlines]. If the amount of wages is in dispute, the employer must give the employee written notice of the wages it concedes are due and pay that amount without condition within the statutory time, and a release required as a condition of that payment is void [^ak-23-05-180-disputed-wages].
The minimum wage under the Wage and Hour Act is currently $14.00 an hour, effective July 1, 2026, and is scheduled to rise to $15.00 an hour on July 1, 2027, with annual inflation adjustments after that [^ak-23-10-065-rates-final-pay]. The regulation also lists debts a written agreement may not make the employee repay, including customer checks returned for any reason, theft or credit default by customers, and damage or breakage unless clearly due to the employee's willful conduct and acknowledged in writing [^ak-8aac-15-160-floor-final-pay]. In our review we found no Alaska appellate decision on deducting a training, tuition or bonus repayment from final wages, so the conservative course is to pay final wages in full and collect any balance separately.

"The provisions of AS 23.05.140 and AS 23.10.085(c) do not limit the right of an employer and employee to enter into a written agreement to provide for deductions of monetary obligations of an employee."[^ak-8aac-15-160-written-deductions]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a repayment against the last paycheck in an amount that pushes pay below the minimum wage risks a penalty of the employee's regular pay from the employee's demand until payment, for up to 90 working days [^ak-8aac-15-160-floor-caution][^ak-23-05-140-penalty-caution].

## Are Alaska training or tuition repayments treated differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Alaska statute specifically regulates training, tuition or relocation repayment agreements in private employment. One deduction rule touches relocation: an employer may deduct from earnings, under a written agreement signed by the employee, the cost of transportation from the place of hire to the place of employment, if the deduction does not reduce the employee's wages below the statutory minimum or overtime pay below one and one-half times the contractual rate [^ak-8aac-15-160-transport-training]. That rule limits how the cost may be collected from pay; it does not decide whether the repayment promise itself is enforceable. A separate statute runs the other way: an employer that furnishes or finances a person's transportation from the place of hire to employ the person must provide return transportation to the place of hire, on request made within 45 days after the employment ends, when the employment ends for a cause the department considers good and sufficient and beyond the person's control, or when the employment contract or a renewal ends [^ak-23-10-380-return-transport-training].
In our review we found no Alaska appellate decision on a private employer's training-repayment or tuition-repayment agreement, so the conservative course is to treat a training repayment as an agreed amount that must meet the penalty test: actual damages must be difficult to ascertain, and the amount must be a reasonable forecast of the damages likely to follow the employee's early departure [^ak-zerbetz-test-training]. Federal law adds a separate floor: under the Fair Labor Standards Act regulations, wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back the whole or part of the wage to the employer, and the regulation's example treats the cost of employer-required tools as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the required minimum or overtime wages [^fed-531-35-kickback-alaska-training][^fed-531-35-tools-alaska-training].

## What happens to an Alaska employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An Alaska employer that pays final wages late may be required to pay the employee a penalty equal to the employee's regular pay from the time of demand to the time of payment, or for 90 working days, whichever is less [^ak-23-05-140-penalty-enforcement]. In an action the Department of Labor and Workforce Development brings, an employer found liable for late final pay must pay that penalty, calculated on the employee's straight-time rate for an eight-hour day [^ak-23-05-140-department-enforcement].
An employer that violates the minimum wage or overtime requirements is liable to the affected employee for the unpaid minimum wages or overtime and, except as the statute provides, an additional equal amount as liquidated damages [^ak-23-10-110-liquidated-enforcement]. The court must also award costs and, with listed exceptions, reasonable attorney fees against the employer [^ak-23-10-110-fees-enforcement]. An action for unpaid minimum wages, overtime or liquidated damages under the Wage and Hour Act is barred unless started within two years after the cause of action accrues [^ak-23-10-130-two-years-enforcement].
An employer suing to collect a repayment promise, rather than deducting it, generally has three years to bring an action on a contract [^ak-09-10-053-contract-enforcement].

## What repayment structure is safest under Alaska law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** An Alaska liquidated damages provision is enforceable only where actual damages would be difficult to ascertain and the agreed amount reasonably forecasts the damages likely to follow a breach [^ak-zerbetz-test-structure]. As a drafting inference, the safest repayment term ties its amount to an estimate, made when the agreement is signed, of the loss the employer expects an early departure to cause, such as unrecovered training or recruitment costs. The opposite approach carries risk: *Kalenka* treats an amount that stays the same for a total or partial breach as a sign that no one tried to calculate actual damages [^ak-kalenka-structure], and in *Wenzell* the court noted that a figure chosen as half the purchase price was essentially conceded not to be a forecast of actual damages [^ak-wenzell-structure]. As a drafting matter, a schedule that reduces the balance as the employee serves the commitment keeps the amount closer to the employer's unrecovered cost.
Collecting the balance by separate demand or action is safer than withholding pay, because the regulation addresses such deductions through a written agreement [^ak-8aac-15-160-written-structure], and a written deduction agreement payable to the employer is invalid if it would reduce the employee's wage rate below the statutory minimum wage or overtime rates [^ak-8aac-15-160-structure], and final wages are due within three working days after the employer ends the employment, or at the next regular payday at least three days after notice when the employee quits [^ak-23-05-140-structure].

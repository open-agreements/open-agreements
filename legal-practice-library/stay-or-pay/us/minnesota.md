# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Minnesota[^about]

Minnesota has no general stay-or-pay statute, but Minn. Stat. § 181.645 bars requiring an employee to pay for training that law or the employer requires for the current job unless the training is required to obtain or maintain a license, registration or certification, and the Attorney General has applied it to training repayment agreements; other repayment terms are enforced as contracts subject to the penalty rule, while Minn. Stat. § 181.79 bars deducting a claimed debt from wages unless the employee authorizes it in writing after the debt arises or, for a loan, before the loan is made. A 2026 Senate bill that would ban stay-or-pay provisions has not been enacted.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Minnesota? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** A Minnesota employer may not require an employee to pay for training that law, or the employer for the current position, requires, unless it is required for a license, registration or certification [^mn-sop-training-cost-enf]. No statute in force bans other stay-or-pay or bonus-repayment terms. Where a contract fixes a sum owed for its breach, the Minnesota Supreme Court asks whether the sum is a penalty: when the damages from the breach can be definitely measured, an agreed amount greatly disproportionate to them is a penalty [^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-enf].

The Minnesota Attorney General reads that statute to allow charging employees only for training required for a license, registration or certification [^mn-sop-ag-reading-enf]. No Minnesota appellate decision located for this guide construes § 181.645 or decides whether a repayment obligation triggered by leaving is a requirement to pay for training; the conservative course is to treat it as one and not attach repayment to training the statute covers.

"On the other hand, when the measure of damages resulting from a breach of contract is susceptible of definite measurement, we have uniformly held an amount greatly disproportionate to be a penalty."[^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-enf]

*Gorco Construction Co. v. Stein* arose from a construction contract, not employment. When damages resulting from breach can be definitely measured, *Gorco* treats an agreed amount greatly disproportionate to those damages as a penalty [^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-enf]; no Minnesota appellate decision located for this guide applies *Gorco* to an employee training or bonus repayment term, and setting the amount by reference to the employer's documented cost is a drafting choice rather than a judicially established damages measure.

Minn. Stat. § 181.988 defines a covenant not to compete as an agreement that restricts the employee, after employment ends, from performing work for another employer for a period, in an area or in a similar capacity [^mn-sop-noncompete-def-enf]. No Minnesota decision located for this guide treats a repayment term as a covenant not to compete. A forfeiture of pay already earned is treated differently from a stipulated sum: in *Capistrant v. Lifetouch National School Studios, Inc.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that the penalty analysis did not apply to a clause cutting off post-employment commissions [^mn-sop-cap-notld-enf], but drew on its precedent that disfavors forfeitures [^mn-sop-cap-disfavor-enf].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment sum set to discourage departure, rather than to recover a documented cost, risks being struck as a penalty when it is greatly disproportionate to the employer's measurable loss [^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-enf].

## Can a Minnesota employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No published Minnesota appellate decision located for this guide decides whether an employer may recover a signing, retention or relocation bonus already paid when the employee leaves early. In *Capistrant v. Lifetouch National School Studios, Inc.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court rejected the employee's argument that the employer's contractual right to stop paying a residual commission was an illegal penalty [^mn-sop-cap-reject-claw], but held that a court may weigh whether the condition was a material part of the agreed exchange and whether the forfeiture is proportionate to the risk protected [^mn-sop-cap-prongs-claw].

"Section 229 consists of two prongs: (1) whether the occurrence of the condition was a material part of the agreed exchange and (2) a proportionality analysis that balances the risk to be protected with the amount to be forfeited."[^mn-sop-cap-prongs-claw]

The court drew on its precedent that disfavors forfeitures [^mn-sop-cap-disfavor-claw]. The case concerned the employer's contractual right to terminate payment of a residual commission, not recovery of a bonus already paid [^mn-sop-cap-reject-claw]. Recovering a paid bonus from later wages is a separate question: as a general rule, Minnesota bars deducting any claimed debt from wages without a written authorization the employee gives after the debt arises, unless a court holds the employee liable [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-claw], subject to statutory exceptions that include a collective bargaining agreement with a contrary provision and a written authorization given before a purchase or loan from the employer [^mn-sop-deduct-loan-claw]. The conservative course is to state in the bonus agreement, before payment, the amount repayable and how it declines over the commitment period, and to recover any balance by demand rather than from wages.

## Can a Minnesota employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Minnesota employer generally needs the employee's voluntary written authorization, given after a repayment debt such as an unpaid training cost or bonus repayment arises, or a court determination of liability, to deduct that debt from wages [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-final]. The rule does not apply where the employee, before making a purchase or taking a loan from the employer, voluntarily authorized in writing that its cost be deducted from wages at regular intervals or on termination of employment [^mn-sop-deduct-loan-final].

"Any agreement entered into between an employer and an employee contrary to this section shall be void. This section shall not apply to the following: (1) in cases where a contrary provision in a collective bargaining agreement exists; (2) any rules established by an employer for employees who are commissioned salespeople, where the rules are used for purposes of discipline, by fine or otherwise, in cases where errors or omissions in performing their duties exist; or (3) in cases where an employee, prior to making a purchase or loan from the employer, voluntarily authorizes in writing that the cost of the purchase or loan shall be deducted from the employee's wages, at regular intervals or upon termination of employment."[^mn-sop-deduct-loan-final]

The timing of the authorization decides which rule applies. A repayment authorization signed before the claimed debt arises does not satisfy the general rule's after-the-fact authorization requirement [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-final], and an agreement contrary to the section is void [^mn-sop-deduct-loan-final]. Under the loan exception, it can support a deduction if the advance is a loan and the authorization was signed before the loan was made [^mn-sop-deduct-loan-final]; no Minnesota decision located for this guide decides whether a training, relocation or sign-on advance is a loan for this purpose. Under the general rule, an authorization given after the debt arises must state the amount to be deducted each pay period [^mn-sop-deduct-period-final], and a deduction may not exceed the amount subject to garnishment [^mn-sop-deduct-garnish-final].

Timing runs while any dispute is open. A discharged employee's earned wages are due on demand [^mn-sop-discharge-demand-final], and the employer is in default if they are not paid within 24 hours after the demand [^mn-sop-discharge-default-final]. An employee who quits must be paid in full no later than the first regularly scheduled payday after the final day of work, unless a collective bargaining agreement provides otherwise [^mn-sop-quit-payday-final]; if that payday is less than five calendar days after the final day, payment may wait until the second payday, but no more than 20 calendar days after the final day [^mn-sop-quit-twenty-final]. Where federal law applies, wages kicked back to the employer do not satisfy the minimum wage and overtime requirements [^mn-sop-cfr-kickback-final], although the Department of Labor allows recovery of the principal of a genuine loan or wage advance even when the deduction cuts into those amounts [^mn-sop-dol-loan-final].

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-paycheck deduction for a training or bonus repayment made without a qualifying written authorization, a court judgment or another statutory exception exposes the employer to a civil action for twice the amount deducted [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-final][^mn-sop-deduct-double-final].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Minnesota? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** A Minnesota employer may not require an employee to pay for training that law, or the employer for the current position, requires, unless it is required for a license, registration or certification [^mn-sop-training-cost-train]. No statute gives relocation or tuition repayment terms outside that rule a separate standard. Where a repayment provision fixes damages for breach, an amount greatly disproportionate to definitely measurable damages is a penalty [^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-train], and recovery from wages is limited by the written-authorization rule for wage deductions [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-train]. A pending bill would change that: S.F. 2533, as amended in the Senate, would declare a stay-or-pay provision unconscionable, against public policy and prohibited [^mn-sop-sf2533-ban-train].

In September 2025 the Attorney General announced settlements with two salon employers whose training repayment agreements covered on-the-job training specific to the employer's products and services [^mn-sop-ag-facts-train], applying the reading that § 181.645 allows charging employees only for training required for a license, registration or certification [^mn-sop-ag-reading-train]. The employers agreed to release employees from those agreements and are barred from collecting under them [^mn-sop-ag-release-train]. A settlement announcement is not a court ruling, but it shows how the state enforcer reads the statute.

"(g) ‘Stay-or-pay provision’ means a written agreement or contract provision that requires an employee to pay the employer or the employer's agent or assignee a sum of money if the employee leaves the employment before passage of a stated period of time, including but not limited to a written agreement or contract provision stating payment of money constitutes reimbursement for training provided to the employee by the employer or by a third party, recruitment and training costs for replacement employees, lost revenue, or liquidated damages."[^mn-sop-sf2533-def-train]

The bill's definition reaches written terms requiring an employee to pay a sum for leaving before a stated period, including reimbursement for training, but excludes an employee's agreement to repay training received from a qualifying apprenticeship program to that program [^mn-sop-sf2533-def-train]. The House companion, H.F. 2567, as amended in March 2026, would likewise prohibit such terms with certain limited exceptions [^mn-sop-hf2567-summary-train]. No Minnesota statute in force enacts the bill, and a version that passes could differ from this text.

Under federal wage law, the Department of Labor has stated, in the circumstances of the request before it, that an employer may recoup tuition money advanced to an employee if the employee clearly understood the tuition payment policy before accepting the payment [^mn-sop-dol-tuition-train]. That opinion addresses the federal minimum wage, not Minnesota's deduction statute, which still bars taking the amount from wages unless the employee authorizes the deduction in writing after the debt arises or a court holds the employee liable [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-train], subject to the statute's exceptions, including a written authorization given before a loan from the employer is made [^mn-sop-deduct-loan-train].

## How are Minnesota repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A Minnesota employee whose wages were reduced by a deduction that § 181.79 does not allow can sue the employer for twice the amount of the deduction [^mn-sop-deduct-double-enforce]. If the deduction left final wages unpaid, a discharged employee may make a demand, which must be in writing [^mn-sop-discharge-writing-enforce]; the employer is in default if the wages are not paid within 24 hours after the demand [^mn-sop-discharge-default-enforce], and the employee may collect a penalty of average daily earnings for each day of default, up to 15 days [^mn-sop-discharge-penalty-enforce]; for an employee who quit, wages not paid by the statutory deadline become payable on demand [^mn-sop-quit-demand-enforce], which must be in writing [^mn-sop-quit-writing-enforce], with the same penalty if they remain unpaid 24 hours after the demand [^mn-sop-quit-penalty-enforce], and an employee may bring a civil action for violations of the final-pay sections directly in district court [^mn-sop-civil-action-enforce].

"An employer who violates the provisions of this section shall be liable in a civil action brought by the employee for twice the amount of the deduction or credit taken."[^mn-sop-deduct-double-enforce]

For training repayment agreements, the Attorney General has obtained settlements in which employers released employees from the agreements and were barred from collecting under them [^mn-sop-ag-release-enforce]. Where the employer's claim rests on a sum stipulated for breaking a promise to stay, rather than on repayment of an amount actually advanced, a sum greatly disproportionate to measurable damages is a penalty [^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-enforce]. If S.F. 2533 were enacted in the form of its first engrossment, the Department of Labor and Industry could fine an employer between $1,000 and $5,000 for each violation, payable to the employee [^mn-sop-sf2533-fine-enforce].

## What repayment structure is safest under Minnesota law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A Minnesota employer should not attach a repayment obligation to training that law or the employer requires for the employee's current position unless the training is required to obtain or maintain a license, registration or certification [^mn-sop-training-cost-safe]. For other repayment terms, the agreement should set an amount that is not greatly disproportionate to the employer's measurable loss [^mn-sop-gorco-penalty-safe]. Tying the amount to the documented cost of the training or the amount of the bonus, and reducing it as the employee serves the commitment, is a drafting choice aimed at that test; no Minnesota decision located for this guide approves a particular schedule. Final wages should be paid in full on time: for an employee who quits, no later than the first regularly scheduled payday after the final day of work [^mn-sop-quit-payday-safe], or the second if the first falls less than five calendar days after the final day, within 20 calendar days [^mn-sop-quit-twenty-safe], and for a discharged employee, within 24 hours after demand [^mn-sop-discharge-default-safe]. Any balance should be collected by demand or suit. As a general rule, a deduction requires a written authorization the employee gives after the debt arises or a court judgment holding the employee liable [^mn-sop-deduct-auth-safe]; among the statute's exceptions, an advance documented from the start as a loan may be deducted under an authorization signed before the loan was made [^mn-sop-deduct-loan-safe].

Because S.F. 2533 would make stay-or-pay provisions prohibited if enacted [^mn-sop-sf2533-ban-safe], an employer relying on one should note that, as of this guide's review on November 24, 2026, no Minnesota statute in force enacts it.

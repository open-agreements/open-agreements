# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Indiana[^about]

Indiana has no general stay-or-pay statute, so a repayment term turns on contract law, and the Court of Appeals has treated a fixed training-cost sum far above the employer's measurable costs as an unenforceable penalty. Ind. Code § 22-2-6-2 allows deductions from wages to repay education or skills training only under a written, personally signed and revocable wage assignment. Since July 1, 2025, a hospital or hospital system may not require a physician employed for at least three years to repay bonuses or training costs for leaving to practice with a new employer.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Indiana? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** No Indiana statute generally governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment agreements in private employment. Where a repayment term fixes an amount as damages for leaving early, the Indiana Court of Appeals treats a sum that is grossly disproportionate to the loss that may result from the breach as an unenforceable penalty rather than liquidated damages [^in-sop-press-a-dent-penalty]. In *Press-A-Dent, Inc. v. Weigel* the court applied that rule to the liquidated sum in a worker's agreement, which the trial court had treated as a claim for training costs, and held it a penalty, because the actual costs of the training could have been calculated with reasonable certainty [^in-sop-press-a-dent-training-held-penalty]. One statute limits repayment directly: since July 1, 2025, a physician and a hospital or hospital system may not enter into an agreement requiring a physician employed for at least three years to repay bonuses or training expenses because the physician goes to practice with a new employer [^in-sop-physician-repayment-noncompete] [^in-sop-physician-ban].

"Simply put, the trial court could have considered these amounts as a component of Press-A-Dent’s claim for damages, had they been offered at trial, and damages could have been calculated with reasonable certainty. Hence, we can only conclude that the liquidated damages provided for in the Weigel Agreement amounted to nothing more than a penalty."[^in-sop-press-a-dent-training-held-penalty]

The trial court in *Press-A-Dent*, affirmed on appeal, had found that liquidated damages were not a suitable remedy for reimbursing training expenses because the amount spent on training was easily ascertainable [^in-sop-press-a-dent-trial-findings], and that $50,000 bore no reasonable relation to the damage the employer was likely to suffer [^in-sop-press-a-dent-no-reasonable-relation]. Whether a stipulated sum is liquidated damages or a penalty turns on the facts, the parties' intention and the reasonableness of the stipulation in the circumstances [^in-sop-press-a-dent-factors]. No other Indiana appellate decision located for this guide applies the rule to a stay-or-pay term, or decides whether a promise to return an advance or reimburse an actual expenditure is liquidated damages at all rather than a debt. The conservative course is to tie the repayment amount to the employer's documented cost and reduce it as the employee serves the commitment.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A fixed repayment sum far above the employer's documented training cost risks being held an unenforceable penalty [^in-sop-press-a-dent-training-held-penalty][^in-sop-press-a-dent-trial-findings].

## Can an Indiana employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Indiana appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a signing, retention or relocation bonus already paid can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. Indiana's wage claims statute defines wages as all amounts at which labor or service is paid, however the amount is calculated [^in-sop-wages-definition], and an employee's direction to deduct from wages to be earned is an assignment of wages that is valid only if it meets the conditions of Ind. Code § 22-2-6-2 [^in-sop-direction-is-assignment] [^in-sop-assignment-conditions-clawback]. The conservative course is to pay every earned amount in full and pursue any repayment separately rather than by keeping back wages.

An employer may not assess a fine against an employee on any pretext and keep it from the employee's wages, and doing so is a Class C infraction [^in-sop-fines-prohibited]. No Indiana decision located for this guide decides whether withholding a fixed sum for leaving early is a fine under that section. The conservative course is to state in the agreement the service condition on which a bonus is earned, and never to cancel or withhold a bonus after it has become payable.

## Can an Indiana employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Apart from recovering an overpayment of wages, which a separate section allows after two weeks' notice and not for an amount in dispute [^in-sop-overpayment-exception], an Indiana employer may deduct a stay-or-pay repayment from wages, including final wages, only under a valid wage assignment. The assignment must be in writing, signed by the employee personally, revocable at any time by the employee on written notice, and agreed to in writing by the employer; an executed copy must reach the employer within ten days; and it must serve a purpose the statute lists [^in-sop-assignment-conditions]. The listed purposes include reimbursement for education or employee skills training and the amount of a loan made by the employer and evidenced by a written instrument the employee signed [^in-sop-listed-purposes-loan] [^in-sop-listed-purposes-training].

"Any assignment of the wages of an employee is valid only if all of the following conditions are satisfied: (1) The assignment is: (A) in writing; (B) signed by the employee personally; (C) by its terms revocable at any time by the employee upon written notice to the employer; and (D) agreed to in writing by the employer. (2) An executed copy of the assignment is delivered to the employer within ten (10) days after its execution. (3) The assignment is made for a purpose described in subsection (b)."[^in-sop-assignment-conditions]

The signature requirement is applied strictly. In *Cox v. Town of Rome City* the Court of Appeals held that an employer could not deduct an insurance premium from a departing employee's last paycheck without a written wage assignment personally signed by the employee, even though the employer acted in good faith [^in-sop-cox-signed-assignment]. Because the assignment must be revocable at any time on written notice [^in-sop-assignment-conditions], an employee who revokes before separation leaves the employer with no authority to deduct the remaining balance from final pay.

No listed purpose names signing, retention or relocation bonuses [^in-sop-listed-purposes-loan]. No Indiana decision located for this guide decides whether a bonus that must be repaid on early departure is a loan evidenced by a written instrument for this purpose. The conservative course for a bonus is either to document it from the start as a loan in a written instrument the employee signs, with any interest no higher than the bank prime loan rate plus 4 percent [^in-sop-loan-interest-cap], or to collect any balance by demand or suit rather than by deduction. Because the loan purpose is subject to the amount limits in section 4(c) of the chapter [^in-sop-listed-purposes-loan], a weekly deduction toward a loan may not exceed the lesser of 25 percent of the employee's disposable earnings for the week or the amount by which those earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum hourly wage [^in-sop-loan-deduction-weekly-cap].

An employee who quits must be paid by the next usual and regular payday the employer has set [^in-sop-voluntary-quit-payday], and when the employer separates the employee, unpaid wages are due at the regular payday for the pay period in which the separation occurred [^in-sop-separation-payday]. In a dispute over wages, the employer must give notice of and pay, without condition, the amount it concedes to be due [^in-sop-disputed-wages].

A deduction is also measured against the minimum wage. Indiana's Minimum Wage Law requires employers it covers to pay at least the federal minimum wage [^in-sop-state-minimum-wage]. Under the federal rule, wages count as paid only when paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^in-sop-flsa-free-and-clear], and the wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^in-sop-flsa-kickback]. The federal rule illustrates the point with employer-required tools: there is a violation in any workweek in which their cost cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^in-sop-flsa-tools-workweek]. The conservative course is to keep any deduction from reducing a workweek's pay below the minimum wage or cutting into overtime pay.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment clause that lets the employer deduct the balance from final wages, without a written, personally signed and unrevoked wage assignment for a listed purpose, exposes the employer to the unpaid wages, a reasonable attorney fee and court costs, and liquidated damages of two times the wages due if the employer was not acting in good faith [^in-sop-assignment-conditions][^in-sop-wpa-fees][^in-sop-wpa-double-damages].

## Are training or relocation repayments treated differently in Indiana? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Training is treated differently in one respect: Ind. Code § 22-2-6-2 expressly lists reimbursement for education or employee skills training as a purpose for which an employee may assign wages, so a valid written, signed and revocable assignment can authorize deductions to repay training costs [^in-sop-assignment-conditions-tr] [^in-sop-listed-purposes-training-tr]. The statute bars a training-repayment assignment where the education or training was provided, in whole or in part, through a federal, state or local economic development incentive [^in-sop-listed-purposes-training-tr]. Relocation costs and signing bonuses are not a listed purpose; the closest listed purpose is a loan made by the employer and evidenced by a written instrument the employee signed [^in-sop-listed-purposes-loan-tr]. For physicians, a hospital or hospital system may not, under agreements originally entered into on or after July 1, 2025, require a physician employed for at least three years to repay bonuses or training expenses because the physician goes to practice with a new employer [^in-sop-physician-repayment-noncompete-tr] [^in-sop-physician-ban-tr].

The listed training purpose governs only how a repayment may be collected from wages. It does not make a repayment amount enforceable, and a liquidated sum sought for training costs has been held a penalty because those costs could be calculated with reasonable certainty [^in-sop-press-a-dent-training-held-penalty-tr]. The statute does not define an economic development incentive, and no Indiana decision located for this guide does so, so an employer whose training was supported by a public grant or tax incentive should confirm the funding source before taking any assignment for its repayment.

The physician provision reaches only agreements between a physician and a hospital, a parent company or affiliated manager of a hospital, or a hospital system, and it does not apply to an agreement originally entered into before July 1, 2025 [^in-sop-physician-ban-tr]. Section 2.3 bars those parties from entering into a noncompete agreement on or after that date and makes an agreement in violation of the section void and unenforceable [^in-sop-physician-ban-tr]. A repayment obligation tied to the length of service rather than to where the physician practices next is outside the definition's second category, which reaches only obligations based solely or primarily on the physician's decision to practice with a new employer [^in-sop-physician-repayment-noncompete-tr].

## How are Indiana repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A stipulated repayment sum that is grossly disproportionate to the loss that may result from the breach is treated as an unenforceable penalty [^in-sop-press-a-dent-penalty-enforcement]. An employee whose wages were not paid as Ind. Code § 22-2-5-1 requires may sue for the unpaid wages, and the court must award a reasonable attorney fee as costs [^in-sop-wpa-fees-enforcement], together with court costs and, where the employer was not acting in good faith, liquidated damages of two times the wages due [^in-sop-wpa-double-damages-enforcement].

The commissioner of labor may refer wage claims under the wage claims chapter to the attorney general, and the attorney fee and liquidated-damages provisions of Ind. Code § 22-2-5-2 apply to civil actions brought on that referral [^in-sop-ag-referral]. An employer that assesses a fine against an employee and keeps it from wages commits a Class C infraction [^in-sop-fines-prohibited-enforcement]. For a physician, a repayment provision that falls within the 2025 definition and is entered into in violation of section 2.3, meaning between a physician and a hospital, a parent company or affiliated manager of a hospital, or a hospital system in an agreement originally entered into on or after July 1, 2025, is void and unenforceable [^in-sop-physician-repayment-noncompete-enforcement] [^in-sop-physician-ban-enforcement].

## What repayment structure is safest under Indiana law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A stipulated repayment sum risks being held a penalty when it is grossly disproportionate to the loss an early departure may cause [^in-sop-press-a-dent-penalty-safest], so, as a drafting recommendation rather than an assured result, the amount should match the employer's documented cost and decline as the employee serves the commitment. Any deduction of a stay-or-pay repayment from wages, including final wages, needs a wage assignment that is written, signed by the employee personally, revocable on written notice and agreed to in writing by the employer, which as a drafting recommendation is best kept in its own document [^in-sop-assignment-conditions-safest], and that serves a listed purpose such as training reimbursement not funded by an economic development incentive [^in-sop-listed-purposes-training-safest]. Where no unrevoked assignment covers the balance, paying final wages in full on time avoids liability for the unpaid wages and a reasonable attorney fee [^in-sop-wpa-fees-safest], and for liquidated damages of two times the wages due where the employer was not acting in good faith [^in-sop-wpa-double-damages-safest]; any balance the employee owes under an enforceable repayment term is then a matter for demand or suit. Under an agreement originally entered into on or after July 1, 2025, a hospital, a parent company or affiliated manager of a hospital, or a hospital system may not impose a repayment of bonuses or training expenses that applies to a physician it has employed for at least three years and is based solely or primarily on the physician's decision to practice with a new employer [^in-sop-physician-repayment-noncompete-safest] [^in-sop-physician-ban-safest]. A service-based trigger may avoid the specifically listed repayment category, but it does not establish that the provision falls outside the definition's broader purpose-or-effect language [^in-sop-physician-repayment-noncompete-safest].

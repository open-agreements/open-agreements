# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Alabama[^about]

Alabama has no statute aimed at private-employer stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment clause is tested as a contract under the penalty rule and, if it is triggered by competing, under the restrictive-covenant statute, while Alabama has no general wage-deduction statute and the federal minimum wage limits taking the money out of pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Alabama? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** No Alabama statute specifically governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or other employee repayment agreements in private employment. Where a repayment term fixes the amount owed on a breach, the Alabama Supreme Court distinguishes enforceable liquidated damages from a void penalty by three criteria [^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria]. Where the payment is triggered by competing, it is also a restraint of trade [^al-sop-watwood-payment-is-restraint], which Alabama voids unless Ala. Code § 8-1-190 allows it [^al-sop-restraint-void].

"First, the injury caused by the breach must be difficult or impossible to accurately estimate; second, the parties must intend to provide for damages rather than for a penalty; and, third, the sum stipulated must be a reasonable pre-breach [estimate] of the probable loss."[^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria]

In *Sutton v. Epperson*, the court restated that three-part test from its earlier decision in *Camelot Music, Inc. v. Marx Realty & Improvement Co.* [^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria]. A clause that fails any one of the three criteria fails as a penalty [^al-sop-sutton-fails-any-criterion], and in cases of doubtful intention Alabama courts lean toward reading the stipulated sum as a penalty [^al-sop-sutton-lean-against]. Because the criteria separately require that the loss be difficult to estimate and that the parties intend damages rather than a penalty, evidence of what the employer spent on training or recruiting does not by itself satisfy all three [^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria].

A payment owed for going to work for a competitor is treated differently. In *Associated Surgeons, P.A. v. Watwood*, the Alabama Supreme Court held that a contract requiring payment of damages if one party competes with the other is a contract restraining the exercise of a lawful profession [^al-sop-watwood-payment-is-restraint]. Casting the provision as liquidated damages rather than as a prohibition did not change that result [^al-sop-watwood-form-not-significant]. *Watwood* applied the predecessor statute; the current statute, Ala. Code § 8-1-190(a), uses the same operative words and voids every contract restraining a lawful profession, trade or business except as that section provides [^al-sop-restraint-void]. The listed exceptions are allowed only to preserve a protectable interest [^al-sop-exceptions-protectable-interest]. One lets an employee of a commercial entity agree not to carry on a similar business within a specified geographic area, so long as the entity carries on a like business there, subject to reasonable time and place limits, and it presumes a restraint of two years or less reasonable [^al-sop-employee-noncompete-exception]. An agreement under the statute must be in writing, signed by all parties and supported by adequate consideration [^al-sop-writing-signed-consideration].

A repayment owed on any early departure, whatever work the employee does next, does not by its terms condition the payment on competing. In *Anniston Urologic Associates v. Kline*, the Alabama Supreme Court held that a reduction in a departing physician's stock price, owed because he left without giving nine months' written notice, was not a restraint of trade, because the term did not restrain him from practicing for any period of time or within any area [^al-sop-kline-notice-covenant-terms][^al-sop-kline-notice-term-no-restraint]. The court declined to read the predecessor restraint statute as voiding a duly bargained-for notice provision [^al-sop-kline-notice-provision-not-void]. *Kline* concerned a notice term under the predecessor statute, and no Alabama appellate decision located for this guide applies Ala. Code § 8-1-190 to a training-repayment or bonus-repayment clause, so the conservative course is to keep competition out of the repayment trigger and to make the amount meet the three *Sutton* criteria.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> Using entry into competing work as the repayment trigger exposes the clause to invalidation as a restraint of trade unless an exception in Ala. Code § 8-1-190(b) applies [^al-sop-watwood-payment-is-restraint][^al-sop-restraint-void].

## Can an Alabama employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Alabama appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a bonus or other payment already made can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. Alabama has no general wage-payment statute of its own [^al-sop-no-state-wage-laws-clawback], and where the term stipulates a fixed sum as damages for leaving early, that sum must meet the three criteria that separate liquidated damages from a penalty [^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria-clawback]. Recovering it out of later paychecks raises a separate question, because Alabama voids assignments of wages to be earned in the future and applies that rule to orders employees give covering future wages [^al-sop-future-wage-assignment-void-clawback].

Forfeiture of an unpaid benefit is analyzed differently from a payment demanded from the employee. In *Courington v. Birmingham Trust National Bank*, a profit-sharing plan forfeited the employer's matching contributions if a former employee went into direct competition, and the Alabama Supreme Court held that the plan did not restrict the employee's entry into a competitive endeavor, because the contributions were forfeited only when he competed [^al-sop-courington-forfeiture-not-restraint]. The court therefore held that the agreement was not the type of contract the restraint-of-trade statute prohibits [^al-sop-courington-not-proscribed]. *Courington* applied the predecessor statute to employer contributions not yet paid out; it does not decide whether money already paid to the employee can be demanded back.

No Alabama decision located for this guide decides whether an employee's authorization letting the employer deduct a repayment from later paychecks is an order covering future wages under Ala. Code § 8-5-21. The conservative course is to collect a disputed clawback by demand or lawsuit rather than by relying on an authorization that covers wages not yet earned.

## Can an Alabama employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Alabama has no general state statute governing final-wage payment or wage deductions [^al-sop-no-state-wage-laws], although Ala. Code § 8-5-21 voids assignments of, and employee orders covering, wages to be earned in the future [^al-sop-future-wage-assignment-void]. The main limit on a deduction for an employer's benefit is the federal minimum wage: under the federal rule, wages count as paid only when paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^al-sop-flsa-free-and-clear], and the federal wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^al-sop-flsa-kickback], which the federal rule illustrates as a violation in any workweek when an employer-required cost cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^al-sop-flsa-tools-workweek].

The federal minimum wage for a covered employee is $7.25 an hour [^al-sop-federal-minimum-wage]. No federal rule quoted here addresses training repayment specifically, so the conservative course is to keep any repayment taken from the final paycheck from cutting into the final workweek's minimum or overtime wages.

No Alabama decision located for this guide applies that section to an employer's own repayment deduction, so the conservative course is to have the employee sign any deduction authorization after the final pay has been earned, rather than relying on an authorization signed at hire.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-pay deduction authorization with no minimum-wage floor lets the deduction take a workweek's pay below the federal minimum wage, which leaves the employer liable for the unpaid minimum wages plus an equal amount as liquidated damages [^al-sop-flsa-liquidated-damages], which the court may reduce or deny only if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds for believing it was not violating the Act [^al-sop-flsa-good-faith].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Alabama? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Alabama has no statute that gives private-employer training, tuition or relocation repayment a separate rule. Where such a term stipulates a sum as damages for leaving early, it is tested under the same liquidated-damages criteria as a bonus repayment [^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria-training]. No Alabama decision located for this guide decides whether a promise to repay an advance of actual training, tuition or relocation costs is stipulated damages or an ordinary debt, so the conservative course is to tie the amount to documented costs and draft it to satisfy those criteria as well. Training matters under the restrictive-covenant statute in one respect: specialized and unique training involving substantial business expenditure directed to a particular employee is a protectable interest that can support a restraint, but only if the training is specifically set forth in writing as the consideration for the restraint [^al-sop-training-protectable-interest].

Job skills in and of themselves, without more, are not protectable interests [^al-sop-job-skills-not-protectable]. The training-reimbursement statutes located for this guide cover public employers and make the new public employer, not the employee, pay. When a law enforcement officer, certified corrections officer or firefighter of the state or a political subdivision is hired by a different public agency within 36 months after completing mandated training, the training expenses must be reimbursed to the agency that paid for the training [^al-sop-public-safety-training-reimbursement], and that agency demands payment from the new employer [^al-sop-public-safety-demand-new-employer]. A parallel rule applies to emergency medical services personnel hired by another public employer within 24 months after training [^al-sop-emsp-training-reimbursement]. Neither statute governs a private employer's repayment agreement. The training provision therefore matters only where the repayment term is also a restraint, such as a payment triggered by competing; it does not authorize a repayment amount that fails the liquidated-damages criteria. For an agreement subject to the restrictive-covenant article, the statute lists actual damages or lawful liquidated damages provided in the contract among the remedies [^al-sop-covenant-remedies-training].

Where the federal minimum-wage rules apply, a training repayment taken from wages is measured against them in the same way as any other deduction for the employer's benefit: the federal wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer or for the employer's benefit [^al-sop-flsa-kickback-training], and the federal rule's example treats such a cost as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^al-sop-flsa-tools-workweek-training].

## How are Alabama repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Where a repayment term is part of an agreement under Alabama's restrictive-covenant article, the remedies for breach include injunctive relief, actual damages or lawful liquidated damages provided in the contract, and attorneys' fees or costs if the contract or other law provides for them [^al-sop-covenant-remedies]. If an employer obtains a judgment and collects it by garnishing wages, Alabama exempts 75 percent of a resident employee's wages from the garnishment [^al-sop-garnishment-exemption], and federal law separately caps an ordinary garnishment at the lesser of 25 percent of weekly disposable earnings or the amount by which those earnings exceed thirty times the federal minimum hourly wage [^al-sop-federal-garnishment-cap]. On the employee's side, where a deduction for the employer's benefit takes pay below the federal minimum wage, the employee may recover the resulting unpaid minimum wages or overtime plus an equal amount as liquidated damages [^al-sop-flsa-liquidated-damages-enforcement], unless the court reduces or denies them because the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds [^al-sop-flsa-good-faith-enforcement].

Under Alabama law, actions to recover wages, damages, fees or penalties accruing under laws respecting the payment of wages must be brought within two years [^al-sop-wage-action-two-years].

## What repayment structure is safest under Alabama law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A stipulated repayment amount survives the penalty rule only where the loss from an early departure is difficult or impossible to estimate accurately, the parties intend to provide for damages rather than a penalty, and the amount is a reasonable pre-breach estimate of the probable loss [^al-sop-sutton-three-criteria-safest]. Making the amount due on any early departure, rather than on joining a competitor, keeps the term from operating as a payment for competing, which the Alabama Supreme Court treats as a restraint of trade [^al-sop-watwood-payment-is-restraint-safest]. Collecting by demand or lawsuit avoids the bar on assignments of, and employee orders covering, wages to be earned in the future [^al-sop-future-wage-assignment-void-safest], and, for an employee covered by the Fair Labor Standards Act, a deduction for the employer's benefit violates the federal wage requirements in any workweek when it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^al-sop-flsa-kickback-safest][^al-sop-flsa-tools-workweek-safest].

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Washington[^about]

Washington repayment terms raise separate questions about payroll deductions, final pay, wage rebates, training costs and the noncompetition statute amended effective June 30, 2027.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Washington? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** From June 30, 2027, a Washington repayment term triggered by the worker engaging in lawful work is a void noncompetition covenant unless it fits the educational-expense exclusion [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027][^wa-nca-all-void-from-2027][^wa-education-carveout-enforceability]. Until then, a repayment taken out of pay during employment needs the employee's express written authorization given in advance for a lawful purpose for the employee's benefit [^wa-ongoing-deduction-written-enforceability].
Under the version of chapter 49.62 RCW in force until June 30, 2027, a noncompetition covenant means every written or oral covenant, agreement, or contract by which an employee or independent contractor is prohibited or restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind [^wa-nca-definition-until-2027]. That definition carries its own exclusions: a nonsolicitation agreement, a confidentiality agreement, a covenant protecting trade secrets or inventions, a qualifying covenant on the sale of a business interest, and a qualifying franchise covenant are outside it [^wa-nca-exclusions-until-2027].
Under that same version, a covenant within the definition is void and unenforceable on any of several grounds. The employer must have disclosed the terms in writing no later than the initial acceptance of the offer of employment, or given independent consideration for a covenant entered into after employment begins; the employee's annualized earnings from the party seeking enforcement must exceed the statutory figure; and where the employee is terminated in a layoff, enforcement requires paying compensation equivalent to base salary for the enforcement period, less what the employee earns elsewhere [^wa-nca-conditions-until-2027]. Where the agreement becomes enforceable only at a later date because the employee's compensation changes, the employer must also specifically disclose that the agreement may be enforceable against the employee in the future [^wa-nca-conditions-until-2027]. A court or arbitrator must also presume any covenant running more than eighteen months after termination unreasonable, rebuttable only by clear and convincing evidence [^wa-nca-eighteen-months].
The earnings figures in the statute are base amounts, not the operative ones. RCW 49.62.040 requires the Department of Labor and Industries to adjust them for inflation every September 30th, effective the following January 1st [^wa-nca-annual-adjustment]. The Department publishes the adjusted figures: for 2026 they are $126,858.83 for an employee and $317,147.09 for an independent contractor [^wa-lni-thresholds]. The operative threshold is the Department's annually adjusted figure, not the number printed in the statute.
From June 30, 2027, the picture changes. The definition then also reaches any provision in an agreement that threatens, demands, requires, or otherwise effectuates that an individual return, repay, or forfeit any right, benefit, or compensation as a consequence of engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027], and beginning on that date all noncompetition covenants are void and unenforceable regardless of when the parties entered into them [^wa-nca-all-void-from-2027].
Today, a reader is governed by the version in force until June 30, 2027. On June 30, 2027 an existing agreement is affected only so far as it is a noncompetition covenant under the new definition. Paragraph (d) adds an express route for a repayment term: a provision requiring repayment as a consequence of the individual engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business is included [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027], alongside the general definition's reach to agreements that otherwise prohibit or restrain lawful work [^wa-nca-general-definition-from-2027]; and an agreement within either route is still outside the definition if it fits the exclusion for a qualifying written agreement to repay out-of-pocket educational expenses [^wa-education-carveout-enforceability]. Money actually taken out of pay is a separate question, governed by the wage rules covered in the sections below.

"(1) Beginning on June 30, 2027, all noncompetition covenants are void and unenforceable regardless of when the parties entered into the noncompetition covenant."[^wa-nca-all-void-from-2027]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A repayment clause that qualifies as a noncompetition covenant is unenforceable from June 30, 2027 if it was drafted on the assumption that signing before that date preserves enforceability [^wa-nca-all-void-from-2027].

## Does Washington's non-compete statute reach a repayment term? {#noncompete-overlay}

**Short answer.** Until June 30, 2027, Washington's noncompetition statute reaches a repayment agreement if it prohibits or restrains an employee or independent contractor from engaging in lawful work [^wa-nca-definition-until-2027-overlay]. The definition also includes an agreement that directly or indirectly prohibits the acceptance or transaction of business with a customer [^wa-nca-customer-until-2027]. From June 30, 2027, the definition is written to include repayment and forfeiture as well — any provision that threatens, demands, requires, or otherwise effectuates that an individual return, repay, or forfeit any right, benefit, or compensation as a consequence of engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027-overlay].
Two related limits matter to anyone drafting today. Until June 30, 2027, a noncompetition covenant is void and unenforceable against an independent contractor unless the contractor's earnings from the party seeking enforcement exceed the statutory figure [^wa-nca-contractor-threshold], which is subject to the same annual adjustment and stands at $317,147.09 for 2026 [^wa-nca-annual-adjustment-contractor][^wa-lni-thresholds-contractor]. And, as the chapter stands today and subject to its own exceptions, an employer may not restrict, restrain, or prohibit an employee earning less than twice the applicable state minimum hourly wage from having an additional job, supplementing their income by working for another employer, working as an independent contractor, or being self-employed [^wa-nca-second-job]. That protection does not reach additional services that raise safety issues for the employee, coworkers, or the public, or that interfere with the employer's reasonable and normal scheduling expectations, and it does not alter an employee's existing obligations including the common law duty of loyalty [^wa-nca-second-job-exceptions].

"A ‘noncompetition covenant’ also includes any provision in an agreement that threatens, demands, requires, or otherwise effectuates that an individual return, repay, or forfeit any right, benefit, or compensation, as a consequence of the individual engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind."[^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027-overlay]

## Can a Washington employer claw back pay already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** An employer may not collect back wages already paid as a rebate, but a deduction the employee expressly authorized in writing and in advance for a lawful purpose for the employee's benefit, with no financial benefit to the employer and openly recorded, is outside that prohibition [^wa-wage-rebate-offence][^wa-rebate-safe-harbour]. The liability has a defined predicate. RCW 49.52.050 makes it an offence for an employer to collect or receive from an employee a rebate of any part of wages already paid, or wilfully and with intent to deprive the employee of any part of his or her wages to pay a lower wage than the employer is obligated to pay by statute, ordinance, or contract [^wa-wage-rebate-offence]. RCW 49.52.070 attaches civil liability to violations of those two paragraphs, not to any recovery from wages an employer might attempt. The predicate is also bounded: RCW 49.52.060 provides that the prohibition does not make it unlawful to withhold or divert wages where a law requires or empowers it, or where the employee expressly authorized the deduction in writing and in advance for a lawful purpose accruing to the employee's benefit, provided the employer derives no financial benefit and the deduction is openly, clearly and in due course recorded in the employer's books [^wa-rebate-safe-harbour]. Where an employer's conduct does meet one of those two paragraphs and falls outside the safe harbour, RCW 49.52.070 makes it liable in a civil action brought by the aggrieved employee, or the employee's assignee, for twice the amount by way of exemplary damages, together with costs of suit and a reasonable sum for attorney fees; that doubling is not available to an employee who has knowingly submitted to such violations [^wa-wage-rebate-damages].
WAC 296-126-030 permits recovery of payments exceeding the agreed wage rate or actual hours worked; it does not establish a general right to recover correctly paid compensation under a repayment agreement [^wa-overpayment-definition]. An overpayment for this purpose occurs only where the employer paid the employee more than the agreed-upon wage rate or for more hours than were actually worked, recouping it may reduce the employee's gross wages below the state minimum wage, and the employer cannot recover where the disputed amount concerns the quality of work [^wa-overpayment-definition]. It also does not apply to public employers [^wa-overpayment-process]. Within that scope, an employer can recover an overpayment from an employee's paycheck provided the overpayment was infrequent and inadvertent — infrequent meaning rarely, not occurring regularly, or not showing a pattern, and inadvertent meaning an error that was accidental, unintentional, or not deliberately done — and the burden of proving the inadvertent error rests with the employer who made it [^wa-overpayment-recovery]. The employer has ninety days from the initial overpayment to detect it and implement a plan with the employee to collect it [^wa-overpayment-ninety-days], and if it is not detected within that ninety-day period the employer cannot adjust the employee's current or future wages to recoup it [^wa-overpayment-not-detected].
The process is prescribed, not left to the employer. Before any adjustment the employer must give the employee advance written notice stating the terms on which the overpayment will be recouped, must provide documentation of the overpayment, and must identify and record all wage adjustments openly and clearly in payroll records; and, regardless of the provisions of that rule, the employer retains a right of private legal action to recover an overpayment where appropriate [^wa-overpayment-process].
Not being able to take money out of pay is not the same as having no claim. RCW 49.48.010(4) provides that the wage-withholding limit in subsection (3) of that section is not to be construed to affect an employer's or former employer's right to sue upon or collect any debt owed by an employee or former employee [^wa-debt-collection-preserved].

"(4) Subsection (3) of this section shall not be construed to affect the right of any employer or former employer to sue upon or collect any debt owed to said employer or former employer by his or her employees or former employees."[^wa-debt-collection-preserved]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A repayment clause authorizing payroll offsets without advance written employee authorization risks an unlawful wage deduction and potential double damages, costs, and attorney fees [^wa-rebate-safe-harbour][^wa-wage-rebate-damages].

## Can repayment come out of a Washington final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A repayment can come out of the final paycheck as a payment to the employer as creditor, if the employee authorized it in advance for the employee's own benefit [^wa-final-creditor-route]. The rule's example is an employer loan repaid by payroll deduction under a written advance agreement. Final pay also has its own timing rule. When an employee ceases to work for an employer, whether by discharge or by voluntary withdrawal, the wages due on account of that employment must be paid at the end of the established pay period [^wa-final-wages-due].
WAC 296-126-025(3) then limits final-paycheck deductions by timing. An employer can deduct wages from a final paycheck for the reasons in (a), (b), (c), and (d) of that subsection, but only when those incidents occurred in the final pay period [^wa-final-deduction-timing], and may not deduct from the final paycheck for incidents that occurred in previous pay periods under (a) through (d) [^wa-final-deduction-prior-periods]. None of the deductions in that subsection may reduce the employee's final gross wages below the state minimum wage in effect at the time the work was performed [^wa-final-deduction-minimum-wage].
Subsection (3) is not the route a repayment usually travels, though. A separate list in subsection (2) covers deductions specifically agreed upon orally or in writing by the employee, and deductions on that list may reduce final gross wages below the state minimum wage [^wa-final-deduction-agreed] — the opposite of the subsection (3) rule above. That list includes a payment to a creditor or third party the employee authorized in advance for the employee's own benefit, and the rule says in terms that the creditor or third party can be the employer. Its worked example is an employer loan repaid by payroll deduction under a written advance agreement [^wa-final-creditor-route]. Which subsection a repayment falls under therefore decides both the authorization required and the wage floor.
Where a final-pay deduction rests on an agreement, the paperwork burden sits with the employer: it is the employer's responsibility to prove the existence of any agreement [^wa-final-deduction-proof].

"An employer may not deduct wages from the final paycheck for incidents that occurred in previous pay periods under (a) through (d) of this subsection."[^wa-final-deduction-prior-periods]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-pay deduction clause relying on WAC 296-126-025(3)(a) through (d) risks an unlawful deduction if it covers incidents from earlier pay periods or permits final wages below the minimum wage [^wa-final-deduction-prior-periods][^wa-final-deduction-minimum-wage].

## Does Washington treat training or tuition repayments differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** A training, tuition, or relocation repayment deducted from pay during employment under WAC 296-126-028(2) requires that the employee expressly authorized the deduction in writing and in advance for a lawful purpose for the employee's benefit, with no financial profit or benefit to the employer [^wa-ongoing-deduction-written][^wa-deduction-no-profit]. During an on-going employment relationship, an employer may deduct wages when the employee expressly authorizes the deduction in writing and in advance for a lawful purpose for the benefit of the employee [^wa-ongoing-deduction-written]. The same regulation adds that neither the employer nor any person acting in the interest of the employer can derive any financial profit or benefit from any of the deductions under it [^wa-deduction-no-profit]. Those requirements — written and in advance, for a lawful purpose benefiting the employee, with no financial profit or benefit to the employer — govern a discretionary repayment deduction that relies on that subsection. The same regulation adds that reasonable interest charged by the employer for a loan or credit extended to the employee is not treated as a financial benefit to the employer [^wa-reasonable-interest]. Calling a repayment a benefit to the employee does not establish that it is one; the lawful purpose has to be real.
The date matters here too. From June 30, 2027, a provision that threatens, demands, requires, or otherwise effectuates that an individual return, repay, or forfeit any right, benefit, or compensation, as a consequence of engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business, falls within the chapter's definition of a noncompetition covenant [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027-training].
Chapter 49.62 RCW carves one repayment term out of that definition by name, and it reaches out-of-pocket educational expenses only — not relocation, and not training costs that are not out-of-pocket educational expenses. From June 30, 2027 the definition of a noncompetition covenant expressly does not include a written agreement to repay out-of-pocket educational expenses where the agreement expires within 18 months of the employee's start date for employment, limits repayment to the pro rata portion of the remaining time of the 18-month period, and releases the employee from the obligation to repay if the separation from employment is based on good cause under RCW 50.20.050, the unemployment-benefits good-cause statute [^wa-education-carveout]. Those three conditions, in addition to the writing and the out-of-pocket educational subject matter, are all part of the exclusion. An educational-expense repayment agreement that runs longer than 18 months, that does not prorate, or that survives a separation for good cause under RCW 50.20.050 does not qualify for the exclusion [^wa-education-carveout]; and if it also meets the trigger in paragraph (d) — repayment as a consequence of the individual engaging in lawful work [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027-training] — it remains inside the definition of a noncompetition covenant and is void from June 30, 2027 [^wa-nca-all-void-from-2027-training]. Failing the carve-out does not by itself put an agreement inside the definition, and fitting the carve-out is what keeps it outside.

"(2) During an on-going employment relationship, an employer may deduct wages when the employee expressly authorizes the deduction in writing and in advance for a lawful purpose for the benefit of the employee."[^wa-ongoing-deduction-written]

## How is Washington's noncompetition statute enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** Washington provides private remedies for violations of its noncompetition statute, subject to a current exception for certain pre-2020 covenants [^wa-nca-action-until-2027][^wa-nca-remedies-from-2027][^wa-nca-pre-2020-bar]. One category is carved out under the current version: no cause of action may be brought regarding a noncompetition covenant signed before January 1, 2020 that is not being enforced or explicitly leveraged [^wa-nca-pre-2020-bar].
Under the version in force until June 30, 2027, upon a violation of the chapter the attorney general, on behalf of a person or persons, may pursue any and all relief, and a person aggrieved by a noncompetition covenant may bring a cause of action for the relief the chapter provides [^wa-nca-action-until-2027]. If a court or arbitrator determines that a noncompetition covenant violates the chapter, the violator must pay the aggrieved person the greater of actual damages or a statutory penalty of five thousand dollars, plus reasonable attorneys' fees, expenses, and costs incurred in the proceeding [^wa-nca-penalty-until-2027].
Under the version taking effect June 30, 2027, the conduct that counts as a violation is spelled out: it is a violation for an employer to enforce, attempt to enforce, or threaten to enforce a noncompetition covenant against an employee or worker, to represent that the employee or worker is subject to one, or to enter into or attempt to enter into one [^wa-nca-violation-from-2027]. From that date, a person aggrieved by a violation of the chapter may bring a cause of action, and the violator must pay the greater of actual damages or the five thousand dollar statutory penalty plus fees, expenses, and costs [^wa-nca-remedies-from-2027].
Which version applies turns on when the case begins, for four named sections. RCW 49.62.010, 49.62.020, 49.62.080 and 49.62.090 apply to all proceedings commenced on or after June 30, 2027, regardless of when the cause of action arose [^wa-nca-application-from-2027], and legal proceedings commenced before that date are governed by the chapter as amended prior to it [^wa-nca-pending-proceedings].

"(2) It is a violation of this chapter for an employer to enforce, attempt to enforce, or threaten to enforce against an employee or worker any noncompetition covenant, to represent that the employee or worker is subject to a noncompetition covenant, or to enter into or attempt to enter into a noncompetition covenant with an employee or worker."[^wa-nca-violation-from-2027]

## What repayment structure is safest under Washington law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A Washington repayment term built to last past June 30, 2027 is safest if it does not require repayment as a consequence of the worker engaging in lawful work [^wa-nca-repayment-from-2027-structure], or, for out-of-pocket educational expenses, if it fits the statutory exclusion's 18-month, pro rata and good-cause conditions [^wa-education-carveout-structure].
Beginning on June 30, 2027, all noncompetition covenants are void and unenforceable regardless of when the parties entered into the covenant [^wa-nca-all-void-from-2027-structure]. From that date it is also a violation for an employer to enforce, attempt to enforce, or threaten to enforce a noncompetition covenant, to represent that a worker is subject to one, or to enter into or attempt to enter into one [^wa-nca-violation-from-2027-structure].
By October 1, 2027, an employer must make reasonable efforts to provide written notice to all current and former employees and independent contractors whose noncompetition covenant is still within its effective time period that the covenant is void and unenforceable [^wa-nca-notice-october-2027]; the duty is one of reasonable efforts to deliver those written notices by that deadline, including to former workers.
For a Washington-based employee or independent contractor, a provision of a noncompetition covenant is void and unenforceable if it requires adjudication outside Washington, to the extent it deprives the person of the protections or benefits of the chapter, or if it allows or requires the application of choice of law principles or the substantive law of another jurisdiction [^wa-nca-choice-of-law].
On the money side, the rule itself points to paperwork: the department recommends that all agreements, policies, and procedures be in writing and signed by the affected employees [^wa-written-signed-agreements].

"A provision in a noncompetition covenant signed by an employee or independent contractor who is Washington-based is void and unenforceable: (1) If the covenant requires the employee or independent contractor to adjudicate a noncompetition covenant outside of this state; (2) To the extent it deprives the employee or independent contractor of the protections or benefits of this chapter; or (3) If it allows or requires the application of choice of law principles or the substantive law of any jurisdiction other than Washington state."[^wa-nca-choice-of-law]

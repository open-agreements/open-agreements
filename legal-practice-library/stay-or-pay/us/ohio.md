# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Ohio[^about]

Ohio has no statute in force that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so courts enforce repayment promises as contracts, test a fixed amount stipulated for leaving under the liquidated-damages penalty rule and limit cost reimbursement to proven costs, while the wage-assignment statutes and the minimum wage separately limit collecting the debt from pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Ohio? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Ohio courts generally enforce a stay-or-pay repayment promise as a contract [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-enforceable], but a fixed amount stipulated as damages for leaving early is enforced only if it is liquidated damages, not a penalty, under the *Samson Sales* test [^oh-samson-tripartite-test-enforceable]. Under that test the damages must be uncertain and difficult to prove, the contract as a whole must not be so unconscionable, unreasonable and disproportionate that it fails to express the parties' true intention, and the parties must have intended the stated amount to follow a breach [^oh-samson-tripartite-test-enforceable]. Ohio has no statute in force that bans or limits stay-or-pay, training-repayment or sign-on bonus repayment terms in private employment.
A stipulated amount is enforced if it was reasonable when the contract was formed and bears a reasonable, though not necessarily exact, relation to actual damages [^oh-lake-ridge-reasonable-relation-enforceable]. A penalty, by contrast, is designed to coerce performance by punishing nonperformance [^oh-lake-ridge-penalty-coerces-enforceable], and courts ordinarily treat a manifestly inequitable and unrealistic amount as a penalty [^oh-samson-manifestly-inequitable-enforceable].
Ohio appellate courts have enforced training repayment, and they have limited recovery to the cost of the training actually given when the agreed figure exceeded it [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-enforceable][^oh-robinson-actual-damages-enforceable]. In *NetJets, Inc. v. Binning*, the Tenth District affirmed a $14,914 judgment against a former employee because a valid contract existed and his voluntary resignation triggered his obligation to reimburse training costs [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-enforceable]. In *United Assn. Local 168 v. Robinson*, the Fourth District held that a $40,000 repayment term for a five-year apprenticeship was a penalty, in part because the figure bore no reasonable relationship to the cost of the less than two years of training actually provided, but it held that the training fund could still recover its actual damages for the training given [^oh-robinson-flat-sum-penalty-enforceable][^oh-robinson-actual-damages-enforceable]. In *GROB Systems, Inc. v. McDermott*, the Third District upheld an award of $739.11 to an employer under an apprenticeship repayment agreement, calculated by prorating its training costs by the days remaining in the commitment, after the trial court treated the reimbursement clause as not a liquidated damages provision [^oh-grob-prorated-damages-enforceable][^oh-grob-days-remaining-enforceable][^oh-grob-not-liquidated-damages-enforceable]. A covenant accompanying a repayment term that restrains the employee from competing with the former employer is tested under *Raimonde v. Van Vlerah*, which asks whether it is no greater than the employer needs, imposes no undue hardship on the employee and does not injure the public [^oh-raimonde-reasonableness-enforceable].
Senate Bill 11 of the 136th General Assembly is a pending bill, not law: it was referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee on January 29, 2025, and its legislative status page showed no later action when we checked it on November 19, 2026 [^oh-sb11-status-committee-enforceable]. As introduced, it would prohibit an agreement requiring a departing worker to reimburse training costs, and agreements imposing liquidated damages or a retraining fee for leaving, and it would void only agreements entered into, modified or extended after it took effect [^oh-sb11-training-reimbursement-enforceable][^oh-sb11-exit-fees-enforceable][^oh-sb11-prospective-only-enforceable].

"If the provision was reasonable at the time of formation and it bears a reasonable (not necessarily exact) relation to actual damages, the provision will be enforced."[^oh-lake-ridge-reasonable-relation-enforceable]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A flat repayment amount that stays the same however much of the commitment the employee served risks being held a penalty, leaving the employer to prove its actual or prorated training cost [^oh-robinson-flat-sum-penalty-caution][^oh-grob-prorated-damages-caution].

## Can an Ohio employer claw back pay already received? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** An Ohio employer can recover a bonus paid in advance when the employee leaves before meeting the agreed conditions [^oh-commuteair-repay-if-left-clawback][^oh-commuteair-judgment-affirmed-clawback], but a bonus the employee has already earned must be paid [^oh-wall-earned-bonus-owed-clawback]. In *CommuteAir, L.L.C. v. Bremer*, the agreement required a pilot trainee to repay a $22,100 new-hire incentive bonus if he left the program, did not complete the required training or was terminated for cause, and the Eighth District affirmed judgment for the employer, holding that the payment was a bonus and not a wage [^oh-commuteair-repay-if-left-clawback][^oh-commuteair-repay-conditions-clawback][^oh-commuteair-judgment-affirmed-clawback][^oh-commuteair-bonus-not-wage-clawback]. In *Wall v. Pizza Outlet, L.P.*, by contrast, the Fifth District held that a manager who had met every condition of a profit-share bonus except continued employment had earned it before his termination, so the employer had to pay it, and it treated a bonus whose timing alone the employer controlled as more in the nature of a commission [^oh-wall-earned-bonus-owed-clawback][^oh-wall-timing-commission-clawback].
No Ohio statute addresses clawing back a sign-on, retention or relocation payment, so recovery turns on the contract: Ohio courts have enforced repayment promises [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-clawback], allowed recovery of actual damages where a fixed figure failed [^oh-robinson-actual-damages-clawback], and ordinarily treat a manifestly inequitable and unrealistic stipulated amount as a penalty [^oh-samson-manifestly-inequitable-clawback]. Earned wages are protected by the minimum wage, which every covered employer must pay [^oh-4111-02-minimum-wage-clawback], and federal law treats wages kicked back to the employer, directly or indirectly, as unpaid [^fed-531-35-kickback-ohio-clawback].
Taking a bonus repayment out of later pay is limited separately. Ohio's wage-payment statute counts repayment of a loan or other obligation among employee authorized deductions [^oh-4113-15-repayment-deduction-clawback], and a payroll deduction plan survives the ban on assigning future wages only if the employee can revoke it at any time before payment [^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-clawback]. Senate Bill 11, a bill referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee on January 29, 2025, with no later action on its status page as of November 19, 2026 [^oh-sb11-status-committee-clawback], states as introduced that it would not prevent paying or forgiving a debt, or providing other benefits, after a person completes a specified period of employment [^oh-sb11-retention-forgiveness-clawback].

## Can repayment come out of an Ohio final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** The statutory safe route for taking a repayment out of Ohio wages, including final wages, is a payroll deduction plan the employee has agreed to and can revoke at any time before payment, with pay kept at or above the minimum wage and any required overtime pay [^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-final-pay][^oh-4111-02-minimum-wage-final-pay][^fed-531-35-tools-ohio-final-pay]. Ohio law makes assignments of future wages invalid except as R.C. 1321.32 provides; apart from court-ordered support, that section leaves agreed wage checkoffs between employers and employees unaffected and preserves deductions under an agreed payroll deduction plan that the employee may revoke, on notice to the employer, up to the time of payment [^oh-4113-16-no-wage-assignment-final-pay][^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-final-pay]. The wage-payment statute defines the wage due as the net amount after taxes, written-agreement fringe-benefit deductions and employee authorized deductions, and it lists repayment of a loan or other obligation as one [^oh-4113-15-net-wage-final-pay][^oh-4113-15-repayment-deduction-final-pay]. R.C. 4113.16 also bars an employer from contracting out of the wage-payment statute, so if the deduction depends solely on the revocable-plan exception, an employee's timely revocation means the employer must collect the balance separately [^oh-4113-16-no-wage-assignment-final-pay][^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-final-pay]. In our review we found no Ohio appellate decision on whether an irrevocable repayment deduction counts as an agreed checkoff, so the conservative course is to use a revocable plan.
Ohio has no separate final-paycheck deadline in that statute; by default, wages earned in the first half of a month are due by the first day of the next month, and wages earned in the second half by the fifteenth [^oh-4113-15-semimonthly-pay-final-pay]. The statute also allows daily or weekly pay and refers to a longer interval customary to a trade or occupation, or a different interval set by written contract or by law [^oh-4113-15-other-intervals-final-pay]. If wages stay unpaid thirty days past the regular payday and no dispute or counterclaim accounts for the nonpayment, the employer also owes liquidated damages of six percent of the undisputed amount or $200, whichever is greater [^oh-4113-15-six-percent-final-pay].
Every covered employer must pay at least the minimum wage [^oh-4111-02-minimum-wage-final-pay], and the conservative course is to keep pay at or above it after any repayment deduction. The Ohio minimum wage for non-tipped employees is $11.00 an hour in 2026 and is scheduled to rise to $11.40 an hour in 2027; employees under 16, and employees of employers grossing less than $405,000 in 2026 or $419,000 in 2027, are owed the federal $7.25 rate instead [^oh-commerce-2026-rate-final-pay][^oh-commerce-2027-rate-final-pay]. Federal law separately treats a payment kicked back to the employer as unpaid wages, and the regulation's example treats the cost of employer-required tools of the trade as a violation in any workweek when it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages [^fed-531-35-kickback-ohio-final-pay][^fed-531-35-tools-ohio-final-pay]. That a deduction is allowed does not make the underlying repayment debt enforceable; the employer must still have a valid claim under the contract, which for a fixed figure that fails the penalty rule is limited to actual damages [^oh-robinson-actual-damages-final-pay]. In our review we found no Ohio appellate decision on deducting a training or bonus repayment from final wages.

"This section and section 4113.16 of the Revised Code shall not affect or invalidate any deduction from the wages or salary made in accordance with a payroll deduction plan agreed upon between the employer and employee provided that the same be revocable at any time by the employee upon notice to the employer up to the time of payment thereof."[^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-final-pay]

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Withholding a repayment from final pay without an arrangement R.C. 1321.32 preserves, such as a revocable payroll deduction plan the employee has not revoked, risks leaving the deducted amount unpaid [^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-caution]; if it stays unpaid thirty days past the regular payday with no genuine dispute or counterclaim accounting for it, the employer owes the statutory six-percent or $200 liquidated damages [^oh-4113-15-six-percent-caution]. A deduction that cuts pay below the minimum wage [^oh-4111-02-minimum-wage-caution] separately exposes the employer to the constitutional remedy of back wages plus damages of twice that amount [^oh-const-34a-double-damages-caution].

## Are Ohio training or tuition repayments treated differently? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** No Ohio statute in force specifically regulates training, tuition or relocation repayment agreements in private employment. Ohio courts enforce a training-repayment promise as a contract [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-training], and they test a fixed amount stipulated as damages for leaving under the same liquidated-damages rule as any other agreed amount [^oh-samson-tripartite-test-training]. Ohio appellate courts have enforced training repayment, and when the agreed figure outran the cost of the training actually given, they have limited recovery to that cost [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-training][^oh-robinson-actual-damages-training].
In *NetJets*, the Tenth District enforced an employee's promise to reimburse training costs after he resigned voluntarily [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-training]. In *Robinson*, the Fourth District treated an apprenticeship repayment term as a work-or-pay provision that let the training fund recoup its education and training costs, not as a non-competition agreement [^oh-robinson-work-or-pay-training]. It held the $40,000 flat figure a penalty because it bore no reasonable relationship to the cost of the training actually provided, and allowed recovery of actual damages for the training given [^oh-robinson-flat-sum-penalty-training][^oh-robinson-actual-damages-training]. The court also concluded that the apprentice and the training fund were not in an employer-employee relationship [^oh-robinson-not-employee-training], so its reasoning applies to an employer's own training program by analogy. In *GROB*, an employer's apprenticeship agreement, the Third District upheld a damages award that prorated the employer's training costs by the days remaining in the commitment, after the trial court treated the reimbursement clause as not a liquidated damages provision [^oh-grob-prorated-damages-training][^oh-grob-days-remaining-training][^oh-grob-valuation-upheld-training][^oh-grob-not-liquidated-damages-training].
An Ohio employer can secure a training repayment with a cognovit note: in *Overdrive Espresso, L.L.C. v. Finein*, the Eighth District held that a cognovit note executed for an agreed $10,000 training cost, arising out of an employment contract, was not a consumer transaction [^oh-overdrive-training-cost-note-training][^oh-overdrive-note-commercial-training]. The employee's remaining challenges, including whether the amount owed adequately represented the employer's training costs, were held not to be meritorious defenses to the judgment [^oh-overdrive-amount-challenge-training][^oh-overdrive-no-meritorious-defense-training].
Federal law adds a floor: wages are not paid where the employee kicks back part of the wage to the employer, and the regulation's example treats employer-required tool costs as a violation in any workweek when they cut into the minimum or overtime wages [^fed-531-35-kickback-ohio-training][^fed-531-35-tools-ohio-training]. Senate Bill 11, referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee on January 29, 2025, with no later action on its status page as of November 19, 2026 [^oh-sb11-status-committee-training], would, if enacted as introduced, void a new agreement requiring a departing worker to reimburse training, orientation or evaluation costs, but only for agreements entered into, modified or extended after it took effect [^oh-sb11-training-reimbursement-training][^oh-sb11-prospective-only-training].

"However, the trial court erred in granting summary judgment in favor of Robinson on the issue of damages because JATC was entitled to recoup its actual damages for training and educating Robinson during the actual time he spent in the apprenticeship program."[^oh-robinson-actual-damages-training]

## What happens to an Ohio employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An Ohio employer that withholds pay below the minimum wage must, within thirty days of a finding by the state or a court, pay back wages, damages of an additional two times the back wages, and the employee's costs and reasonable attorney fees [^oh-const-34a-double-damages-enforcement]. An employee or the attorney general may sue within three years of the violation, or of when a continuing violation ceased, or within one year after the employee is notified of the state's final disposition of a complaint, whichever is later [^oh-const-34a-three-years-enforcement].
Wages left unpaid thirty days past the regular payday, with no dispute or counterclaim accounting for the nonpayment, also carry liquidated damages of six percent of the undisputed amount or $200, whichever is greater [^oh-4113-15-six-percent-enforcement].
An employer suing to collect a written repayment promise has six years after the claim accrues to bring the action [^oh-2305-06-six-years-enforcement]. In *Overdrive Espresso*, the Eighth District upheld a judgment confessed on a cognovit note executed for an agreed $10,000 training cost, holding that the note arose out of an employment contract rather than a consumer transaction and that the employee raised no meritorious defense [^oh-overdrive-training-cost-note-enforcement][^oh-overdrive-note-commercial-enforcement][^oh-overdrive-no-meritorious-defense-enforcement].

## What repayment structure is safest under Ohio law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** The safest Ohio repayment term is a clear contractual promise, which Ohio courts enforce [^oh-netjets-training-reimbursed-structure], with an amount that is reasonable when signed and bears a reasonable relation to the employer's likely loss, because that is what Ohio courts require to enforce an agreed amount [^oh-lake-ridge-reasonable-relation-structure][^oh-samson-tripartite-test-structure]. As a drafting inference, that means tying the amount to documented training or hiring costs and reducing it as the employee serves the commitment: the Fourth District refused a flat figure that ignored how much training had been provided, and the Third District approved damages that prorated training costs by the days remaining [^oh-robinson-flat-sum-penalty-structure][^oh-grob-prorated-damages-structure][^oh-grob-days-remaining-structure].
If the employer wants to deduct a balance from pay, the deduction should run through a payroll deduction plan the employee agrees to and may revoke at any time before payment, because that is the arrangement R.C. 1321.32 expressly preserves from the ban on assigning future wages [^oh-1321-32-revocable-plan-structure][^oh-4113-16-no-wage-assignment-structure], and it should leave at least the minimum wage and any required overtime pay [^oh-4111-02-minimum-wage-structure][^fed-531-35-tools-ohio-structure]; the wage-payment statute counts repayment of a loan or other obligation as an employee authorized deduction [^oh-4113-15-repayment-deduction-structure]. Final wages remain due on the regular pay schedule, which by default is semimonthly unless a customary, contractual or legal interval applies [^oh-4113-15-semimonthly-pay-structure][^oh-4113-15-other-intervals-structure], so the conservative course is to pay them in full and collect any disputed balance separately.

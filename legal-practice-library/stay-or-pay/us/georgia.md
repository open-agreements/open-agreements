# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Georgia[^about]

Georgia has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment, so a repayment promise is enforced as a contract, a sum fixed as damages for leaving early must pass the three-part liquidated-damages test, and federal wage law limits taking the money out of pay.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Georgia? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** Georgia has no statute that limits stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms in private employment. In *City of Pembroke v. Hagin*, the Georgia Court of Appeals rejected a public-policy challenge to a police officer's signed promise to repay $1,800 of training costs upon voluntary departure within twelve months [^ga-sop-pem-terms-enf][^ga-sop-pem-policy-enf]. Where a repayment term fixes a sum owed as damages for leaving early, rather than returning an amount actually advanced, it is tested as liquidated damages: Georgia law lets parties agree in their contract to a sum that liquidates their damages [^ga-sop-slf-agree-enf], and the Georgia Supreme Court's three-part test asks whether the injury is difficult to estimate, whether the parties intended damages rather than a penalty, and whether the sum is a reasonable pre-estimate of the probable loss [^ga-sop-slf-test-enf].

"We conclude that the agreement at issue in this case is reasonably related to the City’s interest in protecting its investment in training a new officer."[^ga-sop-pem-related-enf]

The court in *Pembroke* also noted that the officer could have avoided the repayment simply by staying employed for twelve months [^ga-sop-pem-avoid-enf]. A stipulated-damages provision must set a reasonable pre-estimate of the probable loss [^ga-sop-slf-test-enf]. In *Physician Specialists in Anesthesia, P.C. v. MacNeill*, a shareholders' agreement let a medical practice keep a departing physician's deferred compensation as liquidated damages if the physician breached the restrictive covenant [^ga-sop-mac-retain-enf], and the Court of Appeals held the clause a penalty because no effort was made to pre-estimate the loss [^ga-sop-mac-penalty-enf]. The touchstone is whether the parties used a reasonable method to arrive at a sum that reasonably approximates the probable loss [^ga-sop-cain-touch-enf].

Georgia's Restrictive Covenants Act, enacted by 2011 H.B. 30, defines a restrictive covenant as an agreement that exists to protect a party's interest in property, confidential information, customer good will, business relationships, employees, or other economic advantages [^ga-sop-hb30-def-enf]. Its enforcement rules address contracts that restrict competition [^ga-sop-hb30-53-enf]. No Georgia appellate decision located for this guide applies the Act to a stand-alone obligation to repay training costs or a bonus. *Pembroke*, decided before the Act, held that the training-repayment agreement was not void for public policy reasons [^ga-sop-pem-policy-enf].

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment sum chosen to discourage departure, rather than worked out from the employer's expected loss, risks being struck as a penalty [^ga-sop-cain-deter-enf][^ga-sop-mac-penalty-enf].

## Can a Georgia employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Georgia appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a sign-on, retention or other bonus already paid in cash can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. Two decisions address forfeiture of compensation on departure. In *Milhollin v. Salomon Smith Barney, Inc.*, an employee agreed to forfeit pay he had put into restricted stock if he left before it vested [^ga-sop-mil-elect-claw], and the Court of Appeals enforced the forfeiture because the plan provided for it unmistakably and was free from legal infirmity [^ga-sop-mil-forfeit-claw]. In *Physician Specialists in Anesthesia, P.C. v. MacNeill*, by contrast, a practice's right to keep deferred compensation as liquidated damages for breach of a restrictive covenant [^ga-sop-mac-retain-claw] was held a penalty because no effort was made to pre-estimate the loss [^ga-sop-mac-penalty-claw].

"The evidence thus shows conclusively that no effort was made to preestimate the loss. It follows that the clause constitutes a penalty."[^ga-sop-mac-penalty-claw]

*Milhollin* also held that the forfeiture did not restrict the employee from working elsewhere and was instead a condition precedent to receiving discounted stock [^ga-sop-mil-restraint-claw]. *Milhollin* treated the discounted-stock condition as a condition precedent, while stipulated damages for breach must satisfy Georgia's three-part liquidated-damages test [^ga-sop-mil-restraint-claw][^ga-sop-slf-test-claw]. The Court of Appeals has said that deterrence should not factor into setting liquidated damages [^ga-sop-cain-deter-claw], so a repayment amount chosen to discourage departure, rather than to approximate the employer's loss, is exposed. Because the question is unsettled, the conservative course is to make the bonus conditional on continued service before it is earned, and to tie any repayment to the unserved part of the commitment.

## Can repayment come out of a Georgia final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** No Georgia statute located for this guide expressly lists the deductions a private employer may take from wages. Georgia's pay-frequency statute covers employers of wageworkers in manual, mechanical or clerical labor, excluding farming, sawmill and turpentine industries and salaried officials and department heads [^ga-sop-ga-34-7-2-cover-final]; for covered employees, each payment must correspond to the full net amount of wages or earnings due for the period [^ga-sop-ga-34-7-2-final], and the Court of Appeals has left open whether an employee may sue under that section [^ga-sop-mil-notreached-final]. Federal law sets a floor: wages are not paid unless paid free and clear [^ga-sop-cfr-free-final], and the Fair Labor Standards Act's wage requirements are not met where the employee kicks back any part of the wage to the employer [^ga-sop-cfr-kickback-final]. The Department of Labor treats a genuine loan or wage advance differently: its principal may be deducted even if the deduction cuts into the minimum wage or overtime pay [^ga-sop-dol-loan-final].

"The wage requirements of the Act will not be met where the employee ‘kicks-back’ directly or indirectly to the employer or to another person for the employer's benefit the whole or part of the wage delivered to the employee."[^ga-sop-cfr-kickback-final]

A deduction that recovers the employer's own costs violates federal law in any workweek in which it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages required [^ga-sop-cfr-tools-final]. Tuition the employer advanced on condition of continued employment may be recouped without violating the FLSA, provided the employee clearly understood the terms before accepting the payment [^ga-sop-dol-tuition-final]. Whether a particular training or bonus repayment is an advance or an employer cost depends on how it was structured. Because no Georgia appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a repayment deduction reduces the full net amount due under Georgia's pay-frequency statute [^ga-sop-ga-34-7-2-final], the conservative course is to obtain the employee's signed written authorization for any deduction and, unless the amount is the principal of a documented loan or advance, to keep each deduction from cutting into the federal minimum or overtime wages.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Netting a training or bonus repayment against the last paycheck exposes the employer to liability for the unpaid minimum wage plus an equal amount as liquidated damages if a deduction for the employer's own costs cuts into the federal minimum wage [^ga-sop-u216-final].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Georgia? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Georgia has no statute located for this guide that treats private-sector training, tuition or relocation repayment differently from other repayment terms. Training repayment is the setting in which Georgia courts have enforced repayment promises: *Pembroke* held a police officer's training-repayment agreement not void for public policy reasons [^ga-sop-pem-policy-train], and in *Southwest Georgia Area Health Education Center, Inc. v. Rowell* the trial court found a training-program contract, including its liquidated damages clause, enforceable [^ga-sop-row-enf-train], and the Court of Appeals adopted that order as its own on that point [^ga-sop-row-adopt-train].

"We conclude that the agreement at issue in this case is reasonably related to the City’s interest in protecting its investment in training a new officer."[^ga-sop-pem-related-train]

In *Rowell*, the student agreed to accept employment only within the sponsor's 38-county service area after completing the physician assistant program and to serve two years there [^ga-sop-row-service-train], and the sponsor sued for the $36,000 in liquidated damages the contract provided [^ga-sop-row-sued-train]. Where a training-repayment amount is stipulated damages for leaving early rather than return of money actually advanced, it still has to satisfy the three-part liquidated-damages test, including that the sum be a reasonable pre-estimate of the probable loss [^ga-sop-slf-test-train].

## What happens to a Georgia employer that over-collects a repayment from pay? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** A deduction for the employer's own costs violates federal law in any workweek in which it cuts into the minimum or overtime wages [^ga-sop-cfr-tools-enf2], and the employer is liable for the unpaid amounts plus an equal amount as liquidated damages [^ga-sop-u216-enf2]. A court may reduce or deny the liquidated damages if the employer shows good faith and reasonable grounds [^ga-sop-u260-enf2]. Recovering the principal of a genuine loan or wage advance is treated differently [^ga-sop-dol-loan-enf2]. In *Milhollin v. Salomon Smith Barney, Inc.*, the Georgia Court of Appeals declined to decide whether the employee could bring a claim under Georgia's pay-frequency statute because his claim was untimely [^ga-sop-mil-notreached-enf2].

"Although he correctly points out that OCGA § 9-3-24 allows six years for bringing an action under a written contract, his allegations in Count 1 were premised solely upon the provisions of OCGA § 34-7-2, the statutory wage law, and so were untimely."[^ga-sop-mil-periods-enf2]

The limitations period depends on the theory. *Milhollin* recognized that a claim on a written contract has six years, but held a claim resting only on the wage statute untimely [^ga-sop-mil-periods-enf2], applying the two-year period for actions to recover wages under wage-payment laws [^ga-sop-mil-two-enf2]. An employer that sues to collect a repayment must also be ready to defend the amount: the employee can defeat a liquidated damages provision by showing that any one of the three parts of the test is lacking [^ga-sop-slf-test-enf2].

## What repayment structure is safest under Georgia law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A Georgia repayment term is on firmest ground when it is reasonably related to protecting the employer's investment, as the training-repayment promise in *Pembroke* was [^ga-sop-pem-related-safe]. As a drafting matter, the agreement should be signed before the employer pays, so that the employee understands the terms before accepting the payment [^ga-sop-dol-tuition-safe], and the amount should be tied to the employer's documented cost and decline as the employee serves the commitment, because a sum must be a reasonable pre-estimate of the probable loss [^ga-sop-slf-test-safe], the parties must use a reasonable method to arrive at it [^ga-sop-cain-touch-safe], and a forfeiture set without any effort to pre-estimate the loss was struck as a penalty in *MacNeill* [^ga-sop-mac-penalty-safe].
Collecting the balance by demand or suit on the written agreement avoids netting it against pay, which matters because, for employees covered by the pay-frequency statute [^ga-sop-ga-34-7-2-cover-safe], each payment must correspond to the full net amount of wages due for the period [^ga-sop-ga-34-7-2-safe]. Separate collection does not by itself avoid the federal rule: wages kicked back to the employer directly or indirectly are treated as unpaid [^ga-sop-cfr-kickback-safe], so recovering the employer's own costs must still leave the minimum and overtime wages intact, while the principal of a genuine loan or advance may be recovered [^ga-sop-dol-loan-safe].

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Illinois[^about]

Illinois repayment terms raise separate questions about competition restrictions, earned bonuses, payroll consent, cash advances, training costs, and wage claims.

## Are repayment terms enforceable? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** For Illinois Freedom to Work Act agreements entered after its January 1, 2022 amendment took effect, repayment triggered by post-employment competition falls within noncompete restrictions for covered employers, excluding governmental and quasi-governmental bodies. [^il-competition-definition] [^il-amendment-date] [^effective-date-clause] [^il-competition-employer] [^il-competition-validity-source] Payroll collection separately requires a wage-deduction exception. [^il-repayment-deduction]
The definition addresses adverse financial consequences triggered by competitive activities after termination. It does not classify every departure-triggered debt as a noncompete. [^il-competition-definition]
The Act’s employer definition excludes governmental and quasi-governmental bodies. [^il-competition-employer]
In 2026, covered noncompetes require actual or expected annualized earnings above $75,000; nonsolicits require earnings above $45,000. These thresholds rise to $80,000 and $47,500 on January 1, 2027. [^il-competition-thresholds-source]
Additional restrictions concern pandemic-like layoffs, public-sector collective bargaining, construction workers (with specified management, engineering or architectural, design, sales, and ownership exceptions), and certain post-January 1, 2025 covenants affecting veterans’ and first responders’ mental-health services. Their statutory conditions still matter even above the earnings thresholds. [^il-competition-exceptions-source]
A covered covenant needs adequate consideration, a valid employment relationship, protection no greater than a legitimate business interest requires, no undue employee hardship, and no public injury. [^il-competition-validity-source]
Adequate consideration can be two years of post-signing employment or adequate professional or financial benefits. [^il-competition-consideration-source]
The employer must advise the employee in writing to consult an attorney and provide a 14-calendar-day review opportunity. The employee may voluntarily sign earlier. [^il-competition-notice-source]

## Can the employer claw back pay already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** An Illinois employer can take back compensation already paid from wages only through a wage-deduction exception. [^il-clawback-definition] [^clawback-deduction-rule] Otherwise the employer must pursue a creditor claim, which remains subject to general contract defenses such as unconscionability. [^il-bland-debt] [^il-razor-unconscionability] Outside the Freedom to Work Act, no Illinois statute in the reviewed authorities decides whether such a repayment promise is valid.
In *Razor v. Hyundai Motor America*, the Illinois Supreme Court described procedural unconscionability as a term so difficult to find, read, or understand that the party cannot fairly be said to have been aware of agreeing to it, taking into account a lack of bargaining power. [^il-razor-unconscionability]
An action on a written contract or other written evidence of indebtedness must be commenced within ten years after the cause of action accrued, which covers an employer's suit on a written repayment agreement. [^il-creditor-written-contract-deadline]
A payroll clawback requires a statutory deduction exception, even when an agreement provides for repayment. [^clawback-deduction-rule]
An earned bonus depends on an unequivocal promise and satisfaction of the agreed conditions. [^il-earned-bonus]
The rules distinguish objective sign-on and relocation incentives from discretionary bonuses. [^il-objective-incentives]
The rules protect a proportionate service bonus after mutual-consent termination or employer termination through no fault of the employee, despite an employment-on-a-particular-date condition. [^il-earned-bonus]
In *Bland v. Edward D. Jones & Co.*, the federal district court rejected an FLSA kickback theory concerning the particular training-debt provision while leaving possible state-law invalidity arguments as contract defenses. [^il-bland-debt]
That federal ruling does not establish the validity of every Illinois repayment clause, and no precedential Illinois decision in the reviewed authorities decides the validity of departure-triggered repayment of compensation already paid. [^il-bland-debt]
*First Peek Ultrasound L.L.C. v. Weideman* is a 2018 Rule 23 order. It falls outside Rule 23’s permission for persuasive citation of qualifying orders entered on or after January 1, 2021; it is not a generally citable persuasive Illinois authority for a wage-clawback rule. [^first-peek-notice] [^il-supreme-court-rule23]

## Can repayment come out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** Repayment may come out of Illinois final compensation only through a permitted deduction route. [^final-pay-permitted-deductions] An ordinary deduction needs written consent freely given at the time of the deduction, so an earlier repayment promise is not by itself an authorization to deduct; the cash-advance and fixed recurring-deduction rules are the exceptions. [^il-deduction-consent]
Final compensation includes wages, salaries, earned commissions and bonuses, earned vacation and holidays, and other compensation owed under the employment agreement. [^final-compensation-definition]
Final compensation must be paid in full at separation if possible, and no later than the next regularly scheduled payday. [^final-payment-deadline]
An ordinary written authorization must be freely given when the deduction occurs. For recurring deductions, an agreement specifying a defined duration and the same deduction amount each pay period is treated as contemporaneously given; that duration cannot exceed six months. Cash-advance consent may instead be given when the advance is made. [^il-deduction-consent]
A cash advance repaid through payroll requires an agreement signed by both parties specifying the advance amount, repayment schedule, and method. [^cash-advance-agreement]
The cash-advance rules exclude wired travel funds when a signed authorization is impracticable. [^travel-wire-exception]
A cash-advance repayment schedule cannot exceed 15% of gross wages or final compensation per paycheck. [^cash-advance-cap]
At termination, a balance above 15% may be withheld only if the agreement signed when the advance was made included that arrangement. [^cash-advance-final]
The 15% rule governs cash-advance payroll collection; it does not supply a general cap for every repayment debt. [^cash-advance-cap]
The deduction rules do not authorize violations of the Illinois Minimum Wage Law or FLSA. [^il-deduction-floors]
The employer bears the burden of proving a section 9 exception. [^il-deduction-proof]
A wage dispute does not excuse timely unconditional payment of amounts conceded due. [^il-conceded-wages]

## How does Illinois treat payroll deductions to repay training or tuition costs? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Illinois distinguishes payroll deductions for voluntary elective tuition advances from deductions for employer-required training: the former follow written cash-advance agreement rules, while the latter require free express written consent at deduction. [^il-tuition-rule] [^required-training-rule]
The elective-course rule covers advances for tuition, textbooks, and other expenses paid to or for the employee. [^il-tuition-rule]
The employer-required-training deduction rule applies even when government also requires the course. [^required-training-rule]
These provisions regulate deduction authority; they do not by themselves establish enforceability of a separate creditor claim. [^il-tuition-rule] [^required-training-rule]
A separate debt claim does not authorize taking FLSA-protected pay. In *Heder v. City of Two Rivers*, the employee retained minimum wages for the withheld final pay periods, leaving the city to collect any residue as an ordinary creditor. [^il-heder-minimum]
*Heder v. City of Two Rivers* supplies a federal overtime limit: repayment cannot leave the employee below FLSA-required overtime compensation. [^il-heder-overtime]
A later federal decision describes *Heder*’s state-law training-repayment analysis as applying Wisconsin law, so that analysis does not establish an Illinois contract rule. [^heder-wisconsin-context]

## How are Illinois wage-payment and noncompete repayment rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee aggrieved by an Illinois Wage Payment and Collection Act violation may file an Illinois Department of Labor complaint or sue in circuit court without exhausting administrative remedies. [^il-administrative-deadline] [^il-civil-action]
A Department complaint requires a signed completed wage-claim application and supporting documents within one year after the wages, final compensation, or wage supplements were due. [^il-administrative-deadline]
The separate civil limitations period for an IWPCA action is ten years after accrual under section 13-206. [^il-civil-deadline]
In *Prorok v. Hiben*, the Illinois Supreme Court applied that period to an IWPCA back-pay claim against a public employer rather than the Tort Immunity Act’s one-year period. [^il-prorok-opinion]
An employee not timely paid may recover the underpayment and damages of 5% of that amount for each month it remains unpaid through a Department claim or a civil action, but not both. [^il-underpayment-remedy]
For a Department claim adjudicated through an administrative hearing, monthly damages accrue until the final order becomes a debt due and owed to the State. [^il-administrative-accrual-source]
A final Department order requiring payment becomes State debt when the employer or other party neither pays within 35 days nor timely files an action for administrative review. [^administrative-state-debt]
In a civil action, the employee also recovers costs and all reasonable attorney’s fees. [^il-civil-fees-source]
The IWPCA permits an action by one or more employees on behalf of themselves and similarly situated employees. [^il-civil-action]
In an employer-filed civil action or arbitration to enforce a covered noncompete or nonsolicit, including a complaint or counterclaim, a prevailing employee recovers costs and reasonable attorney fees regarding that claim; the court or arbitrator may award appropriate relief. [^ftwa-defense-fees]
The Illinois Attorney General may initiate or intervene in a civil action on reasonable cause to believe a person or entity is engaged in a prohibited pattern and practice. [^ftwa-ag-trigger]
In an action under the Freedom to Work Act, the Attorney General may obtain monetary damages to the State, restitution, and equitable relief, including injunctions. [^ftwa-ag-remedies]
The Attorney General may request, and the court may impose, a civil penalty up to $5,000 for each violation or $10,000 for each repeat violation within five years. Each violation for each person subjected to an unlawful agreement is separate. [^ftwa-ag-remedies]
An employer ordered to pay that neither seeks timely review nor complies within 15 days after a Department demand or 35 days after an order also owes a 20% penalty to the Department and a penalty to the employee of 1% per calendar day of delay. [^iwpca-order-penalties]
Discharging or discriminating against an employee for a wage complaint, proceeding, or testimony is a Class C misdemeanor, and the employee may recover all appropriate legal and equitable relief through a Department claim or civil action, but not both, with costs and reasonable attorney's fees in a civil action. [^il-iwpca-retaliation-source]
Corporate officers or agents who knowingly permit the employer to violate the IWPCA are deemed employers of its employees. [^iwpca-individual-liability]

An employee who has been unlawfully retaliated against shall be entitled to recover through a claim filed with the Department of Labor or in a civil action, but not both, all legal and equitable relief as may be appropriate. In a civil action, such employee shall also recover costs and all reasonable attorney's fees." *820 ILCS 115/14(c)* <https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/082001150K14.htm>

[^iwpca-individual-liability]: **820 ILCS 115/13** — "In addition to an individual who is deemed to be an employer pursuant to Section 2 of this Act, any officers of a corporation or agents of an employer who knowingly permit such employer to violate the provisions of this Act shall be deemed to be the employers of the employees of the corporation." *820 ILCS 115/13* <https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/082001150K13.htm>

# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment in Pennsylvania[^about]

Pennsylvania has no general statute on stay-or-pay or training-repayment terms, so a repayment promise is tested as a contract under the penalty rule, while the Wage Payment and Collection Law allows deductions from wages only as the law or a Department of Labor and Industry regulation authorizes them. A 2024 statute on health care practitioners' non-competes preserves recovery of certain relocation and training expenses.

## Are repayment terms enforceable in Pennsylvania? {#repayment-enforceability}

**Short answer.** A Pennsylvania employee repayment term that fixes an amount owed on early departure is tested under the penalty rule, which refuses to enforce a liquidated damages clause intended as a penalty rather than as a measure of foreseeable business losses [^pa-sop-holts-penalty-enf]. A county trial court has applied that rule to refuse a training-related early-departure charge [^pa-sop-mannino-excessive-enf]. No Pennsylvania statute of general application governs stay-or-pay, training-repayment or bonus-repayment terms in private employment, and parties may agree in advance on liquidated damages, particularly where actual damages would be hard to estimate or prove [^pa-sop-pantuso-difficult-enf]. The one statute located for this guide that addresses repayment is limited to health care practitioners: the Fair Contracting for Health Care Practitioners Act preserves an employer's recovery of reasonable relocation, training and patient-base expenses that meet its conditions [^pa-sop-act74-expense-enf].

"A penalty, by contrast, is fixed, ‘not as a pre-estimate of probable actual damages, but as a punishment, the threat of which is designed to prevent the breach.’"[^pa-sop-pantuso-penalty-enf]

In *Pantuso Motors, Inc. v. Corestates Bank, N.A.*, the court distinguished a penalty, fixed as a punishment, from a pre-estimate of probable actual damages [^pa-sop-pantuso-penalty-enf], and stated that contracting parties may provide for pre-determined liquidated damages, particularly where actual damages would be difficult to estimate in advance or to prove after a breach [^pa-sop-pantuso-difficult-enf]. In *Holt's Cigar Co. v. 222 Liberty Associates*, the Superior Court reaffirmed Pennsylvania's policy of refusing to enforce a liquidated damages clause intended as a penalty rather than as a measure of foreseeable business losses [^pa-sop-holts-penalty-enf]. Neither decision involved an employee repayment term, and no Pennsylvania appellate decision located for this guide applies them to a training-repayment or stay-or-pay clause. In *Mannino v. Lazerpro Inc.*, a Centre County Court of Common Pleas decision, an employment contract set liquidated damages of three months' salary for leaving before a two-year commitment as compensation for lost training and workload redistribution [^pa-sop-mannino-terms-enf][^pa-sop-mannino-excessive-enf]. The court stated that such costs are difficult to measure but that the amount must still be a reasonable forecast of the harm [^pa-sop-mannino-forecast-enf], and it held the $8,250 charge unreasonable on the evidence presented [^pa-sop-mannino-excessive-enf]. The conservative course is to set any fixed repayment amount by reference to a documented estimate of the employer's loss from an early departure, such as the cost of the training or the hiring incentive, rather than as a deterrent.

A repayment term that operates as a restraint on later work raises a second question. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court has stated that restrictive covenants are not favored in Pennsylvania and have historically been viewed as a trade restraint that prevents a former employee from earning a living [^pa-sop-hess-disfavored-enf], and it enforces one only if it is ancillary to employment, supported by adequate consideration, reasonably limited in duration and geographic extent, and designed to protect the employer's legitimate interests [^pa-sop-socko-test-enf]. No Pennsylvania appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a repayment obligation that applies whether or not the employee competes is a restrictive covenant, so the conservative course for a repayment triggered only by competitive work is to draft it to meet the *Socko* requirements as well.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Drafting note.**
>
> A repayment schedule that fixes an amount unrelated to any estimate of the employer's loss from an early departure risks being struck as a penalty [^pa-sop-pantuso-penalty-enf][^pa-sop-holts-penalty-enf].

## Can a Pennsylvania employer claw back pay the employee already earned? {#clawback-earned-comp}

**Short answer.** No Pennsylvania appellate decision located for this guide decides whether a bonus or other payment that has already vested can be recovered under a stay-or-pay term. The Wage Payment and Collection Law gives an employee no substantive right to compensation beyond the right to enforce payment of what the agreement provides [^pa-sop-hartman-contract-claw], and a federal district court applying Pennsylvania law has required a departing executive to repay a portion of incentive awards under the award terms [^pa-sop-willis-repay-claw].

The Wage Payment and Collection Law defines wages to include all earnings of an employee, however calculated [^pa-sop-wpcl-wages-claw]. In *Willis Re Inc. v. Hearn*, the court stated that a bonus is a protected wage under the statute if it is earned, meaning the right to it vested under the terms of employment [^pa-sop-willis-earned-claw], and it found that the executive, who owed repayment unless he retired, had not retired under the award agreements [^pa-sop-willis-repay-claw]. The statute's protections themselves cannot be contravened or set aside by a private agreement [^pa-sop-wpcl-nonwaiver-claw], but no decision located for this guide decides whether that bars a separate obligation to repay compensation that had already vested. The conservative course is to state in the award or bonus agreement, before payment, that the payment is an advance that vests only over the commitment period, rather than to try to recover pay the agreement had already made unconditional.

Recovering the money from wages is a separate question. Wages may be paid net of a deduction only where the deduction is provided by law or authorized by Department of Labor and Industry regulation [^pa-sop-wpcl-deductions-claw], so an enforceable repayment debt does not by itself permit an employer to withhold it from pay.

## Can a Pennsylvania employer take a repayment out of the final paycheck? {#final-pay-deduction}

**Short answer.** A Pennsylvania employer may deduct a repayment from final wages only when the deduction is provided by law or authorized by Department of Labor and Industry regulation [^pa-sop-wpcl-deductions-final]. The regulation's list includes repayment of a bona fide loan from the employer when the employee authorizes the deduction in writing when the loan is made or afterward [^pa-sop-9-1-loans-final]. A deduction that does not fit a listed category needs the employee's written authorization and the Department's approval [^pa-sop-9-1-other-final].

"(10) Deductions for repayment to the employer of bona fide loans provided the employe authorizes such deductions in writing either at the time the loan is given him or subsequent to such loan."[^pa-sop-9-1-loans-final]

The written authorization alone is not enough for an unlisted deduction. In *Ressler v. Jones Motor Co.*, the Superior Court held that a wage deduction of the kind before it, which the regulation did not list, is unlawful absent a showing of both Department approval and the employees' written authorizations [^pa-sop-ressler-holding-final], and it found that without Department approval the employer had wrongfully withheld wages even though employees had signed authorizations [^pa-sop-ressler-approval-final]. The regulation also lists deductions for an employee's purchases from the employer of goods, services, facilities and similar items, if authorized in writing or by a collective bargaining agreement [^pa-sop-9-1-purchases-final]. No Pennsylvania decision located for this guide decides whether a training, relocation or sign-on bonus repayment obligation is a bona fide loan or a purchase of services for these purposes, so the conservative course is to document the advance as a loan with a written deduction authorization, or to collect the balance by demand rather than from wages.

The minimum wage still limits a deduction. The deduction rules do not authorize a deduction below the Minimum Wage Act minimum for an expense or charge the employer requires or authorizes in connection with the employee's assigned duties [^pa-sop-9-2-floor-final], and the state minimum is $7.25 an hour for all hours worked, subject to the Act's exclusions and exemptions [^pa-sop-min-rate-final]. An agreement to work for less than the minimum wage is no defense to the employee's action for the shortfall [^pa-sop-mwa-no-agreement-final]. Where federal law applies, wages count as paid only when they are paid finally and unconditionally, or free and clear [^pa-sop-free-clear-final].

Timing runs while the deduction question is open: a separated employee's earned wages are due no later than the next regular payday on which they would otherwise be due [^pa-sop-final-pay-final]. The statute separately provides that an employer's good-faith assertion of a set-off is not a violation if the employer has paid all wages due in excess of the amount asserted [^pa-sop-wpcl-setoff-final]. No Pennsylvania appellate decision located for this guide reconciles that set-off provision with the deduction rules for a training or bonus repayment, so the conservative course is to pay the final wages in full and pursue the repayment separately. If withheld wages remain unpaid for 30 days beyond the regular payday and no good-faith dispute or set-off accounts for the nonpayment, the employee may also claim liquidated damages of 25 percent of the wages due or $500, whichever is greater [^pa-sop-wpcl-ld-final].

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A final-paycheck deduction for a training or bonus repayment that is not a listed deduction and has no Department approval risks being treated as an unlawful deduction even when the employee signed an authorization, leaving the withheld amount owed as unpaid wages [^pa-sop-ressler-holding-final].

## Are training or tuition repayments treated differently in Pennsylvania? {#training-repayment}

**Short answer.** Pennsylvania training, tuition and relocation repayment terms generally face the same penalty rule [^pa-sop-pantuso-penalty-train] and wage-deduction rules [^pa-sop-wpcl-deductions-train] as bonus repayment terms, because no statute of general application gives them a separate rule. Covered health care practitioners are the exception, under a statute effective January 1, 2025 [^pa-sop-act74-effective-train] that covers medical doctors, doctors of osteopathy, certified registered nurse anesthetists, certified registered nurse practitioners and physician assistants [^pa-sop-act74-practitioner-train].
The Fair Contracting for Health Care Practitioners Act preserves enforcement of a provision that lets an employer recover reasonable expenses that are directly attributable to the practitioner and accrued within three years before separation, unless separation is caused by dismissal; that relate to relocation, training and establishment of a patient base; and that are amortized over up to five years from separation [^pa-sop-act74-expense-train].

"(c) Construction.--Nothing in this section shall be construed to: (1) Prohibit the enforcement of a contract provision that allows an employer to recover reasonable expenses from a health care practitioner, if the expenses are: (i) Directly attributable to the health care practitioner and accrued within the three years prior to separation, unless separation is caused by dismissal of the health care practitioner. (ii) Related to relocation, training and establishment of a patient base. (iii) Amortized over a period of up to five years from the date of separation by the health care practitioner."[^pa-sop-act74-expense-train]

The Act's main rule is a limit on non-competes. A health care practitioner noncompete covenant entered into after the effective date is void and unenforceable [^pa-sop-act74-void-train], except that an employer may enforce one of no more than one year if the practitioner was not dismissed [^pa-sop-act74-exception-train]. The Act defines a noncompete covenant by its effect: an agreement that impedes the practitioner's ability to continue treating patients or accepting new patients after the term of employment [^pa-sop-act74-covenant-def-train]. Subsection (c)(1) is a rule of construction for that section, so an expense-recovery provision that meets its conditions is not prohibited by the non-compete rule; it still has to be enforceable as a contract. The three-year accrual condition in subsection (c)(1)(i) carries an exception where separation is caused by dismissal of the practitioner [^pa-sop-act74-expense-train], and the Act does not say how that exception changes the recovery; no Pennsylvania decision located for this guide decides whether an expense-recovery provision outside those conditions is itself a noncompete covenant. The conservative course for a covered practitioner is to limit any repayment to expenses that meet all three conditions and not to seek recovery from a practitioner the employer dismissed.

A pending bill would add a general rule. H.B. 421, introduced in the 2025-2026 session, would bar an employer from requiring a training repayment agreement as a condition of employment and would make such an agreement void [^pa-sop-hb421-void-train]. As of November 19, 2026, the bill's last recorded action is referral to the House Labor and Industry Committee on January 30, 2025 [^pa-sop-hb421-status-train], so it is not current law.

## How are Pennsylvania repayment and wage-deduction rules enforced? {#enforcement}

**Short answer.** An employee whose wages were withheld for a repayment can sue under the Wage Payment and Collection Law, which lets any employee or other party to whom wages are payable bring an action [^pa-sop-wpcl-action-enforce]. The court must award reasonable attorneys' fees against the defendant in addition to any judgment for the plaintiff [^pa-sop-wpcl-fees-enforce]. The employer's own repayment claim is an ordinary contract claim, enforceable only to the extent the term is not a penalty [^pa-sop-pantuso-penalty-enforce].

When wages remain unpaid for 30 days beyond the regular payday, the employee may also claim liquidated damages of 25 percent of the wages due or $500, whichever is greater, unless a good-faith contest, dispute or assertion of a set-off or counterclaim accounts for the nonpayment [^pa-sop-wpcl-ld-enforce]. Liquidated damages are tied to a claim for unpaid wages: in *Aita v. NCB Management Services, Inc.*, where the employer had paid late retention bonuses before the employee sued, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that the employee had no claim for unpaid wages and so could not bring a standalone claim for liquidated damages [^pa-sop-aita-standalone-enforce]. A Wage Payment and Collection Law claim must be brought within three years after the wages were due [^pa-sop-wpcl-limit-enforce]. A deduction that takes pay below the minimum wage also supports a Minimum Wage Act action for the shortfall with costs and attorney's fees, and an agreement to work for less is no defense [^pa-sop-mwa-no-agreement-enforce].

## What repayment structure is safest under Pennsylvania law? {#safest-structure}

**Short answer.** A Pennsylvania employee repayment provision presents less penalty risk when its amount estimates the employer's foreseeable loss from an early departure rather than deterring departure [^pa-sop-holts-penalty-safe]. It documents the advance as a bona fide loan with the employee's written authorization to deduct repayment given when the loan is made or afterward [^pa-sop-9-1-loans-safe]. Any deduction should leave pay at or above the minimum wage [^pa-sop-9-2-floor-safe], and a repayment that is not a listed deduction should be collected by demand rather than from wages [^pa-sop-ressler-holding-safe].

For a covered health care practitioner, the expense-recovery provision should be limited to reasonable expenses that are directly attributable to the practitioner, accrued within three years before separation, related to relocation, training and establishment of a patient base, and amortized over up to five years from separation [^pa-sop-act74-expense-safe]. A repayment that applies only if the employee goes to work for a competitor should also meet the requirements for a restrictive covenant: ancillary to employment, supported by adequate consideration, reasonably limited in duration and geography, and designed to protect a legitimate interest [^pa-sop-socko-test-safe].

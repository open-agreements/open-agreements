# Stay-or-Pay and Employee Repayment: When a Worker Can Be Made to Pay an Employer Back Across the United States[^about]

The cross-state framework for stay-or-pay and employee-repayment terms — whether a repayment obligation is enforceable, whether an employer can claw back pay already earned, when repayment can come out of the final paycheck, and how training-repayment agreements are treated — with links to the per-state practice notes and the 50-state survey.

## What is the earned-versus-forfeitable line, and why does it decide everything? {#earned-vs-forfeitable}

**Short answer.** In New York, the decisive wage-law question is whether the money at stake has already become the worker's earned compensation or is still a contingent benefit the worker has not yet earned [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli]. Earned compensation is hard for an employer to recover; an unearned, forfeitable benefit can be conditioned on continued service. New York states the distinction crisply, separating pay that is vested and mandatory from pay that is discretionary and forfeitable [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli].

A stay-or-pay term is a promise that a worker will pay an employer back if the worker leaves before a set date; commentary on New York's 2025 statute describes stay-or-pay provisions as terms that require employees to pay their employer if employment ends before a stated period of time [^paul-hastings-stay-or-pay-provision-description].

The same pivot appears under different labels everywhere. A retention award the worker has not yet earned can be forfeited on early departure; pay the worker has already earned is much harder to take back. New York's Fourth Department frames it as the difference between compensation that is vested and the kind that remains contingent, in *Matter of William Mattar, P.C. v. Riley* [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli].

"The dispositive factor in determining whether compensation constitutes wages is not the labeling used in a compensation plan but whether the compensation is vested and mandatory as opposed to discretionary and forfeitable"[^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli]

Whether a state voids repayment outright, blocks recovery of earned wages, or enforces repayment as an ordinary contract, the analysis still turns on this line. In New York, a genuinely discretionary award can be forfeited when the worker leaves before a continued-employment condition is met [^truelove-discretionary-bonus-not-wages-pillar][^truelove-continued-employment-bonus-pillar], while an employer that withholds earned bonus wages from a departed worker violates the wage-deduction bar [^mattar-withheld-bonus-deduction-pillar].

## How widely do states differ — from outright bans to freedom of contract? {#three-regimes}

**Short answer.** States differ widely, from a statutory ban in California to Texas, where a stay-or-pay amount is tested under the rule that a contractual damages amount must be a reasonable forecast of just compensation [^california-bpc-16608-b1-ab1697][^fpl-energy-v-txu-2014]. At one pole, California has enacted a ban that, for contracts entered into on or after January 1, 2027 and subject to listed exceptions, will bar terms requiring a worker to pay a debt when employment ends and will make a violating term void [^california-bpc-16608-b1-ab1697][^california-labor-code-sec-926]. At the other, Texas enforces repayment as an ordinary contract, bounded mainly by the rule that a damages amount must be a reasonable forecast of just compensation rather than a penalty [^fpl-energy-v-txu-2014]. New York sits between: until Labor Law Article 37 is scheduled to take effect on December 19, 2026, it has no stay-or-pay statute in force [^ny-labor-law-1052-promissory-note-bar][^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-effective-date], but its wage law bars an employer from withholding pay already earned [^ny-labor-law-193-deduction-bar-regimes][^mattar-withheld-bonus-deduction-regimes].

The three pilot states map the full spectrum. California is the prohibition pole: as amended by AB 1697, Labor Code Section 926 will make a repayment term that violates Business and Professions Code Section 16608 void as a matter of public policy, but only in a contract entered into on or after January 1, 2027.

"(a) A contract or contract term that violates Section 16608 of the Business and Professions Code is void as contrary to public policy only if entered into on or after January 1, 2027."[^california-labor-code-sec-926]

Texas is the freedom-of-contract pole: a repayment term is enforced like any other contract, and the main limit is the penalty doctrine, which asks whether the amount is a genuine estimate of harm.

"In Phillips v. Phillips, we acknowledged this principle and restated the two indispensable findings a court must make to enforce contractual damages provisions: (1) ‘the harm caused by the breach is incapable or difficult of estimation,’ and (2) ‘the amount of liquidated damages called for is a reasonable forecast of just compensation.’"[^fpl-energy-v-txu-2014]

New York occupies the middle: no statute in force today targets stay-or-pay, but an employer cannot withhold earned bonus wages from a departed worker under a continued-employment condition [^mattar-withheld-bonus-deduction-regimes]. From December 19, 2026, when New York Labor Law Article 37 is scheduled to take effect, an employment promissory note required as a condition of employment will be void unless it fits one of the exceptions listed in Labor Law Section 1052(2) [^ny-labor-law-1052-promissory-note-bar][^ny-labor-law-1052-listed-exceptions][^paul-hastings-trapped-at-work-effective-date]. Read the per-state notes for the controlling rule in each jurisdiction — [California](/practice-guides/stay-or-pay/us/california), [New York](/practice-guides/stay-or-pay/us/new-york), and [Texas](/practice-guides/stay-or-pay/us/texas) — and the [50-state survey](/surveys/stay-or-pay/us) for the rest.

## What recurring limits must a stay-or-pay term survive? {#drafting-spine}

**Short answer.** In Texas, a contractual damages amount is enforceable only if the harm from the breach is difficult to estimate and the amount is a reasonable forecast of just compensation [^fpl-energy-v-txu-2014-drafting]. A Texas employer may withhold wages to recover a repayment with the worker's written authorization for a lawful purpose [^tex-labor-code-61-018]. In New York, whether a bonus can be withheld turns on whether it is vested and mandatory rather than discretionary and forfeitable [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-drafting].

Whatever a state's headline rule, three drafting questions tend to decide a stay-or-pay term's fate. First, is the figure tied to a documented, prorated cost so it reads as a reasonable forecast of harm rather than a punitive penalty [^fpl-energy-v-txu-2014-drafting]. Second, is any deduction from wages authorized — many states, like Texas, bar an employer from withholding wages without a court order, a statute, or the worker's written authorization [^tex-labor-code-61-018]. Third, is the money structured as a benefit the worker earns only by staying, so an early exit is a forfeiture rather than a recovery of vested pay [^matter-of-william-mattar-p-c-v-riley-2025-ny-sli-drafting].

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> A single repayment clause used in every state is the common drafting mistake. The same term can be void in California for a contract entered into on or after January 1, 2027 [^california-labor-code-sec-926-drafting], can fail the Texas penalty test if its amount is not a reasonable forecast of just compensation [^fpl-energy-v-txu-2014-drafting], and cannot justify withholding a New York bonus the worker has already earned [^mattar-withheld-bonus-deduction-drafting]. The governing state's note and the [50-state survey](/surveys/stay-or-pay/us) set the controlling rule.

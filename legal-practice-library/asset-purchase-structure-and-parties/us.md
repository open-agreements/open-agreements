# Small-Business Asset Purchase Agreements: Structure and Parties[^about]

Whether to buy a small business through an asset purchase or a stock purchase, when the buyer can become responsible for the seller's debts, whether the owners must approve the sale, and when the owner should sign as well as the company.

## Should the business be bought through an asset purchase or a stock purchase? {#asset-or-stock-purchase}

**Short answer.** An asset purchase gives the buyer more protection from the seller's liabilities than a stock purchase, subject to successor-liability exceptions, while a stock purchase transfers the company with all of its liabilities but generally leaves its contracts in place, so the choice turns on liability, tax and third-party consents. [^p1-kddk-stock-vs-asset-liabilities][^p1-kddk-asset-buyer-exceptions][^potomac-stock-purchase-contracts-stay]

**Liabilities.** In a stock purchase, the buyer acquires the company as an entity, and the company keeps all of its liabilities, so the buyer bears them as the new owner. [^p1-kddk-stock-vs-asset-liabilities] In an asset purchase, the buyer generally does not take on the seller's debts, but in some cases an asset buyer can be held liable for certain liabilities of the seller; the [successor liability question](#successor-liability) covers those exceptions. [^p1-kddk-asset-buyer-exceptions]

**Tax.** In an asset sale, each asset is classified and its gain or loss is figured separately, so the seller's result depends on the mix of assets; for example, the sale of inventory produces ordinary income or loss. [^p1-irs-inventory-ordinary] The buyer's basis in purchased property is generally its cost, [^p1-usc-1012-cost-basis] which the [price allocation](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-price-and-payment/us#purchase-price-allocation) spreads across the assets.

In a stock sale, the owner sells shares instead of assets and usually realizes capital gain or loss. [^p1-irs-corporation-interest-capital] The buyer's basis in the shares it buys is generally their cost; [^p1-usc-1012-cost-basis] because the company itself sells nothing, its basis in its own assets does not change unless the parties make one of the elections described next.

**Both at once.** A stock purchase can receive asset-sale tax treatment through an election under 26 U.S.C. § 338(h)(10), which treats the target corporation as if it sold all of its assets in a single transaction. [^p1-usc-338h10-deemed-asset-sale] The regulations make that election available when the buyer acquires the stock from a selling consolidated group, a selling affiliate or the shareholders of an S corporation, [^p1-reg-338h10-s-corp-sellers] but only in a qualified stock purchase, which the statute limits to stock acquired by another corporation. [^p1-usc-338d3-corporate-buyer] Section 336(e) supplies a similar election when a corporation sells all of the stock of a subsidiary that meets the statute's ownership test. [^p1-usc-336e-election] The regulations extend that election to dispositions by the shareholders of an S corporation, [^p1-reg-336-s-corp-disposition] and they state that its results should generally coincide with those of a § 338(h)(10) election. [^p1-reg-336-coincide-results]

"an election may be made to treat such sale, exchange, or distribution as a disposition of all of the assets of such other corporation, and no gain or loss shall be recognized on the sale, exchange, or distribution of such stock."[^p1-usc-336e-election]

An election is not a unilateral buyer decision: a § 338(h)(10) election is made jointly by the buyer and the sellers, and S corporation shareholders who do not sell must also consent, so a stock purchase agreement that depends on an election addresses it expressly. [^p1-reg-338h10-joint-election]

**Consents.** Transferring contracts in an asset purchase typically triggers any anti-assignment clause and may require the counterparty's consent, [^potomac-asset-purchase-anti-assignment] while a stock purchase generally does not, because the company continues to own its contracts after closing. [^potomac-stock-purchase-contracts-stay][^dla-piper-stock-purchase-simple-anti-assignment] A stock purchase can still trigger a change-of-control clause, which may require notice or consent or give the other party a termination right; the [consent question](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-closing-and-handover/us#consent-failure) covers what happens when a consent is not obtained. [^potomac-stock-purchase-can-trigger-change-of-control][^potomac-change-of-control-notice-consent-termination]

**Approvals.** A Delaware corporation may sell all or substantially all of its property and assets through its board of directors when authorized by a resolution of the holders of a majority of its outstanding voting stock, adopted at a meeting called on at least 20 days' notice. [^p1-dgcl-271a-approval] In a stock purchase, the buyer instead buys the owners' shares in the corporation or limited liability company, so each selling owner signs for its own shares, while the [signing question](#owner-signing-capacity) covers who signs for the seller in an asset purchase. [^p1-mellor-stock-purchase-shares]

Buyers often prefer an asset purchase, which the rest of this guide assumes, [^p1-kddk-buyers-prefer-asset] while a stock purchase may suit a business whose key contracts cannot be assigned without consent, because the company keeps those contracts. [^potomac-stock-purchase-contracts-stay]

## Do the owners have to approve a sale of the company's assets? {#owner-approval-of-asset-sale}

**Short answer.** A Delaware stock corporation that sells all or substantially all of its property and assets needs both its board's decision and a resolution adopted by the holders of a majority of its outstanding stock entitled to vote, under 8 Del. C. § 271(a). [^p3-dgcl-271]

Whether a sale reaches all or substantially all of the assets is not only a matter of size, because Delaware's Court of Chancery has held that a sale is beyond the board's power alone if it is of assets quantitatively vital to the operation of the corporation, is out of the ordinary, and substantially affects the corporation's existence and purpose. [^p3-gimbel-substantially-all]

Section 271 is a corporate statute, and for a Delaware limited liability company management is vested in the members in proportion to their interests in profits, with members owning more than 50 percent controlling, unless the LLC agreement provides otherwise or provides for management by a manager, so the LLC agreement is the first document that answers who approves a sale of the business. [^p3-dellc-18-402]

A buyer can make the approvals a closing condition, as in one filed agreement where the buyer's obligation to close depended on receiving certified copies of the seller's board and shareholder resolutions authorizing the agreement and the transaction; the [closing-deliverables question](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-closing-and-handover/us#closing-exchange) lists where those certificates fit. [^p3-greenfield-resolutions-condition]

## Can the buyer become responsible for the seller's debts? {#successor-liability}

**Short answer.** Sometimes: an asset buyer can become liable for the seller's unpaid sales tax under California, New York and Texas statutes, and for some federal labor and employment claims under federal case law. [^ca-rtc-6812-liability][^ny-tax-1141c-liability][^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability][^p2-teed-federal-standard][^p2-steinbach-standard] Before paying, a buyer protects itself by filing the state's tax clearance request or notice and holding back enough of the price to cover the seller's tax until clearance or a statutory release permits payment, with any amount the agency claims kept for the tax, [^p2-ca-reg1702-release][^p2-ny-tbst70-form][^p2-tx-9817-before-closing] and by checking for pending employment claims so their cost can be reflected in the price or an indemnity. [^p2-golden-state-price-indemnity]

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| New York sales tax | State statute: N.Y. Tax Law § 1141(c) | A bulk sale outside the ordinary course with no notice to the tax department; liability up to the higher of the price or the assets' fair market value [^ny-tax-1141c-liability] | Notice by registered mail at least ten days before paying or taking possession [^ny-tax-1141c-notice], then holding back the amount of any tax claim the department identifies [^p2-ny-tax-1141c-withhold] |
| Texas state taxes | State statute: Tex. Tax Code § 111.020 | The buyer of the business or its stock of goods pays without withholding enough to cover what the seller owes; liability up to the value of the price [^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability] | A comptroller certificate that no tax is due [^p2-tx-111020-certificate], or withholding until the seller produces a comptroller receipt or that certificate [^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability] |
| Texas taxes after a sham or below-value sale | State statute: Tex. Tax Code § 111.024 | The seller transferred the business to evade tax or without receiving reasonably equivalent value [^p2-tx-111024-fraudulent-transfer] | A reasonably equivalent price answers only the below-value ground, not a transfer made to evade tax [^p2-tx-111024-fraudulent-transfer]; withholding alone is no defense if the amount withheld falls short and the price was not reasonably equivalent [^p2-tx-111020-no-defense] |
| Federal employment claims (NLRA, Title VII, FLSA) | Federal case law: *Golden State Bottling* (NLRA) [^p2-golden-state-orders-run], *MacMillan Bloedel* (Title VII) [^p2-macmillan-holding], *Steinbach* [^p2-steinbach-holding] and *Teed* (FLSA) [^p2-teed-federal-standard] | Decided case by case [^p2-macmillan-holding]; the factors include notice of the claim, the seller's ability to provide relief and continuity of the business [^p2-macmillan-factors]; sale terms that disclaim the liability do not control [^p2-teed-disclaimer] | Diligence on pending charges and suits; a lower price or an indemnity clause [^p2-golden-state-price-indemnity] |

The tax statutes reach a buyer that skips the state's withholding or notice step. California and Texas cap the buyer's liability at the purchase price and New York at the higher of the price or the assets' fair market value, [^ca-rtc-6812-liability][^ny-tax-1141c-liability][^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability] and the Texas rule covers amounts owed under the state-taxation title of its Tax Code. [^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability]

Federal courts decide labor and employment successor claims case by case, weighing factors that start with whether the buyer had notice of the claim and whether the seller can provide relief, and include continuity of the business. [^p2-macmillan-holding][^p2-macmillan-factors]

Outside the tax and employment rules, the traditional common-law starting point is that an asset purchaser does not take on the seller's liabilities unless it agreed to, the deal amounts to a consolidation or merger, the buyer is a mere continuation of the seller, or the transfer was made to escape the debts fraudulently, [^ray-successor-liability-exceptions] subject to other doctrines in particular states, such as California's product-line rule for product claims. [^ray-product-line-holding]

### Tax clearance timeline: California, New York and Texas

Each of the three states gives the buyer a way to learn what the seller owes before the money goes out, and each releases the buyer if the agency does not answer in time. [^p2-ca-reg1702-release][^p2-ny-tbst70-missed][^p2-tx-111020-release]

| State | Form, certificate or notice | Agency | Who files | When, relative to closing or payment | If the agency misses its deadline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| California | A written request for a tax clearance certificate [^p2-ca-reg1702-release] | California Department of Tax and Fee Administration (the statute and regulation still refer to the board) [^p2-cdtfa-pub74-clearance] | The purchaser [^p2-ca-reg1702-release] | No fixed lead time; the agency has 60 days from the latest of its receipt of the request, the date of sale or the date the seller's records are made available for audit [^p2-ca-reg1702-release] | The purchaser is released from further obligation to withhold [^p2-ca-reg1702-release] |
| New York | Form AU-196.10, Notification of Sale, Transfer, or Assignment in Bulk [^p2-ny-tbst70-form] | Tax Department [^p2-ny-tbst70-form] | The purchaser [^p2-ny-tbst70-form] | At least 10 days before paying for or taking possession of any business assets, whichever comes first [^p2-ny-tbst70-form]; the department answers within 5 business days with a release (Form AU-197.1) or a notice of claim (Form AU-196.2) [^p2-ny-tbst70-five-days] and states any amount due within 90 days [^p2-ny-tax-1141c-ninety-days] | No notice of claim within 5 business days of a proper, timely filing: the purchaser is not liable and may pay the full price [^p2-ny-tbst70-missed]; no amount stated within 90 days: released from withholding [^p2-ny-tax-1141c-ninety-days] |
| Texas | Form 86-114, Joint Request for Certificate of No Tax Due [^p2-tx-9817-joint-form] | Comptroller of Public Accounts [^p2-tx-111020-certificate] | The seller and purchaser jointly [^p2-tx-9817-joint-form] | Before the sale closes [^p2-tx-9817-before-closing]; the comptroller has 60 days after the request or after the seller's records are made available, and no more than 90 days after the request [^p2-tx-111020-certificate], and usually answers within 10 business days when no audit is needed [^p2-tx-9817-processing] | The purchaser is released from the obligation to withhold or pay [^p2-tx-111020-release] |

The release does not end every exposure: in New York, assets bought from the seller may still be subject to the department's liens if there are outstanding warrants or judgments against the seller, [^p2-ny-tbst70-liens] and in Texas, a transfer made to evade tax or for less than reasonably equivalent value carries a separate liability, [^p2-tx-111024-fraudulent-transfer] against which withholding is no defense if the amount withheld falls short and the price was not reasonably equivalent. [^p2-tx-111020-no-defense] California's regulation adds that when it is unclear whether a purchase triggers the rule, the purchaser should obtain a certificate. [^p2-ca-reg1702-doubt]

**A worked illustration.** A buyer agrees to pay $1,000,000 for the assets of a California retailer that owes $120,000 in unpaid sales tax, and the purchase agreement gives the buyer an unsecured seller indemnity for that tax. If the buyer holds back enough of the price to cover that amount until the seller produces a receipt or a certificate that nothing is due, [^ca-rtc-6811-withhold] the tax is paid out of money the buyer still controls. If the buyer instead pays the full $1,000,000 at closing, it becomes personally liable for the $120,000 it should have withheld, [^ca-rtc-6812-liability] and recovery under that unsecured indemnity may be worth far less if the seller has spent the proceeds. [^p2-meyers-individual-seller] The figures are invented for the illustration.

For a state not covered here, the place to look is that state's revenue department guidance on successor liability, bulk sales or tax clearance; this guide covers only the three states in the table.

### State tax statutes: California and New York

Some state tax statutes impose successor liability directly and set out a procedure for avoiding it. California uses a withholding rule: when a person liable for an amount under the state's sales and use tax law sells out the business or its stock of goods, the buyer must withhold enough of the price to cover that amount until the seller produces a receipt showing payment or a certificate that nothing is due. [^ca-rtc-6811-withhold] A purchaser that fails to withhold becomes personally liable for the amount it should have withheld, up to the purchase price. [^ca-rtc-6812-liability]

New York uses a notice rule instead: when a seller that is required to collect sales tax makes a bulk sale of business assets outside the ordinary course, the purchaser must notify the tax authority at least ten days before taking possession or paying, whether or not the seller says it owes tax. [^ny-tax-1141c-notice] A purchaser that fails to comply becomes personally liable for the seller's sales taxes, up to the higher of the purchase price or the fair market value of the assets. [^ny-tax-1141c-liability] Giving the notice does not end the matter: if the tax authority tells the purchaser that a possible tax claim exists, or if the purchaser never gave notice, the price still owed to the seller is subject to a first-priority lien for the seller's taxes, and the purchaser may not pay it over to the extent of the state's claim. [^p2-ny-tax-1141c-withhold] The tax authority has ninety days from receiving the notice to state the amount it claims, and if it does not, the purchaser is released from any further obligation to withhold. [^p2-ny-tax-1141c-ninety-days]

These procedures affect the closing timetable and the amount paid at closing: the California amount is withheld until the seller produces the receipt or no-tax-due certificate, so a holdback or escrow for it can be released against that document rather than on a fixed date, [^ca-rtc-6811-withhold] while the New York notice has to go out at least ten days before the buyer pays or takes possession, [^ny-tax-1141c-notice] and any amount the tax authority claims cannot be paid over to the seller. [^p2-ny-tax-1141c-withhold]

### Texas: withholding until the comptroller's receipt or certificate

Texas uses a withholding rule like California's, and it is not limited to sales tax: when a person liable for an amount under the state-taxation title of the Tax Code sells the business or its stock of goods, or quits the business, the buyer must withhold enough of the purchase price to pay the amount due until the seller provides a receipt from the comptroller showing payment or a certificate that no amount is due. [^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability] A buyer that fails to withhold is liable for the amount it should have withheld, up to the value of the purchase price. [^p2-tx-111020-withhold-liability]

The purchaser and seller jointly request clearance on Form 86-114. [^p2-tx-9817-joint-form] Under the statute, on the comptroller's form, the purchaser may ask for a certificate that no tax is due or a statement of the amount that must be paid first, and the comptroller must answer within 60 days after the request or after the seller's records are made available for audit, whichever is later, and in any case within 90 days of the request. [^p2-tx-111020-certificate] If the comptroller does not mail the certificate or statement within that period, the purchaser is released from the obligation to withhold or pay. [^p2-tx-111020-release] The limitation period for enforcing the buyer's obligation starts on the later of the sale or a determination against the seller, so the exposure can surface after closing. [^p2-tx-111020-limitation]

Withholding does not cover every case. A separate section makes a person who acquires a business or its assets through a fraudulent transfer or sham transaction liable for all of the seller's tax, penalty and interest, and a transfer counts as one if the seller made it to evade or hinder collection of the tax or without receiving reasonably equivalent value. [^p2-tx-111024-fraudulent-transfer] The two grounds are alternatives, so a reasonably equivalent price answers only the second; a transfer made to evade or hinder collection is covered whatever the price. [^p2-tx-111024-fraudulent-transfer] Compliance with the withholding rule is not a defense to that liability when the amount withheld does not fully cover the seller's liability and the price was not reasonably equivalent to the value of the business. [^p2-tx-111020-no-defense]

### Federal employment claims: notice and continuity

Claims under federal labor and employment statutes follow a different rule from the state-law exceptions. In *Teed v. Thomas & Betts Power Solutions, L.L.C.*, the Seventh Circuit explained that courts apply a distinct federal standard to those statutes because successor liability will often be needed to achieve their goals, and it extended that standard to suits under the Fair Labor Standards Act. [^p2-teed-federal-standard] The buyer there knew of the workers' pending lawsuit when it bought the business at a receiver's auction, which the court counted in favor of liability, [^p2-teed-notice] and the sale conditions that would have protected it under Wisconsin law did not control under the federal standard. [^p2-teed-disclaimer]

The Supreme Court applied successor liability under the National Labor Relations Act in *Golden State Bottling Co. v. NLRB*. [^p2-golden-state-orders-run] It explained that when a new employer has acquired substantial assets of its predecessor and continued its business operations without interruption or substantial change, retained employees will view their job situations as essentially unaltered, [^p2-golden-state-continuity] and it stated that the Board's orders run to the bona fide purchaser as well as to an evader. [^p2-golden-state-orders-run]

The Sixth Circuit applied the doctrine to Title VII discrimination claims in *EEOC v. MacMillan Bloedel Containers, Inc.*, holding that Title VII does not prohibit the successor doctrine but mandates its application, while stressing that a successor's liability is not automatic and is decided case by case. [^p2-macmillan-holding] It listed the factors courts weigh, starting with whether the successor had notice of the charge and whether the predecessor can provide relief, and continuing through continuity of operations, plant, work force, supervisors, jobs, equipment and product. [^p2-macmillan-factors]

The Ninth Circuit held in *Steinbach v. Hubbard* that successor liability exists under the Fair Labor Standards Act, [^p2-steinbach-holding] borrowing the standard from the labor cases, under which successor liability can attach when the buyer was a bona fide successor and had notice of the potential liability. [^p2-steinbach-standard] *Steinbach* also shows the limit: the court found that a company exploring an acquisition was not a bona fide successor and not liable, [^p2-steinbach-holding] because the arrangement was a temporary trial lease of the assets. [^p2-steinbach-trial-lease]

Notice is what lets a buyer protect itself. The Supreme Court in *Golden State* reasoned that because the successor must have notice before liability can be imposed, its potential liability can be reflected in the price it pays, or it can secure an indemnity clause in the sales contract. [^p2-golden-state-price-indemnity] The Ninth Circuit in *Steinbach* gave the same reason for the notice requirement: it guarantees that a successor had an opportunity to negotiate a lower price or an indemnity clause. [^p2-steinbach-notice-reason] Diligence on pending charges, wage claims and suits is what gives a buyer that opportunity before the price is fixed. [^p2-steinbach-notice-reason]

### The four common-law exceptions

The traditional rule, as the California Supreme Court described it in *Ray v. Alad Corp.*, is that an asset purchaser does not assume the seller's liabilities unless one of four exceptions applies. [^ray-successor-liability-exceptions] The New York Court of Appeals states the same rule for tort claims: a corporation that purchases another corporation's assets is not liable for the seller's torts, subject to four exceptions. [^p2-semenetz-four-exceptions] In *Omnicare Pharmacy of Florida, LLC v. Lake City Nursing, LLC*, an unreported 2026 ruling on a personal-jurisdiction motion, the Delaware Superior Court applied the same general rule [^p2-omnicare-general-rule] and the same four exceptions: assumption, de facto merger or consolidation, mere continuation and fraud. [^p2-omnicare-four-exceptions] Other states' versions of the rule need separate review; one federal appeals court has observed that many states have adopted rules expanding successor liability in products liability cases. [^p2-berg-states-expand]

In California, *Ray v. Alad Corp.* also held that a party that acquires a manufacturing business and continues its product line, in the circumstances presented there, assumes strict tort liability for defects in units of that line the predecessor made; that product-line rule is a separate California doctrine for product claims, not a fifth general exception. [^ray-product-line-holding] Other states have declined to adopt it: the New York Court of Appeals rejected a product-line fifth exception in *Semenetz v. Sherling & Walden, Inc.*, [^p2-semenetz-product-line] and the Wisconsin Supreme Court declined to adopt it in *Fish v. Amsted Industries, Inc.* [^p2-fish-product-line]

A governing-law clause in the purchase agreement does not necessarily decide which state's rule applies to a creditor's successor-liability claim: in *Berg Chilling Systems, Inc. v. Hull Corp.*, the Third Circuit, applying Pennsylvania choice-of-law rules, held that the asset purchase agreement's choice-of-law provision should not apply to whether the transaction in fact constituted a merger. [^p2-berg-choice-of-law]

The purchase agreement bears directly on the first exception, which turns on whether there was an express or implied agreement of assumption. [^ray-successor-liability-exceptions] One filed agreement states that the buyer will not assume any obligation or liability of the seller outside its definition of assumed liabilities. [^p2-grove-no-other-liabilities] In *Omnicare*, the court held that the creditor had failed to establish that the new operators of the business expressly or impliedly assumed the old operators' liabilities. [^p2-omnicare-no-assumption] The same opinion states that courts narrowly construe the mere-continuation theory. [^p2-omnicare-continuation-narrow]

### Indemnity and escrow

A seller indemnity for retained liabilities allocates the loss between the parties, but an unsecured indemnity from an individual seller who has spent the proceeds is worth far less than its face value. [^p2-meyers-individual-seller] A [holdback or escrow](/practice-guides/asset-purchase-price-and-payment/us#escrow-holdback-and-deferred-payment) keeps part of the price available for those claims; one filed agreement held its escrow amount as security for the seller's indemnification obligations. [^p2-luna-escrow-security]

### Sales tax exposure in stock deals

In a stock purchase the company keeps its past sales tax liabilities, so the buyer inherits them through the company it now owns: the company being acquired retains all of its liabilities as a matter of law. [^p2-kddk-stock-retains-liabilities] The state clearance procedures for asset buyers do not reach this case; New York's bulk-sale guidance gives the purchase of all of a corporation's stock as an example of a transaction that is not a bulk sale, because the company's assets have not been transferred. [^p2-ny-tbst70-stock]

The exposure has grown since 2018. In *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.*, the Supreme Court overruled its earlier decisions requiring a seller's physical presence in a state before the state could make it collect sales tax, [^p2-wayfair-overruled] and stated that physical presence is not necessary to create a substantial nexus. [^p2-wayfair-no-physical-presence] The South Dakota statute before the Court, as it stood in 2018, reached sellers delivering more than $100,000 of goods or services into the state or engaging in 200 or more separate transactions there each year. [^p2-wayfair-nexus]

Those 2018 figures are not today's rule everywhere. South Dakota dropped the 200-transaction test effective July 1, 2023, leaving only the $100,000 sales test. [^p2-sd-2023-threshold] California applies a $500,000 test to sales of tangible personal property for delivery in the state in the preceding or current calendar year, [^p2-ca-wayfair-threshold] New York has a business with no physical presence in the state check for more than $500,000 in sales of tangible personal property delivered in the state and more than 100 such sales over the preceding four sales tax quarters, [^p2-ny-nexus-threshold] and Texas excuses a remote seller with less than $500,000 of Texas revenue in the previous 12 months. [^p2-tx-remote-safe-harbor] The current threshold for any other state is on that state's revenue department guidance for remote sellers.

Sales tax diligence on a stock deal covers:

- **Sales by state against each state's threshold.** The count can include marketplace sales: Texas includes them in its $500,000 calculation even when the marketplace collects the tax. [^p2-tx-mp-count]
- **Registrations.** The requirements and marketplace exceptions vary by state. In New York a business with no physical presence that meets the thresholds must register as a vendor, [^p2-ny-nexus-register] and in South Dakota a remote seller over the $100,000 test must be licensed; [^p2-sd-2023-threshold] Texas does not require a permit from a remote seller selling only through a marketplace that has accepted in good faith the marketplace provider's certification that it will collect the tax, [^p2-tx-mp-no-permit] but a remote seller over the $500,000 threshold that also sells through its own website must collect tax on those website sales. [^p2-tx-mp-website]
- **Exemption and resale certificates.** In New York, a seller that accepts a properly completed exemption certificate in good faith within 90 days need not collect the tax, [^p2-ny-pub750-certificate] and a seller that accepts one later shares the burden of proving the exemption and may be liable for the tax. [^p2-ny-pub750-late]
- **Marketplace-facilitator sales.** In Texas a marketplace provider is required to certify to the seller that it will collect the tax, [^p2-tx-mp-cert] and in New York the provider's obligation to collect applies whether or not the seller must register. [^p2-ny-mp-collect]
- **The tax software's configuration**, tested against what the target actually charged, since the failure alleged in the case discussed next was sales tax software that was not set up properly. [^p2-ohn-avalara]

*Online HealthNow, Inc. v. CIP OCL Investments, LLC* (Del. Ch. 2021) shows how the problem surfaces after closing. [^p2-ohn-spa-fraud] The buyer of a company's stock sued the sellers for fraud, alleging that representations in the stock purchase agreement were known to be false when made. [^p2-ohn-spa-fraud] It alleged that the company had not been using its sales tax software properly [^p2-ohn-avalara] and that significant state sales and use taxes went uncollected over several years, [^p2-ohn-uncollected] and that another bidder had put the exposure at $8 to 9 million [^p2-ohn-estimate] and had asked for an escrow of at least $15 million or a price reduction. [^p2-ohn-escrow] Those were allegations, and the decision came on the defendants' motion to dismiss, which the court denied. [^p2-ohn-mtd-denied] The agreement provided that all representations terminated at closing, [^p2-ohn-survival-clause] but the court held that this survival clause did not defeat the buyer's claim that the representations were knowingly false. [^p2-ohn-survival-holding]

For a small deal the case bears on drafting. The sellers relied on survival, anti-reliance and non-recourse provisions, [^p2-ohn-clauses] and the court held that under Delaware law a party cannot invoke provisions of a contract it knew to be an instrument of fraud to avoid a claim grounded in that fraud. [^p2-ohn-instrument] Where the agreement gives little or no indemnity, or only an unsecured indemnity from an individual seller, [^p2-meyers-individual-seller] a claim that the representations were knowingly false may be the buyer's main recourse after closing, so the wording of those three clauses bears directly on what recourse remains.

Three protections respond to the exposure:

- **A voluntary disclosure agreement.** A target that finds unpaid tax before closing can come forward under a state program; in Texas, the agreement lets a taxpayer pay previously unpaid tax with relief from penalties and, in most cases, interest, [^p2-tx-vda-relief] the review is limited to four years except for tax collected and not remitted, [^p2-tx-vda-lookback] and the program is closed to a business the comptroller has already contacted about the liability or notified of an audit. [^p2-tx-vda-eligibility] A business with exposure in several states can approach them together through the Multistate Tax Commission's National Nexus Program. [^p2-tx-vda-mtc]
- **A separate tax indemnity.** One filed stock purchase agreement defines its Indemnified Taxes to include the company's unpaid pre-closing taxes [^p2-airspan-indemnified-taxes] and keeps the indemnity for them in force until 60 days after the limitation period for assessing or collecting those taxes expires, longer than its general representations survive. [^p2-airspan-tax-survival]
- **An escrow.** Part of the price is held back against the exposure, as the other bidder in *Online HealthNow* had proposed. [^p2-ohn-escrow]

## Should the company owner sign as well as the company? {#owner-signing-capacity}

**Short answer.** When the owner of a selling company personally holds a U.S. copyright used in the business, 17 U.S.C. § 204(a) requires a transfer of that copyright, other than by operation of law, to be in a writing signed by that owner or the owner's authorized agent. [^ip-contracts-asset-and-liability-perimeter]

The company signs as seller of the assets it owns, and the intellectual-property conveyances can be made in separate short-form assignments, as in two filed asset purchase agreements that called for separate trademark and copyright assignments at closing alongside the bill of sale. [^p3-grove-ip-assignments][^p3-greenfield-ip-assignments]

Recording the short form protects the buyer against a later purchaser who takes without notice: a patent assignment is void against a later purchaser or mortgagee for value without notice unless it is recorded in the Patent and Trademark Office within three months from its date or before the later purchase or mortgage. [^p3-usc-261-recording] A trademark assignment is void against a later purchaser for value without notice unless the prescribed information is recorded in the Patent and Trademark Office within 3 months after the assignment or before the later purchase. [^p3-usc-1060-recording] Copyright transfers are recorded in the Copyright Office instead, and between two conflicting transfers the one executed first prevails if it is recorded within one month after execution in the United States, two months if executed abroad, or at any time before the later transfer is recorded. [^p3-usc-205d-priority] Recording in the Copyright Office gives constructive notice only if the document identifies the work and the work has been registered. [^p3-usc-205c-notice]

Patent and trademark assignment records at the Patent and Trademark Office are open to public inspection, [^p3-cfr-1-12-public][^p3-cfr-2-200-public] and so are the Copyright Office's records of recorded documents. [^p3-usc-705-public]

Under Delaware law, a member of a limited liability company is not personally obligated for the company's debts, obligations and liabilities solely by reason of being a member, [^p3-dellc-18-303a] but a member may agree, in the LLC agreement or another agreement, to be personally obligated for any or all of them. [^p3-dellc-18-303b] An owner can therefore join only for purposes of named sections, as the selling company's shareholder did in one filed agreement by becoming a party solely for purposes of one section, [^p3-greenfield-joinder] under which he indemnified the buyer for breaches of two listed seller representations and for fraud. [^p3-greenfield-shareholder-indemnity] In another, an individual agreed that, solely for purposes of three listed sections, he would be treated as a shareholder and bound by a shareholder's obligations under them. [^p3-meta-lodge-joinder]

An owner can also join to give a seller restrictive covenant, as in one filed agreement that required non-competition agreements executed by each of the selling parties at closing. [^p3-grove-ip-assignments] Evidence that the company authorized the sale belongs in the closing deliverables; one filed agreement required a secretary's certificate attaching the board and shareholder resolutions approving the agreement, [^p3-luna-secretary-certificate] and [the owner-approval question](#owner-approval-of-asset-sale) covers whether the owners' approval is legally required.

In a stock or membership-interest purchase, by contrast, the buyer buys the owners' shares in the corporation or limited liability company, so each owner sells its own interest; [the structure question](#asset-or-stock-purchase) compares the two. [^d1-mellor-owner-shares]

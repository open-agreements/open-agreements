# Sharing Performance Compensation Between a Registered Adviser and a Fund Sponsor: Fees and Special Limited Partner Allocations[^about]

A guide for an SEC-registered investment adviser that shares in a private fund's performance compensation with the fund's sponsor, covering sub-advisory and direct advisory structures, counterparty risk, a measurement base protected from sponsor overhead, the tax differences between a cash incentive fee and a special limited partner allocation, the mechanics that protect an allocation, Advisers Act and disclosure rules, and public precedents.

## How can a registered adviser share a private fund's performance compensation with the fund's sponsor? {#sharing-performance-compensation-structures}

**Short answer.** There are three common routes in public filings: the sponsor pays the adviser part of its fee under a sub-advisory agreement, the fund pays the adviser directly under an advisory agreement to which the fund is a party, or the adviser holds a non-voting interest in the fund that receives an allocation of profits. [^pgim-subadviser-paid-by-manager] [^ceres-partnership-pays-advisor] [^hatteras-subadviser-allocation-account]

**Sub-advisory, paid by the sponsor.** A 2024 prospectus for a private credit fund describes the sponsor-paid model: "The Manager will pay a portion of the Management Fee and Incentive Fee it receives from the Fund to the Subadviser. No advisory fees will be paid by the Fund directly to the Subadviser."[^pgim-subadviser-paid-by-manager] [^pgim-subadviser-paid-by-manager]

**Direct advisory, paid by the fund.** In a 2022 management agreement among a commodity pool, its general partner and a trading advisor, the fund itself pays the advisor's incentive fee. [^ceres-partnership-pays-advisor]

**An allocation through a special interest.** A 2009 sub-advisory agreement for a registered fund of private equity funds paid the sub-adviser an asset-based fee from the adviser, but delivered the sub-adviser's performance share as an allocation: the sub-adviser held non-voting interests in the fund in an incentive allocation account kept solely to receive it. [^hatteras-subadviser-allocation-account] Other filings call the holder a special advisory member [^bacap-special-advisory-member-nonvoting] or a special limited partner. [^nb-cmp-slp-professionals]

The choice changes whom the adviser relies on to be paid, how its share is taxed, what it must disclose and what the fund documents must say; the [limited partnership agreement guide](/practice-guides/fund-formation/us/hedge-fund-limited-partnership-agreement) covers performance fees, hurdles and high-water marks from the fund's side. The routes can be combined. In the 2009 agreement, the asset-based fee followed the sub-advisory route while the performance share followed the allocation route. [^hatteras-subadviser-allocation-account] Many of the precedents in this guide are registered funds or business development companies, because those are the funds whose agreements are public; a private fund's documents are usually not filed, so the precedents show drafting patterns, not private-fund market frequency.

## Should the adviser be paid by the sponsor under a sub-advisory agreement or by the fund under a direct advisory agreement? {#sub-advisory-or-direct-advisory-credit-risk}

**Short answer.** Under a sub-advisory agreement the adviser's claim runs against the sponsor, so its pay depends on the sponsor's solvency and on the sponsor passing the money on; public sub-advisory agreements manage that risk with short pay-through deadlines, direct-payment rights and limits on the sponsor's power to waive fees. [^xanthus-subadviser-paid-out-of-fee] [^hms-fifty-percent-direct-pay] Under a direct agreement the fund pays the adviser, which removes the sponsor as a credit risk but makes the adviser a party to the fund's own contract. [^ceres-direct-three-party]

**Counterparty and credit risk.** A sub-adviser paid by the sponsor is paid out of the sponsor's own receipts. A 2011 sub-advisory agreement for a registered multi-manager fund requires the investment adviser to pay the sub-adviser out of the fee the adviser receives from the fund, within five business days after the adviser receives its own fee. [^xanthus-subadviser-paid-out-of-fee] If the sponsor never collects, or collects and does not pay, the sub-adviser by default holds an unsecured contract claim against the sponsor, not a claim against the fund, unless it has negotiated a guarantee, security or an enforceable right to be paid directly by the fund. No source we found states that default as a rule; it follows from who the parties to each contract are.

Public agreements add three protections:

1. **Direct payment.** A 2012 sub-advisory agreement for a business development company splits the adviser's base management and incentive fees evenly with the sub-adviser and lets the sub-adviser require the fund to pay its share directly, on the same day the fund pays the adviser. [^hms-fifty-percent-direct-pay] A 2010 real estate sub-advisory agreement goes further and assigns to the sub-advisor the right to receive its percentage of the incentive fee directly from the company. [^phillips-edison-assignment-direct-payment]
2. **No unilateral waiver.** The same 2012 agreement bars the adviser from waiving or deferring the sub-adviser's portion without the sub-adviser's written consent, [^hms-fifty-percent-direct-pay] and a 2015 business development company makes any deferral, reduction or waiver of the adviser's fees subject to the sub-adviser's prior written consent. [^gbsp-waiver-consent]
3. **A short pay-through deadline.** The 2012 agreement requires payment within three business days after the adviser receives the fees. [^hms-fifty-percent-direct-pay]

**Direct advisory.** In the 2022 commodity-pool agreement, the general partner, the partnership and the trading advisor are all parties, and the partnership pays the advisor. [^ceres-direct-three-party] [^ceres-fee-payment-deadline] The adviser then depends on the fund's assets rather than the sponsor's balance sheet, but it has a contract with the fund itself, which matters for disclosure and for custody. Under the SEC's custody rule, custody includes any arrangement under which an adviser is authorized to withdraw client funds or securities held by a custodian on the adviser's instruction. [^custody-rule-custody-definition] An adviser that will instruct the fund's custodian to pay its own fee, or that holds a general partner or comparable position, should therefore confirm how the custody rule applies to it. [^custody-rule-custody-definition]

## How does the adviser keep the sponsor's overhead out of the profits its share is measured on? {#protecting-the-measurement-base}

**Short answer.** By defining the measurement base in the agreement: the adviser's share is computed on the fund's investment profit before the sponsor's own fees and overhead, with verification and audit rights so the adviser can check the number. A filed commodity-pool agreement determines the net assets behind the advisor's fees with no adjustment for management, administrative, selling-agent or incentive fees, [^ceres-net-assets-gross-of-fees] while still charging ongoing fund expenses against the advisor's share. [^ceres-ongoing-expenses-attributed]

When an adviser's share is a percentage of the sponsor's performance compensation, everything that reduces the fund's profit before that compensation is calculated reduces the adviser's share too. SEC examination staff have reported that some private fund advisers charged funds for costs the fund documents did not permit: "Advisers charged private fund clients for expenses that were not permitted by the relevant fund operating agreements, such as adviser-related expenses like salaries of adviser personnel, compliance, regulatory filings, and office expenses, thereby causing investors to overpay expenses."[^risk-alert-adviser-expenses] [^risk-alert-adviser-expenses] Charges like those also shrink the base for a second adviser's share.

Filed agreements show three drafting tools:

1. **Measure before the sponsor's fees.** The 2022 commodity-pool agreement computes net assets without deducting management, administrative, selling-agent or incentive fees. [^ceres-net-assets-gross-of-fees]
2. **Exclude costs unrelated to the adviser.** The same agreement does not exclude every fund expense: it attributes ongoing fund expenses, including offering and organizational expenses, to the assets the advisor manages, but leaves out litigation that does not involve the advisor's activities. [^ceres-ongoing-expenses-attributed] [^ceres-litigation-excluded]
3. **Exclude items charged to only some investors.** A 2009 sub-advisory agreement measures net profits without giving effect to any item not charged ratably to all members. [^hatteras-net-profits-definition]

The negotiating objective is to separate three kinds of cost: the sponsor's own business overhead, which should stay out of the base; legitimate fund expenses, which normally reduce it; and costs attributable to a particular adviser or investor, which the agreement should allocate expressly.

**Verification and audit rights.** The base is only as good as the valuation behind it. SEC staff have observed that valuing fund holdings inconsistently with the disclosed process led to overcharging management fees and carried interest. [^risk-alert-valuation] Institutional-investor guidance summarized by a law firm calls for fund expenses to be reviewed and certified by the fund's independent auditor. [^torys-auditor-certification] For the adviser, the practical rights are delivery of the calculation with each payment or allocation, access to the fund's books and the audited financial statements, a true-up once the audit is final (see the mechanics question below), and a dispute process.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> Measuring the base before the sponsor's overhead is different from taking a share of the fund's gross income. Proposed Treasury regulations from 2015 would presume that an allocation of gross income lacks significant entrepreneurial risk and is a disguised payment for services, which would be taxed as a fee rather than as a partnership allocation. [^disguised-payment-gross-income-presumption] The statute behind them treats an allocation and distribution as a payment to a non-partner when the services and the allocation, viewed together, are properly so characterized. [^irc-707-a-2-a] We did not find those regulations finalized as of this guide's date. An allocation route should keep the adviser's share tied to the fund's net investment profit, with losses counted, and keep only the sponsor's own overhead out of the base.

## Is a share of a performance allocation taxed differently from a share of a performance fee? {#fee-or-allocation-tax-character}

**Short answer.** Often, yes. An allocation keeps the character of the fund's income as it passes through the partnership, so long-term capital gain can reach the adviser's owners at capital-gain rates; for gain subject to the carried-interest rule in section 1061, that generally means gain on assets the fund held more than three years. [^mazars-character-retained] [^irs-1061-three-years] A fee is ordinary income. At top 2026 federal rates, that is roughly 23.8 percent against about 40 percent on gain that qualifies, and much less difference on gain that does not.

**Character and section 1061.** Accounting-firm commentary explains why an allocation can be worth more than the same amount paid as a fee: "The most salient being that the portion of current income attributable to unrealized gains remains unrealized for the manager because the character of the income (ordinary, long-term capital gain, portfolio deduction) is retained when passed through a partnership to its partners."[^mazars-character-retained] [^mazars-character-retained] Section 1061 reaches a partnership interest held in connection with substantial services in an applicable trade or business, which includes raising or returning capital together with investing in or developing specified assets, [^irc-1061-api-definition] [^irc-1061-applicable-trade-or-business] and the IRS summarizes its effect: "The provision generally requires that a capital asset be held for more than three years for capital gain allocated with respect to any applicable partnership interest (API) to be treated as long-term capital gain."[^irs-1061-three-years] [^irs-1061-three-years] A fund that trades actively may produce little gain that clears three years, so the rate benefit of an allocation can be small for a hedge fund. [^mazars-hedge-funds-1061] The regulations leave some long-term items out of the section 1061 computation altogether, including section 1231 gain, section 1256 gain on contracts marked to market (relevant to futures funds) and qualified dividends, so those items are not recharacterized by the three-year rule. [^reg-1061-4-excluded-items]

**Rates.** For 2026 the top individual rate is 37 percent, [^irs-2026-top-rate] the top rate on most long-term capital gain is 20 percent, [^irs-topic-409-capital-gains-rate] and the net investment income tax adds 3.8 percent above the statutory thresholds [^irs-niit-rate] on investment income, which includes capital gains. [^irs-niit-capital-gains-included] Income taken into account for self-employment tax is excluded from net investment income, so a fee that is self-employment income bears the Medicare self-employment tax rather than the net investment income tax. [^irc-1411-c-6-se-income]

**Self-employment tax.** Gain from the sale or exchange of a capital asset is excluded from net earnings from self-employment, so a capital-gain allocation is generally not self-employment income. [^irc-1402-a-3] Commentary treats management fees that flow to an individual as self-employment income, [^mazars-fees-self-employment-income] and above the Social Security wage base the remaining Medicare portion is 2.9 percent [^irc-1401-b-medicare] plus a 0.9 percent additional tax above a threshold. [^irc-1401-b-2-additional-tax] An S corporation adviser is different; see the pass-through question below. The exclusion for a limited partner's distributive share [^irc-1402-a-12-13] is unsettled: in August 2026 the Fifth Circuit withdrew its January 2026 opinion in *Sirius Solutions* [^rsm-sirius-substituted] and, in a substituted opinion now captioned *K Alain, L.L.L.P. v. Commissioner*, held that a limited partner is a partner who plays no significant role in managing or running a business, vacating the Tax Court's judgment and remanding. [^ca5-k-alain-limited-partner-meaning] That case concerns the limited partner exclusion only; the capital-gain exclusion in section 1402(a)(3) applies independently of it, so the case does not by itself make a genuine capital-gain allocation self-employment income. Investment management is also a specified service business, [^irc-199a-sstb] and a specified service business is not a qualified trade or business for the section 199A deduction; [^irc-199a-d-1-qualified-business] below a taxable-income threshold plus a phase-in range the exclusion does not apply, and within the range only part of the income counts, measured at the owner's level. [^irc-199a-d-3-phase-in] The illustration below assumes an owner above the range.

**An illustration.** Take a hypothetical fund with $40 million of capital that earns a 10 percent return in a year, or $4,000,000, and pays 20 percent of that, $800,000, as performance compensation. Suppose the adviser's share is 40 percent of it, or $320,000. These figures are invented for the example.

| Assumption or result | Allocation of long-term gain on assets held more than three years | Cash performance fee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Character to the adviser's individual owner | Long-term capital gain | Ordinary income |
| Federal income tax rate (top bracket, 2026) | 20% | 37% |
| Medicare-type tax | 3.8% net investment income tax | about 3.8% self-employment Medicare tax (2.9% + 0.9%) |
| Combined federal rate assumed (simple sum of statutory rates) | 23.8% | 40.8% |
| Federal tax on $320,000 | $76,160 | $130,560 |
| After federal tax | $243,840 | $189,440 |

The difference in this simplified example is $54,400. The 40.8 percent figure adds statutory rates; it is not an effective rate. Computed more closely, self-employment tax applies to 92.35 percent of net earnings, [^irc-1402-a-12-13] and half of the 2.9 percent Medicare tax is deductible for income tax purposes. [^irc-164-f-half-se-tax] On $320,000 that gives a self-employment base of $295,520, Medicare taxes of $11,229.76 at 3.8 percent, a deduction of $4,285.04, and federal tax of about $128,044, or about 40.0 percent, leaving about $191,956 after tax and a difference of about $51,884.

The comparison assumes an individual owner of an adviser taxed as a partnership, or a sole proprietor, whose fee share is self-employment income; an S corporation owner is taxed differently (see the pass-through question below). It also assumes the owner is in the top bracket with income above the net investment income tax and additional Medicare thresholds and above the Social Security wage base, ignores state and local tax, assumes the section 199A deduction is unavailable, and assumes all the fund's gain is long-term gain on assets held more than three years that section 1061 would otherwise reach. If the gain were short-term, or long-term but on assets held three years or less, the allocation would be taxed at ordinary rates plus the net investment income tax, close to the fee's rate, and most of the rate advantage would disappear; the timing, self-employment and investor-side differences discussed in the next question could remain.

## How does a fee or an allocation affect deferral of unrealized gains and the fund's investors? {#fee-or-allocation-deferral-and-investors}

**Short answer.** An allocation can defer the adviser's tax on its share of unrealized gains until the fund realizes them, while a cash fee is taxed when the adviser receives or accrues it. [^mazars-unrealized-deferral] For individual investors, an allocation reduces the income the fund allocates to them, while a fee paid by an investing (not trading) fund is a miscellaneous itemized deduction, which section 67(h), made permanent by Public Law 119-21, disallows for every taxable year beginning after 2017. [^irc-67-h] [^kitces-carry-reduces-lp-income]

**Deferral.** A performance share measured on unrealized as well as realized gains is common; the 2009 sub-advisory agreement takes unrealized appreciation into account. [^hatteras-net-profits-unrealized-appreciation] Paid as a fee, that share is cash income to the adviser even though the fund has not sold anything. Allocated, the adviser's share of unrealized gain stays unrealized for tax purposes until the fund sells. [^mazars-unrealized-deferral] Some fee structures avoid paying on unrealized gain at all: a 2024 private credit fund accrues, but does not pay, its capital-gains incentive fee on unrealized appreciation. [^pgim-accrue-not-pay]

**Investors' deduction.** Section 67(h) now reads: "Notwithstanding subsection (a), no miscellaneous itemized deduction shall be allowed for any taxable year beginning after December 31, 2017."[^irc-67-h] [^irc-67-h] That text is the former subsection (g), which the 2017 tax act limited to 2018 through 2025; section 70110 of Public Law 119-21 struck the 2026 end date and redesignated it as subsection (h), effective for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025. [^plaw-119-21-70110] [^irc-67-notes-70110] [^plaw-119-21-70110-effective-date] Commentary explains that a fund classified as an investor rather than a trader passes its fees through to individual investors as miscellaneous itemized deductions, [^mazars-trader-investor] and that an allocation instead reduces the gains allocated to them. [^kitces-carry-reduces-lp-income]

Who bears that difference depends on how the fund pays the sponsor, not only on how the sponsor pays the adviser. If the fund pays the sponsor through an allocation and the sponsor pays the adviser a cash sub-advisory fee, the investors' tax position is the same as if the sponsor kept everything, and the difference falls on the sponsor and the adviser. If the fund pays the adviser a cash fee directly, an investing fund's individual investors bear a fee they cannot deduct. We found no public source that addresses directly how a sponsor that receives a capital-gain allocation and pays an ordinary sub-advisory fee should treat the payment. As a general matter, the character of the sponsor's allocated gain is fixed at the partnership level, as if realized directly by the partnership, whatever the sponsor later does with the cash; [^irc-702-b-character] whether the sub-advisory fee it pays is deductible, and against what, depends on whether it is a business expense of the sponsor or an investment expense limited by section 67. [^irc-67-h] That is a question for the sponsor's tax adviser, and no character-matched offset should be assumed.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice note.**
>
> The tax benefits of an allocation assume a valid partnership interest and a valid allocation. The IRS does not treat receipt of a profits interest for services as a taxable event if the conditions of its revenue procedures are met, [^rev-proc-2001-43-profits-interest] but a capital interest, including a share of value the fund had already accrued when the adviser joined, can be taxable on grant. Allocations that lack substantial economic effect are reallocated by the partners' interests in the partnership. [^irc-704-b-substantial-economic-effect] Have the fund's tax counsel confirm how the interest is granted, how existing accrued value is treated, and how book appreciation credited to the adviser's capital account maps to taxable gain.

## Does it matter whether the adviser receiving the allocation is a pass-through entity or a C corporation? {#pass-through-or-c-corporation-recipient}

**Short answer.** Yes. Section 1061 does not apply to a partnership interest held by a corporation, but a C corporation pays the same 21 percent rate whatever the character of its income, so a C corporation adviser gains little on rate from an allocation. An S corporation is not a corporation for this purpose and is subject to the three-year rule. [^reg-1061-3-corporation] [^reg-1061-3-s-corporation-qef] [^irc-11-b]

The section 1061 regulations say: "An API does not include any interest directly or indirectly held by a corporation."[^reg-1061-3-corporation] [^reg-1061-3-corporation] They then exclude S corporations and certain electing foreign funds from the meaning of corporation. [^reg-1061-3-s-corporation-qef] A C corporation's tax is 21 percent of taxable income, [^irc-11-b] and commentary notes that corporate partners are indifferent to the holding period of the fund's investments. [^mazars-corporate-indifferent] The same commentary cautions that a C corporation adds a second layer of tax when profits are distributed to its owners. [^mazars-c-corp-double-tax]

For a C corporation adviser, then, the case for an allocation rests mainly on timing, investor-side effects and credit position rather than rate. For an adviser taxed as a partnership (including an LLC taxed as one) and owned by individuals, the rate and self-employment analysis in the previous questions passes through to the owners. An S corporation adviser differs on self-employment tax: the IRS treats non-wage distributions to shareholders as not subject to employment taxes, [^irs-s-corp-reclassify-wages] but an S corporation must pay a shareholder-employee reasonable compensation, as wages, before making non-wage distributions. [^irs-s-corp-reasonable-compensation] It remains subject to section 1061.

## What terms make a special limited partner allocation safe for the adviser? {#special-interest-mechanics}

**Short answer.** Unless the agreement turns a crystallized allocation into a fixed distribution obligation, an allocation stays in the adviser's capital account in the fund until it is withdrawn, so the protective terms are a right to withdraw promptly when the allocation crystallizes, true-up after the audit, tax distributions, a clawback that is limited and clearly defined, rules for what happens to the interest after the adviser's role ends, and an understanding of where the adviser ranks if the fund fails. One filed agreement lets the sub-adviser withdraw up to 95 percent on unaudited figures, with a true-up after the audit. [^hatteras-95-percent-true-up]

**Withdrawal on crystallization, and the true-up.** The 2009 sub-advisory agreement says: "After the close of an allocation period with respect to a Member, and subject to certain limitations, the Sub-Adviser may withdraw up to 95% of the Incentive Allocation, computed on the basis of unaudited data, that was credited to its Incentive Allocation Account with respect to such allocation period."[^hatteras-95-percent-true-up] [^hatteras-95-percent-true-up] After the audit, the fund credits any shortfall and the sub-adviser repays any excess. [^hatteras-95-percent-true-up] A 2011 agreement allows up to 100 percent on unaudited data by the last business day of the following month, with any additional amount paid within 30 days after the audit and any excess repaid. [^xanthus-100-percent] A 2002 agreement lets the adviser withdraw its allocation at any time after it is made. [^bacap-withdraw-any-time] A fee-route precedent uses the same idea: 90 percent of the incentive fee within 30 days after year-end and the balance within 30 days after the audit. [^bessemer-90-percent-audit]

**Tax distributions.** An allocation can create taxable income before cash is distributed. A 2017 fund of private equity funds lets its board, in its sole discretion, make distributions to the special limited partner designed to help it satisfy income tax on allocated income and gains. [^nb-cmp-tax-distribution] An adviser should ask for a mandatory tax distribution rather than a discretionary one, computed at an assumed combined rate.

**Clawback.** Institutional-investor guidance favors clawbacks gross of taxes paid, [^torys-clawback-gross-of-taxes] and escrow of accrued carried interest. [^torys-carried-interest-escrow] From the adviser's side, the points to settle are whether the clawback is net of taxes, whether liability is several and capped at what the adviser itself received, and how long it lasts.

**After the adviser's role ends.** The 2009 agreement closes an allocation period on the day the sub-adviser's status ends, but provides that no allocation is payable if the sub-adviser ends it voluntarily or the board ends it for gross negligence or willful misconduct. [^hatteras-termination-period] A fee-route agreement pays a terminated sub-adviser an incentive fee computed through the termination date, 90 percent within 30 days and the balance after the audit. [^bessemer-termination-stub] A 2002 agreement provides for the admission of a substitute special advisory member when the advisory agreement ends. [^bacap-substitute-member] An adviser should ensure that a terminated adviser's interest stays outstanding, with its capital account, until a final settlement or a stated long-stop date, rather than being forfeited or cancelled at termination. We found no public precedent that uses the term long-stop for this; it is a drafting suggestion, not a market report.

**Creditor ranking and drawdown risk.** Unless the partnership agreement fixes a crystallized allocation as a distribution owed, an allocated amount stays in the adviser's capital account and rises and falls with the portfolio until it is withdrawn; the adviser should confirm which the agreement does. Subject to the partnership agreement and to sections 17-607 and 17-804, once a partner becomes entitled to a distribution, Delaware law gives it a creditor's status for that distribution. [^del-17-606-creditor-status] In a winding up, creditors, including partners who are creditors, are paid first (other than for distributions owed under sections 17-601 and 17-604); then, unless the agreement provides otherwise, partners and former partners for those distributions; then partners for the return of contributions and in respect of their partnership interests. [^del-17-804-priority] A partnership may not make a distribution to the extent that, afterward, its liabilities (other than liabilities to partners on account of their interests and non-recourse liabilities) would exceed the fair value of its assets, and reasonable compensation for services is not a distribution for this purpose. [^del-17-607-a-limit-test] A limited partner who knew when it received a distribution that it violated that limit is liable for the amount, [^del-17-607-limit] and unless otherwise agreed that liability ends three years after the distribution. [^del-17-607-c-three-years] Whether a particular performance allocation payment is a distribution or reasonable compensation for services under that test is not settled by the statute. A cash fee owed by the fund is, by default, the claim of an ordinary creditor unless it is secured; a fee owed by the sponsor is a claim only against the sponsor unless a guarantee or direct-payment right reaches the fund. Which position is better depends on whose insolvency is more likely, which is a credit judgment rather than a rule.

## Is a performance allocation still a performance fee under the Advisers Act? {#performance-allocation-advisers-act}

**Short answer.** Yes. Section 205(a)(1) reaches compensation based on a share of capital gains or appreciation, and the SEC describes carried interest as a form of performance-based compensation, so an allocation needs the same qualified-client exemption as a fee unless the fund relies on section 3(c)(7). [^advisers-act-205-a-1] [^sec-carried-interest-performance-comp] [^advisers-act-205-b-4]

**The rule.** Section 205(a)(1) prohibits a registered adviser's contract that "provides for compensation to the investment adviser on the basis of a share of capital gains upon or capital appreciation of the funds or any portion of the funds of the client;"[^advisers-act-205-a-1] [^advisers-act-205-a-1] The SEC's 2026 proposing release says: "Carried interest is a form of performance-based compensation where a portion of profits is allocated to a general partner before the remaining profits are distributed to limited partners."[^sec-carried-interest-performance-comp] [^sec-carried-interest-performance-comp] Nothing in the statute turns on whether the share is paid in cash or credited to a capital account.

**Qualified clients and the 3(c)(1) look-through.** Rule 205-3 permits the compensation when the client is a qualified client. [^rule-205-3-a] For a fund relying on section 3(c)(1), each equity owner charged the performance compensation is treated as the client, except the adviser itself and owners not charged. [^rule-205-3-b] Under SEC Order IA-6961, dated April 28, 2026, the two dollar tests are at least $1,400,000 under the adviser's management immediately after entering the contract, or a net worth the adviser reasonably believes is more than $2,700,000 immediately before. [^ia-6961-thresholds] Those are two routes, not the whole definition: a qualified purchaser also qualifies, as do certain executive officers and investment personnel of the adviser, and a natural person's primary residence is left out of net worth. [^rule-205-3-d-1-qualified-client] The order took effect on June 29, 2026 and does not generally apply to contracts entered into before then, subject to the rule's transition provisions. [^ia-6961-effective] Under those provisions, a person who becomes a party later, including a new equity owner of the fund, is tested under the thresholds in effect at that time. [^rule-205-3-c-1] A sub-adviser paid by the sponsor should not assume that its contract with the sponsor removes the need to test the fund's investors: the look-through treats the fund's charged equity owners as the clients. [^rule-205-3-b]

**The 3(c)(7) exception.** The prohibition does not apply to a contract with a fund excepted under section 3(c)(7). [^advisers-act-205-b-4]

**A pending change.** In a proposal published in the Federal Register on October 6, 2026, with comments due December 7, 2026, the SEC would revise the qualified-client definition to include accredited investors. [^sec-2026-proposal-comment-dates] [^sec-2026-proposal-accredited] As of this guide's date it is a proposal, not law.

## What must the fund's offering documents and the adviser's Form ADV say about shared performance compensation? {#disclosure-ppm-and-form-adv}

**Short answer.** The offering documents must describe the arrangement accurately and consistently with the partnership agreement, because an adviser to a pooled vehicle may not make a materially misleading statement to an investor or prospective investor. [^rule-206-4-8] On Form ADV, the adviser reports performance-based fees, describes the conflicts they create in its brochure, and reports its and its related persons' ownership of the private fund. [^adv-1a-item-5e] [^adv-2a-item-6] [^adv-schedule-d-ownership-questions]

**Antifraud rule.** Rule 206(4)-8 makes it fraudulent for an adviser to a pooled investment vehicle to make an untrue statement of a material fact, or to omit a material fact needed to make its statements not misleading, to any investor or prospective investor in the vehicle, or otherwise to engage in fraudulent, deceptive or manipulative conduct toward them. [^rule-206-4-8] The rule covers each adviser to the fund, not only the sponsor.

**Inconsistent documents.** In *Global Infrastructure Management* (2021), the SEC found that a private fund adviser's offering memoranda and partnership agreements said different things about how management fees would be calculated after a partial disposition, and that the adviser lacked policies to confirm that its offering memorandum and partnership agreement were consistent on key points. [^gim-ppm-lpa-inconsistent] The adviser agreed to a $4.5 million penalty and consented to findings that it violated Rule 206(4)-8, among other provisions. [^sec-2021-266] An adviser sharing in performance compensation should check that the offering memorandum, the partnership agreement and its own agreement describe the same base, rate, timing and termination terms. SEC staff have also reported that advisers did not adequately disclose economic relationships with select investors and others. [^risk-alert-financial-relationships]

**Form ADV.** The Form ADV glossary defines a performance-based fee as a fee based on a share of capital gains on, or capital appreciation of, client assets, [^adv-glossary-performance-based-fee] and Part 1A, Item 5.E asks the adviser to check performance-based fees if it is compensated that way. [^adv-1a-item-5e] Part 2A, Item 6 requires the brochure to disclose performance-based fees and to explain the conflicts of managing accounts with and without them. [^adv-2a-item-6] For each private fund it advises, the adviser completes Section 7.B.(1) of Schedule D unless another SEC-registered adviser or SEC exempt reporting adviser reports the fund there, as may be the case for a subadviser, in which case it completes Section 7.B.(2) instead. [^adv-1a-item-7b-subadviser] Section 7.B.(1) asks for the approximate percentage of the fund beneficially owned by the adviser and its related persons, whether sales of a 3(c)(1) fund are limited to qualified clients, and whether the reporting adviser is a subadviser. [^adv-schedule-d-ownership-questions] A special limited partner interest is an ownership interest in the fund that may need to be reflected there. The question asks for approximate beneficial ownership, not a valuation, and a 20 percent performance share is not the same as 20 percent ownership of the fund; we found no SEC statement on how to measure a profits-only interest for that question, so the adviser should document the method it uses and confirm it with regulatory counsel.

## What do public filings show about how advisers share performance compensation? {#public-precedents-performance-sharing}

**Short answer.** Filed agreements show both routes in use: sub-advisers paid a share of the sponsor's fees, with direct-payment and anti-waiver protections, and advisers or sub-advisers holding non-voting special interests that receive incentive allocations, with prompt withdrawal and audit true-ups.

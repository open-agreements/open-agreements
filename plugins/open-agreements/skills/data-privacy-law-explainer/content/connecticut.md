# Connecticut Consumer Privacy Law (CTDPA)[^about]

The Connecticut Data Privacy Act gives Connecticut consumers rights over their personal data and imposes notice, universal-opt-out, contracting, and consent duties on controllers that meet its applicability tests — it is enforced exclusively by the Attorney General, its cure period sunset at the end of 2024, and it provides no private right of action.

## Does the Connecticut Data Privacy Act apply to your business? {#does-ctdpa-apply}

**Short answer.** Since July 1, 2026, three alternative tests trigger the CTDPA, the first two excluding payment-only data: processing 35,000 or more consumers' personal data in the preceding calendar year while doing business in or targeting Connecticut, controlling or processing consumers' sensitive data, or offering consumers' personal data for sale [^stat-516-apply]. The volume and sensitive-data tests exclude data processed solely to complete a payment transaction, and none of the three tests turns on revenue [^stat-516-apply].

Before July 1, 2026, the thresholds were 100,000 consumers, or 25,000 consumers for a business that derived more than 25% of its gross revenue from selling personal data [^stat-516-former].

The CTDPA exempts entire entities such as state agencies, nonprofit organizations, and HIPAA covered entities and business associates [^stat-517-entities]. It also exempts certain data, including HIPAA protected health information and consumer-report activity to the extent it is regulated by and authorized under the Fair Credit Reporting Act [^stat-517-data]. Since July 1, 2026, being subject to Title V of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act no longer exempts a financial institution as a whole, though GLBA Title V data remains exempt [^stat-517-history]. Insurers, regulated broker-dealers and investment advisers, and banks and credit unions that engage only in financial activities, are examined by a banking regulator, and run a personal-data compliance program keep entity exemptions of their own [^stat-517-entities]. A consumer is a Connecticut resident acting in an individual or household context, not an employee or business contact.

## What must your Connecticut privacy policy contain? {#privacy-policy-contents}

**Short answer.** A controller must provide a reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful privacy notice whose contents, since July 1, 2026, include the categories of personal data sold, whether personal data is collected, used, or sold to train large language models, and the notice's last-updated month and year [^stat-520-notice].

Section 42-520(b)(1) supplies the full statutory checklist for a template privacy policy: the categories of personal data processed and the purpose of processing; how consumers exercise and appeal their rights; the categories of personal data sold to third parties and of the third parties that buy it; a clear and conspicuous disclosure of any processing or sale for targeted advertising; an active email address or other online contact; a statement of whether the controller collects, uses, or sells personal data to train large language models; and the month and year of the last update [^stat-520-notice].

The notice must be reachable through a conspicuous link that includes the word *privacy* on the website home page and, for an app, on its store or download page and in its settings menu or a similarly conspicuous and accessible location [^stat-520-posting]. A controller without a website uses a medium through which it regularly interacts with consumers, such as mail, and the notice must be offered in each language in which the controller provides the products or services it covers and be accessible to individuals with disabilities [^stat-520-posting].

Connecticut also limits collection to what is reasonably necessary and proportionate to the disclosed purposes, and it bars processing a consumer's sensitive data unless the processing is reasonably necessary and the consumer consents [^stat-520-duties]. Where the controller knows, or wilfully disregards, that the consumer is a child, the child's sensitive data must instead be processed in accordance with COPPA [^stat-520-duties]. The practices the notice describes must therefore line up with the consents actually collected. If you sell personal data or process it for targeted advertising, the policy must clearly disclose that and how to opt out.

## What must your contracts with processors say? {#vendor-contracts}

**Short answer.** A contract between a controller and a processor must govern the processor's data processing on the controller's behalf — making a data processing agreement a statutory requirement, not a best practice [^stat-521-contract].

Section 42-521 then specifies the required terms: processing instructions, the nature and purpose of processing, the type of data and duration, a duty of confidentiality, deletion or return of data at the controller's direction, the information needed to demonstrate compliance, cooperation with assessments, and a requirement to bind subcontractors by written contract to the same obligations [^stat-521-contract]. A compliant template DPA tracks each of these.

## Must you honor a universal opt-out signal? {#universal-opt-out}

**Short answer.** Since January 1, 2025, a Connecticut controller must let consumers opt out of targeted advertising and the sale of their personal data through an opt-out preference signal that a platform, technology, or mechanism sends with the consumer's consent, not just a website link [^stat-520-uoom]. The July 1, 2026 amendments kept this duty, now at section 42-520(c)(1)(A)(ii) [^stat-520-uoom-2026].

This puts Connecticut among the states (with California and Colorado) that require honoring universal opt-out signals. A template privacy program should wire opt-out-preference-signal handling into its consent and preference logic. The opt-out is part of a fuller set of consumer rights: access (including inferences derived from the consumer's personal data), correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out of targeted advertising, sale, and profiling in furtherance of any automated decision with legal or similarly significant effects [^stat-518-rights]. Since July 1, 2026, a consumer whose data was profiled for such an automated decision may also, where feasible, question the result, learn the reason for it, and review the personal data used, with correction and reevaluation for a housing decision [^stat-518-rights]. Separately, any consumer may obtain a list of the third parties to which the controller sold the consumer's personal data, or, if the controller keeps no such list, a list of all third parties to which it has sold personal data, without the controller revealing trade secrets [^stat-518-rights]. A controller must respond to a rights request within 45 days and may extend that period once by 45 more days when reasonably necessary, if it tells the consumer of the extension and the reason within the first 45 days [^stat-518-deadline].

## Can a consumer sue your business under the CTDPA? {#consumer-lawsuit}

**Short answer.** No. The CTDPA states that nothing in it provides a basis for a private right of action, so consumers cannot sue under it [^stat-525-nopra]. Enforcement belongs to the Connecticut Attorney General, who treats violations as unfair trade practices.

There is an important timing wrinkle: the CTDPA's mandatory right-to-cure ran only from July 1, 2023 through December 31, 2024 [^stat-525-cure]. Since the start of 2025, a cure is discretionary, not guaranteed — the Attorney General may, but need not, offer one. The compliance posture is to build the privacy notice, opt-out, and contracting controls up front rather than counting on a cure window that has lapsed.
